Novels, Deep-Read · Roberto Bolaño · No.62

2666

Roberto Bolaño · died 2003 / published posthumously 2004 · five parts · roughly 1,100 pages

中文 →

The wind in Sonora blows all day. A Chilean philosophy teacher, exiled into a job on the Mexican border, pins an old geometry book to the clothesline in his back yard. No reason. He just wants to see what the wind, the sun and the dust will teach it.
At the other end of the same city, near dawn, a woman is found on a vacant lot. The police write down what she was wearing, where the wounds are, whether anyone has claimed her. Then the page is filed.
There are more than a hundred such pages in this novel.

This page contains complete spoilers — who the invisible German novelist really is, how he is related to the man jailed for the border killings, and the most important fact about the book: the murderer is never identified, and the novel has no intention of identifying him. Everything worth saying about 2666 lives past its ending; if you don't know what it refuses to give you, there is nothing to discuss.

In one sentence

Four European academics fly to the Mexican border hunting a German novelist nobody has ever seen, and fail to find him; in the same city more than a hundred women, most of them factory workers, are raped, killed and dumped in the desert, and not one case is solved; only in the fifth book do we learn that the novelist's own nephew is the man jailed as the prime suspect — and the novel ends with the old man having just booked his flight to Mexico, before he has met a single person there.

Coordinates

Roberto Bolaño (1953–2003), Chilean, moved with his family to Mexico City as a teenager, and in his early twenties founded a small poetry faction there with the poet Mario Santiago — infrarrealismo, whose main activity was heckling other people's readings. He spent the second half of his life in Blanes, a small town on the Spanish coast. He always considered himself a poet; he only turned seriously to fiction around forty, to support a family, and then in a single decade wrote The Savage Detectives, Distant Star and By Night in Chile. He died of liver failure in 2003, aged fifty, still waiting for a transplant.

2666 is the last and largest thing he left. He had instructed that it be split into five books published a year apart, so the royalties would support his two children over time; his heirs, together with the critic Ignacio Echevarría, who was entrusted with the manuscript, decided to publish it whole. It appeared in 2004; Natasha Wimmer's English translation came out in 2008 and took the National Book Critics Circle Award for Fiction.

Its place in literary history is unusual: it is the last full-scale revival of the leviathan novel in the twenty-first century — the Moby-Dick line, the kind that refuses to trim its material into shape. Except that the thing at its center is not a whale. It is something that was still happening on the Mexican border while the book was being written, and that has still not been resolved.

The story

Act one · Four people and a writer nobody has seen

Four European academics come first: Pelletier the Frenchman, Espinoza the Spaniard, Morini the Italian, Liz Norton the Englishwoman. They have exactly one thing in common — all four study the same German novelist, Benno von Archimboldi, an old man whose books keep growing in reputation while he himself has not been seen in decades. His publisher deals only with an agent. Every autumn someone tips him for the Nobel.

They meet at conference after conference, form an alliance, become friends. Morini has multiple sclerosis and uses a wheelchair; he is the quietest and the most observant of them. Pelletier and Espinoza both become Norton's lovers, each aware of the other, the three of them sustaining a strange arrangement almost free of jealousy. Morini says nothing.

Bolaño spends more than a hundred pages making these four thoroughly likeable. Then he stages a scene in London. A late-night taxi; the Pakistani driver works out from the mirror what the three of them are to each other and says something obscene about Norton. The two men drag him from the car and kick him on the ground until he stops moving, supplying reasons the whole time — for Rushdie, for oppressed women, for civilization. Then they wash their hands and go to dinner. Norton says nothing.

The break comes as a rumour: at a conference someone mentions that an old German matching Archimboldi's description has surfaced in Mexico and gone to Santa Teresa, a border city in Sonora.seed ① Three of them fly out (Morini's health keeps him home). Their guide is a distracted, visibly unsuccessful Chilean philosophy lecturer at the local university, named Amalfitano.seed ②

They stay a long time and find nobody. They sit by the hotel pool, complain about the heat, argue about scholarship, and now and then catch a line in the paper about another woman's body, and turn the page. Norton flies back first and writes to say she is with Morini. Pelletier eventually says something to the effect of: he is here, we are here, this is as close to him as we will ever get. They stop looking.

Act two · A geometry book on a clothesline

The second book hands the camera to the guide.payoff ② ↑ The middle-aged walk-on from part one, faintly ridiculous, turns out to be worth an entire novel: Óscar Amalfitano, Chilean, an exile, once in Barcelona, now teaching philosophy in this border city with his seventeen-year-old daughter Rosa.

His wife Lola left him years ago for a poet confined in a Spanish asylum — hitchhiking, begging, sending letters, coming back briefly after some years, very ill, then leaving again to die elsewhere.

Unpacking, Amalfitano finds a book he has no memory of buying: a Spanish treatise on geometry. He pegs it to the clothesline. He knows exactly whom he is imitating — Duchamp had his sister hang a geometry textbook on her balcony so the wind could work through it and the weather could tear the pages out one by one; the piece is called an unhappy readymade. Amalfitano cannot say why he is doing it. He only looks at it every morning on his way out.

Then he starts drawing diagrams: philosophers' names arranged inside triangles and rectangles, as if he were looking for a relation. And he starts hearing a voice, possibly his father's, possibly his grandfather's, telling him to stay calm, telling him he is not going mad.

What he is actually afraid of is written very lightly: he has a seventeen-year-old daughter, in a city where girls disappear every month.seed ③ He cannot say it, and saying it would not help, so the fear goes into the diagrams and the book in the wind. This part has almost no plot. It is the most poisoned hundred pages in the novel: a highly educated man in a city where he can protect no one, gradually beginning to do things that make no sense.

Act three · An American reporter sent to cover a fight

The third book switches to someone entirely unconnected. Quincy Williams, who writes as Oscar Fate, a reporter for a Black magazine in Harlem; his mother has just died. He goes first to Detroit to interview a former Black Panther and hears the old man preach a sermon in a church on five words: danger, money, food, stars, and finally usefulness.

Then the magazine's sports man dies and Fate is sent to Santa Teresa to cover a boxing match, about which he knows nothing. Once there he quickly works out what is actually happening in the city and calls the desk asking to write about the murdered women instead. He is refused: we're a magazine for readers in Harlem, not a Mexican crime desk.

He stays anyway, falling in with some local men — a journalist, a club owner, a video-store owner — and a young woman named Rosa Amalfitano. At a late party he smells it: the way these men talk, the way they look at her, the extremely polite violence of the house. He takes her out. Her father, at the door, is close to begging him: get her across the border, as fast as you can.payoff ③ ↑ Everything the man in part two could not say out loud is executed here by a stranger.

Before leaving, Fate goes with a badly frightened Mexican journalist to the prison — her predecessor on this story was killed. In the yard they see the accused: Klaus Haas, German-born, huge, blond, surrounded by other inmates like a boss.seed ④ He says he is innocent, and points out that women have kept dying since he was locked up. Part three ends in a car heading for the border — the only time in the whole novel anyone is got out.

Act four · More than a hundred reports

Then comes The Part About the Crimes, the longest, hardest and most important section: three hundred-odd pages running from January 1993 to 1997.

The method is this. Paragraph after paragraph, each one a dead woman. January 1993, Esperanza Gómez Saldaña is found in the Las Flores district — she is generally taken to be the first. Then the second, the third. The syntax barely varies: where she was found, what she was wearing, her age, which maquiladora she worked at (the foreign-owned assembly plants along the border, staffed largely by young women), what time her shift ended, how many stab wounds, whether she was raped, who caught the case, and then — almost always this line — no progress, file closed.

Living people are threaded through it. Inspector Juan de Dios Martínez is chasing a lunatic who breaks into churches, smashes statues and eventually kills, known as the Penitent; the asylum director he falls in love with recites the names of phobias to him — fear of churches, of bridges, of going to bed — a list that reads like the city's medical chart. A country boy called Lalo Cura (say the name fast and it is the Spanish for madness) becomes a policeman and works cases out of criminology manuals he teaches himself from; he is the only person in the book who genuinely wants to solve them. An American small-town sheriff, Harry Magaña, crosses the border on his own to look for a missing American woman and disappears into Mexico himself.

And there is Sergio González, a reporter sent up from Mexico City — a real person: the author of the investigative book on the Ciudad Juárez killings that Bolaño consulted extensively while writing, and whom he then simply wrote into the novel.

In 1995 a German-born computer-store owner, Klaus Haas, is arrested and then charged with case after case. From prison he holds press conferences naming a drug lord's son. It does not end anything. Women go on dying. Late in the part a congresswoman from an old family finds the reporter and tells him about her missing friend, and by the end she is very nearly cursing the country itself.

Part four ends in 1997 with no conclusion. The only certainty is that the list is not finished — the book simply turned the page.

Act five · The seaweed boy, walking all the way to that name

The last book goes back to Prussia in 1920. Hans Reiter: a father who lost a leg in the first war, a one-eyed mother. The boy barely speaks. He dives, stays under a long time, is obsessed with seaweed; the village calls him the seaweed boy. Later he has a sister, Lotte.

He works as a servant in a baron's house, then lives in Berlin off odd jobs and a friend who teaches him how to look at paintings and how to forge them, and meets a young woman named Ingeborg. Then the war: he goes with the Wehrmacht to Romania, Ukraine, Crimea, and in a Romanian castle watches through a passage in the wall as the baron's daughter takes a Romanian general to bed — a general whose own men will later crucify him.

What decides his life is a Ukrainian village called Kostekino. Billeted in an empty house, he finds a stack of notebooks behind the stove. They belonged to Boris Ansky, a young Soviet Jewish writer who joined the revolution, ghosted for a science-fiction writer who was later purged, and did not survive either.seed ⑤ Reiter reads the notebooks over and over — on revolution, on Courbet's paintings, on what it is to write under someone else's name. This is his real education. Later, at the Dnieper, a bullet goes through his throat and he lives.

After the war he is held in an Allied prison camp. An elderly minor official there, Leo Sammer, tells him his story in the evenings: during the war a trainload of Greek Jews was delivered to the small district he administered, with no orders and nobody to take them off his hands; he filed reports and nobody answered; in the end he had his men and some local teenagers march them into the woods and shoot them in batches. He keeps insisting he had no choice and that every step was procedurally correct. Reiter barely interrupts. That night he strangles Sammer in his bunk, and nobody investigates.

Released, he works as a bar doorman in Cologne, rents a typewriter, writes a first novel and sends it to a publishing house. The publisher is Jacob Bubis, a Jew returned from exile in England — and Bubis's wife is the baron's daughter from the house where Reiter once served. He gives himself a pen name: Benno von Archimboldi. Half of it comes from the Mexican president Benito Juárez, half from the sixteenth-century painter who built human faces out of heaped fruit and books. He never appears under his own name again.payoff ⑤ ↑ A man who deletes himself from his own name learned the trick beside a dead man's notebooks: Ansky wrote under someone else's name, Reiter under the name of someone who does not exist, and both of them survived by disappearing.

He lives with Ingeborg until her illness kills her. He writes book after book, grows famous, and is seen less and less.payoff ① ↑ Only here does the rumour that sent four academics across the Atlantic acquire a cause — and the cause is far clumsier than any literary explanation they could have imagined.

Lotte marries and has a son, Klaus Haas.payoff ④ ↑ The boy grows up, goes to the United States, then to Mexico, opens a computer shop in Santa Teresa, and is arrested. Lotte has not seen her brother in decades — until she reads, in a German novel, a detail only family could know, and understands who the legendary writer is. She goes to the publishing house and gets word to him. Archimboldi, in his eighties, comes to see her, listens, and takes the papers about his nephew.

The last scene of the book: the old man eating ice cream in a Hamburg park beside a vendor whose surname happens to be that of the man who invented the three-flavour ice cream, and who talks at length about his family history. Then Archimboldi gets up and leaves for Mexico. Eleven hundred pages, and the book ends before he arrives.

Put the five together and the fuse is not in any person; it is in a position. All five books walk toward Santa Teresa and not one of them walks in. The academics sit by the pool there, the philosopher hangs a book out to dry, the reporter gets one person out, the police file a hundred-odd dossiers — and the one man with a reason to fight for this city, the eighty-year-old German, is not allowed by the novel to arrive. Every thread is in place, everyone is nearby, and the thing simply does not happen. That is not an unfinished book. That is the answer: this sort of thing goes on happening precisely because everyone who knows is, by a little, somewhere else.

Three scenes in slow motion

1 · The kicking in London: make you like them first

Two literature professors kick a Pakistani driver until he stops moving. What makes the scene unbreathable is not the violence but where it has been placed. Bolaño has spent a hundred pages letting you get to know these four — their jokes, their conference gossip, their arguments about a novel. You arrive at this page well disposed toward them.

The narration is nastier still. It runs in free indirect style: third person on the surface, pressed right up against the minds doing the kicking, so that we watch the shoes come down while hearing the reasons being manufactured inside — for an insulted friend, for oppressed women, for civilization itself. This is the book's first demonstration of its real subject: violence never lacks reasons. What lacks reasons is restraint.

One detail is easy to miss: the driver has no name. In this section he is "the Pakistani," and he never appears again. Eight hundred pages later, the hundred-odd women of part four have names, ages, employers, shoe sizes — and nobody follows up on them either. Whether a name is given is not the point. The point is who gets to have the next page.

2 · The clairvoyant on television: two hundred pages of held breath

The prose of The Crimes runs at almost no temperature: autopsy format, police vocabulary, the passive voice. Two hundred pages in, a reader goes strangely numb — and that is the moment Bolaño puts an old countrywoman on a trashy TV show. She sells herbs, claims second sight, and is known as the saint. Halfway through the taping something takes her over; she sees the desert on live television, sees the girls, and begins to weep and shout into the camera.

The craft here is that the emotion has been quarantined twice: it is not the narrator's, it is a character's; and that character's credibility has been deliberately pushed as low as it will go — a TV psychic, treated by host and audience as content. The cry comes from a position anyone is free not to take seriously.

Its information content is zero: she says nothing the reader does not already know. Its effect is the largest in the book. Bolaño trades two hundred pages of cold for one page of heat, then locks that heat inside an unreliable mouth so it cannot curdle into sentiment. A novel that refuses to emote finally permits one deranged old woman to cry on everyone's behalf — and in this city the only person still able to weep for the dead is precisely the person nobody will listen to.

3 · Sammer's confession: thirty pages of explanation, one sentence of consequence

What makes the near-monologue in the prison camp appalling is that the tone is entirely correct. He is aggrieved. He cites procedure. He stresses that he asked for instructions. He complains that his superiors never called back, and says he tried to get those people fed. He never uses a bloody word; he uses administrative language — placement, quota, processing, scope of duties.

Bolaño then makes a brutal choice: he supplies no rebuttal. No narrator steps in, Reiter does not interrupt, no moral judgment is offered anywhere. The passage is nothing but Sammer's voice, and the reader is made to soak in it until the logic starts to track — which is what the passage is for. It wants you to notice that you can follow him.

Then the sentence. That night Reiter strangles him; the novel spends almost nothing on it, and next morning nobody looks into it. Thirty pages of explanation against one sentence of consequence: the disproportion is the verdict. Bolaño denies the reader the pleasure of justice arriving — Reiter makes no speech and the killing means nothing afterwards. The hands close only to make the explaining stop.

The machine inside this book

2666 is not driven by plot. It runs on a set of structural devices, and once they are laid out it stops being difficult.

Device one · Five books, one hollow

The five parts stand alone, their casts barely overlap, and their idioms are entirely different: European campus comedy, a monologue of internal collapse, American noir on the road, a police archive, a German century-epic. What binds them is not a character but a place name.

Santa Teresa is invented; its original is Ciudad Juárez. From the 1990s on, large numbers of young women working there were murdered, the cases went largely unsolved, and the word femicidio entered public argument because of it. Bolaño does not adapt this. He leaves it sitting in the middle and lets five kinds of narrative rush it from five directions and miss.

Device two · The catalogue: watching yourself start to skim

Part four is most often mistaken for an author piling up material. It is doing something only a novel can do: turning the reader's fatigue into an argument.

The first ten cases you read word for word. By the thirtieth you are scanning. By the eightieth you are hunting each paragraph for the one line that differs. You are skimming murdered people — which is exactly what the city did to them. A more "literary" treatment would let a reader cry comfortably and then close the book. Bolaño refuses to provide that exit.

And he hooks the format from inside: nearly every entry contains one detail the format cannot digest — the shoes were new, she had been at the plant eleven days, it was her sister who reported her, no name but three piercings in one ear. The format numbs you; the detail jabs you while you are numb.

Device three · The Arcimboldo principle

The pen name is not casual. Giuseppe Arcimboldo's famous paintings build a human face out of fruit, vegetables, fish and books: up close, every element is only a turnip or a carp; step back and it is a face.

That is how this book handles evil. There is no center to it. The scholars are merely vain, the police merely lazy, the plants merely cutting costs, the politicians merely counting votes, the traffickers offstage, the reader merely reading — no single component is sufficient to kill anyone. Step back eleven hundred pages and it is a face. Which is also why the novel cannot name a murderer: name one, and the face collapses back into a pile of turnips.

Device four · Borrowed genres, withheld payoffs

It borrows the engine of every genre going — campus satire, the search-for-a-man mystery, noir, the police procedural, the war epic — and cuts each one off where it should pay: the scholars never find him, the reporter never files, the police never solve it, the writer never arrives. But be honest about this: it is a literary device, not a moral proof. The best thing about this book and the most suspect thing about it are the same thing, and the next section is where the argument starts.

What it forces you to think about

Question one
Turning a hundred-odd real victims into three hundred pages of autopsy report — is that commemoration or consumption?

Take the case for "this is the only honest way" at its strongest. The conventional treatment would pick three girls, give them dreams, boyfriends, a dress they saved up for, make the reader love them, and then kill them — the reader weeps, receives a cleansing sort of comfort, closes the book, and forgets by the next day. That method turns the dead into raw material for a reader's emotional experience. Bolaño refuses to supply that experience. He uses the least moving prose available, lays out more than a hundred people without omitting one, and gives none of them a starring role. This is the most complete answer possible to "they were only a statistic" — a statistic is one number, and he wrote it a hundred-odd times. And he insists on the name, the age, the payroll card, the new shoes: precisely what official files erase first.

Now take the other side at its strongest. Across those three hundred pages, the overwhelming majority of women enter the text already dead, described by their wounds, their remaining clothing and where they were penetrated; while the men in the book — scholars, police, reporters, writers — get childhoods, sexualities, philosophies, inner lives. A book premised on not turning women into material still has, on its surface, a hundred-odd female bodies being examined in sequence. Worse is the effect: repetition is supposed to wake you up, and it also desensitizes — by the eightieth case it doesn't hurt, so what exactly have those pages trained the reader to do? And what all of it bought was a masterpiece, a prize, and a writer's immortality. The dead take no share.

Neither side is a straw man, and I would rather leave it as an unresolved tension: any act that turns real atrocity into art is commemorating and extracting at the same time, and the two cannot be separated or set off against each other. Bring it home. We scroll past a disaster headline. We finish an episode of a true-crime podcast and find it rather satisfying. We repost a screenshot of the list of the dead and collect some likes. We do the thing Bolaño stands accused of every day, just at a smaller scale and without a byline.

Question two
Refusing to give an answer: honesty or evasion?

The case for honesty is powerful. The real Ciudad Juárez has no single answer: not one serial killer but a stack of overlapping causes — the mass arrival of cheap female labour, night shifts and unlit commutes, police who are incompetent and also bought, the drug economy, impunity for the wealthy, families with no standing to be heard. Any single murderer would be a lie, because the murderer is an arrangement. And the detective form carries a harmful built-in promise: that the world is solvable, and that catching the man restores order. Dismantling that promise is a refusal to issue the reader a forged certificate of reassurance.

The other side is equally powerful. "It's structural" is the most useful escape hatch of our era: once responsibility is spread evenly across everyone, nobody has to do anything on Monday morning. Writing "evil is everywhere" and writing "who took the money, who destroyed the evidence, who reclassified a murder as a crime of passion" are two completely different acts. The second is harder and more useful, and novels can absolutely do it — nineteenth-century realism did little else. Besides, Bolaño does not quite abstain: the suspect, the drug lord's son, the rumours of parties for the powerful, he supplies them all and then declines to confirm any, which is having it both ways.

Bring it home: the last time you said "it's a systemic problem," had you actually seen the mechanism, or did you just prefer not to say a particular name? Those two states feel almost identical from the inside, which is what makes it dangerous.

Question three
A lifetime of reading the best books — does it make anyone better?

This is the splinter Bolaño buried in the whole book, and he did not plan to exempt himself. The four critics are a sample of civilization: several languages, a life devoted to a great writer, exquisite talk about the literature of suffering. Then they leave a driver on the pavement, fly to a city where people are being killed, and complain about the heat by the pool.

The pessimistic side can be put hard: humanistic cultivation does not produce goodness, it produces more refined self-justification. Knowing what suffering is, and being unable to bear letting anyone suffer, are two different capacities, and the first can crowd out the second — read enough books about atrocity and atrocity becomes a familiar, discussable subject. The critics reach for humanitarian vocabulary at the exact moment they start kicking; that is not an ironic coincidence, that is the mechanism.

But the other side should not be waved away, and Bolaño himself stands on it: he did, after all, write this book. If literature really does nothing, these eleven hundred pages are their own counterexample — an extraordinarily clumsy, exhausting effort to make it impossible for a reader in Europe to know nothing about Sonora. The book also supplies direct evidence: Reiter's entire personality grows out of notebooks a dead Jewish boy left behind a stove. Writing did save one person — only with no guarantees and no way to repeat it.

So the honest answer is probably this: reading does not make you good, but it occasionally makes it impossible to pretend you don't know. What it offers is not virtue but difficulty — certain cheap sentences stop being available to your mouth. That is a pitiful return, and it is not zero. And what you are reading right now is an article about a book about more than a hundred dead women, which ought to be entered into this question too.

The motif: what Bolaño kept writing

Line his books up and the same obsessions keep surfacing; 2666 is their sum.

First: the poet and the murderer are the same man. In Distant Star an air-force pilot-poet writes his poems in the sky with an aircraft and photographs the women he has killed, then mounts a private exhibition of the pictures. Set that novella beside 2666 and you see Bolaño asking one question for thirty years: why can aesthetic capacity and cruelty grow in the same person, and feed each other.

Second: literature upstairs, torture in the basement. By Night in Chile gives us a priest and literary critic who tutors the junta in Marxism and also attends literary salons at a villa where people are being interrogated downstairs; the guests know, and go on discussing poetry. The four academics by the pool in part one are that scene, enlarged.

Third: set out to find someone, fail, disperse in the desert. The Savage Detectives is nothing but young poets chasing a vanished woman poet, ending in the Sonora desert — the same desert as Santa Teresa. He wrote one motion his whole life: further and further out, losing the reason for looking somewhere along the way, with the desert always there. He also had a compulsion for catalogues: Nazi Literature in the Americas is an entire book of invented author entries, part four of 2666 an entire book of victim files — he did not believe in the representative case; he wanted all of them.

Criticism and controversy

  • The unfinished question. Bolaño left instructions to split the book into five volumes published a year apart so his family would have steady income; his heirs and editor published it as one volume with a note explaining the decision. The editor also reported a jotting among his papers saying that the narrator of 2666 is Arturo Belano, the alter ego who recurs across his fiction — though nothing in the text establishes that. Add several threads (the Penitent, the American sheriff) that simply stop, and the unavoidable question is: are these severed ends design, or did he run out of time? Nobody can answer, including the book's warmest champions.
  • The ethics of part four. The charge: women exist overwhelmingly as corpses and injury inventories while male characters get full interiority, and a real femicide crisis was made into a monument aimed at the European and American literary market, with the proceeds going to the author. The defence — including from some feminist critics — is that precisely this refusal to aestheticize is what makes it impossible, for once, to treat these deaths as background noise. There is no consensus in this argument, and there should not be.
  • The Bolaño myth. After his death the English-language publishing and media apparatus largely packaged him as a legend: exile, junkie, rogue of Latin American letters. The heroin claim rests mainly on reading an autobiographical-sounding short piece as documentary; his widow and several friends publicly denied it. Accounts also differ over whether he was in Chile during the 1973 coup and briefly detained. Stated here only as an existing dispute.
  • The estate fight. Around 2010 his widow moved the rights from the publisher he had worked with for years to an international literary agency; the critic who had prepared 2666 was cut out and said so publicly. It has nothing to do with the work, but it changed how the remaining papers were handled.
  • The right to the material. The Juárez killings were still going on and families were still fighting for investigations when a Chilean-Spanish writer turned them into a novel and won enormous prestige in Europe and the United States while the actual cases stalled; Mexican critics have raised this. In fairness, Bolaño wrote after consulting the journalist who had spent years investigating the case, and put him into the book. But the question of who is entitled to tell this story does not dissolve because the author behaved decently.

Ten sentences that stay

1. The center of this book is a hole: five novels walk toward one border city, none of them enters, and that is the answer.

2. The murderer is unnamed not out of laziness — naming one would tell the reader "catch him and it's over," and that is a lie.

3. Those three hundred pages of autopsy reports are not piled-up material. They are measuring something: which victim you start skimming at.

4. A hundred pages to make you like four scholars, then two of them kicking a driver on the ground: violence never lacks reasons; restraint does.

5. Arcimboldo heaped fruit into a face; Bolaño heaps vanity, sloth, cost-cutting and votes into evil. Up close each one is innocent.

6. The geometry book on the clothesline is the novel's self-portrait: a text thrown into the desert wind to see what survives.

7. The worst thing about Sammer's confession is not that he killed people. It is that you can follow his grievance.

8. The only person permitted to cry out loud is a psychic on daytime television — in this city, whoever can still weep for the dead is whoever nobody will hear.

9. The eighty-year-old writer is finally going to Mexico, and the novel ends one sentence before he leaves. Always short by a little.

10. It will not make you better, but a few cheap sentences will stop being available to you. That is roughly all this book offers, and it is not nothing.
