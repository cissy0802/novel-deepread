All Quiet on the Western Front

Im Westen nichts Neues · Erich Maria Remarque · 1929

中文 →

Nine kilometres behind the line, the company halts in a meadow for breakfast. A hundred and fifty men went up; eighty came back. The cooks have prepared rations for a hundred and fifty and the head cook stands over his pots refusing to hand out more than one portion a man, because that is what the regulations say.
Somebody asks him who exactly he is saving the other seventy portions for.
After some argument every man gets a double helping, and double tobacco. They eat until they are full, propped against their packs, and one of them belches and says it has been a good day.
A book about a generation destroyed begins with a breakfast that left everyone stuffed.

In one sentence

A nineteen-year-old schoolboy enlists with his whole class at his teacher's urging, and over two years learns to identify shells by sound, to take boots off the dead, and to spend a day and a night in a shell hole beside a French printer he has just knifed; and by the time his friends have all been killed and he is shot himself, on an unnaturally quiet day in October 1918, the army's report for the day runs to a single line: all quiet on the Western Front.

Coordinates

Erich Maria Remarque (1898–1970) was born in Osnabrück in north-west Germany and trained as a schoolteacher. Conscripted in 1916, he reached the Western Front in June 1917 and was hit by shell splinters in the arm, leg and neck on 31 July; he spent the rest of the war in a military hospital in Duisburg. His time at the front amounts to a few weeks — a fact used to attack him for the rest of his life, and one this page deals with head-on further down.

The novel was serialized in the Vossische Zeitung from November 1928 and published in book form in January 1929; it sold over a million copies in Germany within the year. It was not the first novel about the First World War — Barbusse's Under Fire appeared in 1916, Jünger's Storm of Steel in 1920, covering the same war in the same dense detail and concluding that war forges men. What is singular here is not the material but the frame: this is the book that nailed the word "generation" into literature, so that from then on you could discuss a war without discussing nations or victory, only what happened irreversibly to one cohort of people. The 1930 Hollywood film won Best Picture; that December, Nazi party members disrupted its Berlin screenings by releasing mice and throwing stink bombs, and the film was promptly banned in Germany. In 1933 Remarque's books were on the burning lists.

This book is neither an accusation nor a confession. It only tries to give an account of a generation that was destroyed by the war, even where it escaped the shells.the epigraph · paraphrased here, not quoted

The story

Act one · A lesson, and eighty rations (1916–1917)

The narrator, Paul Bäumer, is nineteen. After that double breakfast the men walk out to the latrines — plain wooden boxes standing in the open field, no screens — and sit in a row playing cards, passing letters around, taking the sun. The change does not begin with the first shot fired. It begins when shame disappears.

Two years earlier. Kantorek, the schoolmaster who took them for gym, spent weeks of class time on duty and the fatherland, and finally marched the whole form to the recruiting office. Twenty boys, one hand going up after another. Only the heavy boy, Josef Behm, said out loud that he did not want to go; the silence of the teacher and the eyes of his classmates pushed him to the back of the queue and then over the line, and he signed tooseed ①. He is the first of the class to die: shot through the eye, crawling in no man's land, nobody able to find him, and it emerges later that he had not been killed outright.

Basic training is handed to Himmelstoss, a postman in peacetime and now the corporal drilling them. He has them crawl through mud, scrub a barrack floor with a toothbrush, turn out eight times in one night; he also invents a cure for bed-wetters — two of them assigned to a bunk bed on alternating nights, so the lower one gets a turn at the consequences. Tjaden is one of the two. On their last evening before the front they throw a bedcover over Himmelstoss's head in the dark, pull his trousers down and give him a thorough beating with a whipseed ②. It reads like a schoolboy prank; Remarque will use it later to ask a distinctly uncomfortable question.

The squad at the front comes down to these men: Katczinsky — Kat — forty years old, a cobbler in civilian life and the most competent human being in the book, able to conjure a goose or a straw mattress out of bare ground; Kropp, the clearest thinker; Müller, who still carries his textbooks in his pack in case the school examinations resume; Leer, the first of them to grow a beard; Haie Westhus, an enormous peat-digger; Detering, a farmer who talks about nothing but his land and his horses; and Tjaden, a locksmith and the biggest eater in the company.

Act two · Kemmerich's boots (1917)

Their classmate Franz Kemmerich is in the field hospital. His leg has already been amputated; he does not know it yet, and only wonders why his foot hurts so much. His mother wept at the station and asked Paul to look after her boy — that request now sits by the bed, and nobody mentions it.

Müller keeps looking at the boots under the bed: English airman's boots, knee-high, soft yellow leather, the finest pair in the companyseed ③. Paul thinks him callous, and then works it out: the moment Kemmerich dies the boots go to an orderly and no one in the squad will ever see them again. Here, whether an object reaches a friend's feet is the only thing these men still get to decide. Müller is not callous. He is one of the few people left in this world still willing to plan one step ahead on a friend's behalf.

Kemmerich dies that afternoon. Paul runs for the doctor, who is on his fifth amputation and says wait; by the time Paul gets back the bed is already being cleared, because there are others outside waiting for the space. Paul walks out into the night with the boots under his arm and finds himself suddenly hungry, suddenly aware of the strength in his legs, suddenly glad to be alive. Remarque will not let him mourn. The hardest judgements in this book are hidden in reactions like that one: grief takes slack to pay for, and these men have none left.

Act three · Front-line arithmetic: shells, horses, recruits (1917)

Replacements arrive, sixteen and seventeen years old. In their first bombardment one of them burrows into Paul's chest, shaking, his trousers wet; Paul tells him it is nothing, that bigger men than him have done it. Half that draft is dead within days: they cannot yet tell which whistle means get down, and they do not know to climb into a hole that has just been blown, because the same spot is rarely hit twice.

The shelling reaches the horses too. Wounded animals scream in the open all night, and the sound is not equine, it is human; it goes on until men go out and shoot them. Detering crouches shaking and says that men killing men is one thing, but dragging animals into it is the vilest thing there isseed ④.

The worst night of all takes place in a cemetery: shells heave the coffins out of the ground, the long-dead are thrown into the air a second time, and the living lie down in the opened graves to shelter from splinters. Then comes gas. Paul counts seconds inside the grey-green fog and watches a recruit pull his mask off too early and cough up pieces of burnt lung. Another recruit has his hip smashed; Kat says quietly that a bullet would spare him two more days of this, and they have the pistol out when footsteps approach and the moment passes. What those two were debating was not whether to save a man, but what being good to him consisted of.

It is on a night like this that Paul records the book's central sensation: when the shelling starts a man throws himself down, presses his face into the soil, talks into it, burrows — for a soldier the earth is his only friend, brother and mother, and every fold of it may be worth a lifeseed ⑤. He also sees two butterflies come over the parapet and settle on the teeth of a skull.

Then the big attack. Flamethrowers come up; Haie's back is torn open and you can see his lung working as he breathes. The man who carries him out is Himmelstoss — who, minutes earlier, the tyrant of the parade ground, was crouched in the corner of a dugout feigning a wound until Paul screamed him out of itpayoff ② ↑. This is the fine move: the novel could have kept him a villain and let the reader enjoy it. Remarque refuses — the man is not a born brute, he simply became one inside a system that handed him power. Himmelstoss is later transferred to the cookhouse and starts sending them decent food, and the account is considered settled; which is much harder to accept than having him blown up, and much truer.

What the veterans talk about instead is chance. A man bends to tie a lace and a splinter goes through the spot where his head just was. And it is precisely because everything is decided by chance that a man can go on living at all — if death could be avoided by being careful, every minute would drive you mad.

Act four · The door of his own room (summer 1917)

Paul gets seventeen days' leave. The train, the familiar street, his sister shouting up the stairs that Paul is home — and he stands in the hallway unable to stop crying, still in uniform with his pack on his back. His mother is in bed with cancer. She peels him an apple and asks whether it is very bad out there, and he says no, there are a lot of us, we manage. The one thing he learns on this leave is how to lie to the people closest to him.

He goes up to his room. The bookshelf is where it was; on the desk sit the half-finished play he was writing before the war, Saul, and a sheaf of poems. He pulls the books out one by one and waits for something to come back to him out of them — and nothing happens. The words are still words, he recognizes them, and they no longer speak to him. He has not lost the books. He has lost the person who used to stand between himself and them.

There is more in the streets. His father wants to take him round to friends so he can tell them about the front; a German master in the beer garden thumps the table explaining where the breakthrough ought to come. But the sharpest stroke is on the barracks square: his classmate Mittelstaedt is now a sergeant, and among the recruits under him is Kantorek — the schoolmaster has been called up, is squeezed into a cut-down old tunic, and is being made to squat and stand while his own former pupil hands him back his classroom phrases one at a timepayoff ① ↑. This is not satisfying. It is a piece of cold arithmetic: nothing about Kantorek's humiliation converts into anything about the night Behm spent in no man's land, and the books will never balance. Kantorek also writes to them, addressing them as "Iron Youth." Paul's answer is that there is no youth here — of the twenty in that class, some are dead, some are maimed, and one is in an asylum.

On his last night he kneels by his mother's bed thinking: I should not have come home. In this chapter, "you can't go back" is not a figure of speech. It is a specific room and a specific shelf of books.

Before returning he is posted to guard a camp of Russian prisoners. On the far side of the wire, men wait at the swill barrels for scraps, sing, make violins. Paul passes his cigarettes through and breaks up the potato cakes his mother baked, and stands there thinking: one order turned these quiet men into my enemies, and another order could turn them back; who gives that order, and by what right. He does not follow the thought further — he knows that if he does he will not be able to go back to the front.

Then the Kaiser comes to inspect. The whole company is issued new uniforms, which are collected again afterwards. That evening they discuss how wars start. Someone says one country offends another, and Tjaden points out that no mountain of his ever offended a mountain in France; Kropp's proposal is that before any war the ministers and generals of both countries should meet in an arena in swimming trunks, armed with clubs, and whoever wins, his country wins.

Act five · The man in the shell hole (spring 1918)

On a reconnaissance patrol the barrage pins Paul in a shell hole. He plays dead with his face in the mud. A man jumps in — French uniform. Paul does not think; his hand has already gone in with the knife, three times.

The man does not die at once. He gurgles and breathes in the same hole for a day and a night. By daylight Paul can see his face. He crawls over, opens the man's collar, gives him water from his own flask, wads a handkerchief against the chest wound — the hand that stabbed him three hours ago now dressing the wound, a symmetry on which Remarque offers no comment whatever. Around midday the man dies. Paul stays where he is a long while and finally goes through his papers: Gérard Duval, compositor, with photographs of a wife and a small girl.

He talks to the corpse at length, promising to send money, to write, to tell the wife how it happened; he even decides he will learn the printing trade. That night he crawls back to his own trench and tells Kat and Kropp about it. They take one look and say that this is nothing, that if you hadn't knifed him he'd have knifed you. The papers are never sent. The novel does not show us the moment he changes his mind; it shows him slowly, almost gratefully, accepting the account his comrades have handed him — and that is the real ending of the chapter.

Afterwards they guard a supply depot in an evacuated village, and it is a good stretch: mattresses, tinned peaches, a suckling pig, potato pancakes fried in a house with its roof blown off, the smoke drawing fire, the men carrying the pan somewhere else and going on frying. The book writes food as closely as it writes death — for a man reduced to his body, pleasure has only that one channel left.

Then comes the evacuation of a village, and Paul and Kropp are hit together. On the hospital train he does not dare get into clean sheets because he is filthy. They are put off at a Catholic hospital where, at the end of the corridor, there is a Dying Room that nobody comes out of. Hamacher in the next bed has a head wound and a doctor's certificate in his pocket stating that he periodically loses responsibility for his actions; he calls it his shooting licence, since nothing he says can be held against him. Another man, Lewandowski, has been lying there most of a year and has not seen his wife in two; when she finally arrives with the baby, the entire ward watches the door, minds the child and turns its face away. In a book famous for its coldness, this is the one moment when everyone conspires to let something tender happen.

Kropp's leg is taken off at the thigh. He says he will not live as a cripple. When Paul is discharged back to the front, Kropp is still lying there, very quiet.

Act six · Subtraction (1918)

What remains is subtraction, and it goes quickly.

Müller is shot point-blank in the stomach with a Verey pistol and burns a long time before he dies; he hands Paul the English boots firstpayoff ③ ↑, and Paul takes them and casually promises them to Tjaden when his turn comes. The boots set out on Kemmerich's feet, passed through Müller, are now on Paul's, and are already booked for the next man — an object outliving the people who wear it, by a wide margin. Nothing else in the novel explains attrition so cheaply.

Berger runs out under fire to rescue a wounded messenger dog and is hit in the pelvis; the man who goes to carry him in takes a bullet in the leg. Bertinck, the company commander, shoots a flamethrower operator and is hit in the chest, and a splinter then takes off his chin — the same splinter goes through Leer's hip, and he bleeds out in minutes. All Paul records is that Leer was the best mathematician in the class, and that it is of no use to him now.

Detering leaves on his own. He sees a cherry tree in blossom in a farmyard, stands looking at it for a long time and talks about his orchard. A few days later he is gone, heading straight for Germany, and the military police pick him up almost at once — the direction was too obvious. After the court-martial the squad never hears anything about him againpayoff ④ ↑. The precision of this is that he is not broken by terror, but by a flowering tree: what destroys a man is never the worst thing on the battlefield, it is the sudden leak of ordinary life.

Last comes Kat. A splinter smashes his shin, and Paul picks him up and carries him a long way, setting him down twice to rest and talk. At the dressing station the orderly takes one look and says the man is dead. Paul says that is impossible, he was talking to him. The orderly shows him a small hole in Kat's head — another splinter, somewhere along the way, with Paul carrying him and noticing nothing. He has carried a dead man for kilometres and cannot say at which step he died. That is chance, the thing they discussed for a whole book, making its last move.

Autumn 1918: rumours everywhere of an armistice. Paul has swallowed some gas and is given fourteen days' rest. Alone, he goes over the years: he is twenty, and all he has been taught is despair, death, fear and a kind of life force locked inside a body; even if he gets home alive he has no idea what to do with himself.

He dies in October 1918. The final paragraph switches abruptly to the third person: he fell on a day so still along the whole front that the army report confined itself to one line — all quiet on the Western Front. When they turned him over his face was calm, almost as though he were glad it had ended this way. He had fallen forward onto the earthpayoff ⑤ ↑.

The fuse is a classroom. Not the shots at Sarajevo — the book never once says who started the war or why. What lights everything is Kantorek spending a few weeks of lesson time looking at twenty boys, one after another, until every hand is up; and everything downstream of it — the boots, the gas, the printer in the shell hole, the door at the end of the corridor — is the aftershock of that lesson. His real accusation is not about who fired first. It is about who gets to tell people who are not yet grown that this is their duty.

Three scenes in slow motion

1 · The shell hole: writing a killing as something that takes a long time

In films, killing takes a second. Remarque stretches it over a day and a night, in three layers. The first is locking the narration inside the hole: the reader is stuck there with Paul, knows nothing of what is happening outside, and can measure time only by the other man's breathing. The second is sequence — Paul sees the wound first, then the face, and only last the name. Putting the name last is the most important piece of information design in the book: a man turns back from obstacle into person in three steps, and Paul cannot undo any of them. The third is the reversal of gesture: the hand that went in with the knife spends the following day doing the opposite — opening a collar, giving water, holding a dressing in place. Remarque passes no moral judgement at all. He simply has one pair of hands do both things.

The ending is harsher still. He does not write Paul into an anti-war conversion; he writes Paul returning to the trench, being soothed by two sentences, and feeling relieved. The damage is not that day. The damage is that after it he learned how to stop such days from troubling him.

2 · The boots: letting an object keep the accounts

There is no extended mourning in this novel because the mourning has been handed to objects. The boots are the one thing that runs the whole length of the book: Kemmerich → Müller → Paul → promised to Tjaden. Every handover is a death, and the novel spends a sentence or two on each. The coldness works better than any amount of weeping, because the reader is made to do the arithmetic, and by the third handover the boots are no longer boots.

The same trick is applied to the uniforms (issued for the inspection, collected afterwards) and the rations (cooked for a hundred and fifty, eaten by eighty). The book has a fixation on numbers: it does not trust adjectives, only a ledger whose two sides refuse to match.

3 · The door of his own room: why the quietest chapter is the lethal one

The front-line chapters are all sound: guns, shouting, horses, breathing. The leave chapter has almost none — a staircase, a hallway, a bookshelf, a mother's hands peeling an apple. Remarque changes the entire tempo here: no action, just a man standing in the middle of his own adolescence waiting for something to happen, and it does not happen.

The choice of vantage is what makes the chapter work: it stays on Paul and never enters his mother's head. Give us her interior and this becomes a story about what war does to families; withhold it and it stays a colder fact — he can no longer get inside anyone, his mother included. The claim that a generation was destroyed even where it escaped the shells is not proved in the trenches. It is proved in this room.

The central device: a radius of perception

The device at the heart of this novel is not a premise but a restriction: write only what one ordinary soldier's body can perceive, write it in the present tense, and say "we" wherever "I" can be avoided. Each of the three rules has consequences.

Rule one: stay inside the radius. No maps, no unit designations, no names of battles, no politicians, no statistics. The world beyond the line leaks in only as rumour, orders and letters from home. The consequence is that the book is structurally incapable of explaining the war — it can only absorb it. Many readers take this for a limitation of Remarque's thinking; it is in fact his method: the moment the narrator is allowed the big picture, the sensation of being material in someone else's hands disappears.

Rule two: the present tense. There is no retrospective narrator who has had time to work things out. The tense is itself the argument — a man still inside an event has no capacity to sum it up.

Rule three: "we" before "I." Sentence after sentence takes a collective subject: we eat, we duck, we are no longer young. This registers the small group as the last thing holding these men up, and it explains why, once Kat is dead, the novel has no structural reason left for Paul to go on living.

What holds up: the physiological accuracy is extraordinary — hunger, incontinence, the ear's discrimination between trajectories, the body's dependence on the ground. None of that can be invented; it comes from two years of listening in a military hospital and from the splinters in his own body.

What is literary necessity rather than record: Paul's language. A nineteen-year-old schoolboy under bombardment does not produce paragraph-length interior monologue with its imagery in good order; the summarizing passages about a generation are really the man at the desk in 1928 speaking on behalf of the soldier of 1918. That is what the novel needs, not what the documents show. Reading this book as a historical source is a category error — it is a highly finished work of art, not a field notebook.

The device breaks exactly where the title is: a first-person present tense cannot narrate its own death, and cannot narrate afterwards. So the final paragraph has to switch to the third person and the camera has to jump to the outermost ring — to that day's communiqué. The whole book completes itself in that jump: the reader has just spent two hundred pages learning how specific Paul's world is, and is then told that from the outside nothing happened that day. The title starts as a piece of military boilerplate and ends as the coldest verdict in the novel.

What it makes you think about

Question 1
A book that made millions of people cry, and it did not stop 1939. What is an anti-war novel actually for?

The case that it does nothing — or worse — can be put hard: the book sold over a million copies in Germany, and plenty of those readers went to a second war a decade later. Worse, it supplies war with an aesthetic: comradeship in the trench, a shared meal, the sensory intensity of pressing your face into the soil. That is what a great many readers actually carry away. An anti-war novel delivers a feeling, and a feeling is worst at exactly the thing that matters — being operative three years later, at a ballot or a call-up.

The other side: asking one novel to stop a war on its own is a test designed for it to fail. What it did do is something else. It made "it is sweet and fitting to die for one's country" embarrassing to say aloud in German, and that kind of change in a language outlives several generations; it let people who had never been to a front imagine a trench without going through official histories. And the banning, the burning, the organized release of mice in the cinemas are themselves the evidence: nobody goes to that much trouble to burn a book that does nothing.

Bring it home: which of your own decisions was changed by the footage, the documentaries, the first-hand testimony you have seen over the years? Or did they give you two bad hours one afternoon, and did those two hours end up standing in for doing anything?

Question 2
In the shell hole, should Paul have gone through the man's pockets?

The "should not" case is stronger than you would like: he is going back onto that line tomorrow, and he will have to shoot. Knowing that the man opposite was called Gérard Duval, set type for a living and had a small daughter is a pure liability — and not only his own, because a man who hesitates for half a second before firing gets the people beside him killed too. Training soldiers not to see the other side as human is neither stupidity nor simple cruelty; it is an effective adaptation. Kat's "this is nothing" sounds cold-blooded, and is in fact an older man pulling a young one back out of a place he should not have gone into.

The "should" case: if the colour of a uniform is sufficient to decide who dies, what distinguishes a man from a device? That half-second of hesitation is the whole of what civilization has left. And the bill cannot be dodged — unpaid in the shell hole, it comes due some night thirty years later. The postwar collapse of so many men was not caused by their having seen too much, but by their having been required at the time to see nothing.

Bring it home: does your own work contain a step where it is better not to think about who is on the other end? A payroll number on a layoff list, a name in a collections call, a person folded into one line of a metric. Why are those processes almost always designed so that no face is visible — for efficiency, or so the person executing them can sleep?

Question 3
Is Kantorek guilty?

Not guilty: he fired nothing, ordered nothing, lied about nothing — he said what all of Germany believed in 1914, and he believed it himself. He was later conscripted, put into a cut-down tunic and drilled by his own pupils, which is a kind of payment. Holding a provincial schoolmaster responsible for an empire's war does not follow; the reason the bill lands on him is that he is the only link in the chain his students can reach. That is scapegoating, not justice.

Guilty: sincerity has never been a defence. What he held was not a weapon but authority — and to an eighteen-year-old, the word "coward" from the front of a classroom has roughly the force of law. He did not have to compel anyone; he only had to look along the row, and the eyes of the other boys finished the job. The only boy in the class who said he did not want to go was also the first to die. Paul's verdict is exact: they should have been the ones leading the way, and instead they handed a set of ready-made phrases to people with no means yet of judging them.

Bring it home: have you ever used the tone of "this is for your own good" to push someone younger down a road — graduate school, a startup, a career change, two more years of sticking it out? You genuinely believed it was right at the time. Is believing it enough?

Question 4
"A destroyed generation" — is that an explanation or a licence?

An explanation: the damage is real. A man whose entire skill set before the age of twenty is reading shellfire, taking boots off corpses and sleeping beside bodies is not going to reconnect with ordinary life, and that is a predictable outcome, not weakness. Demanding that such men climb out by themselves is the cheapest kind of morality.

A licence: that self-description was later put to large-scale use in Germany — the "front generation" became a whole political rhetoric, and the Freikorps and later the SA were full of men narrating their own destruction. Once a group accepts the identity of people who cannot return to normal life, it has also collected a permit to stop answering for what it does. Remarque himself is plainly not on that side: he left Germany in 1931 and was stripped of his citizenship in 1938, and his sister Elfriede was condemned to death by the People's Court in 1943. But it has to be said honestly — this book supplied that rhetoric with its most moving version.

Bring it home: at what point does trauma stop being "this explains why I am like this" and become "and therefore I need not change"? Who gets to draw that line — the person himself, or the people he affects?

The recurring obsession

Remarque spent his life writing not about the dead but about the ones who failed to die. He followed this novel immediately with The Road Back (1931), picking up on the demobilization train: men returning to a country that has kept no place for them.

After that the obsession simply moves house. Three Comrades (1936) gives three veterans a small repair garage, not enough money, tuberculosis and a short horizon, so that all they can do is love the present as hard as possible. Arch of Triumph (1945) is about undocumented refugees in Paris, a German doctor forbidden to practise who buys the right to stay by operating for others — how a piece of paper decides whether a person exists is the same problem that began with a soldier's number. Spark of Life (1952) is set in a camp and is dedicated to his executed sister; The Night in Lisbon (1962) prices a visa.

The obsessions come out clearly: a small group of people and a shared meal are all that stands between a person and nothing; love compressed into the present because the time is known to be short; and hardest of all, survival as a problem requiring an account rather than a happy ending. Seen in that line, All Quiet is the origin: it is not about being killed, it is about the part these men had already paid while they were still alive.

Criticism and controversy

  • How long was he actually at the front. Remarque reached the Western Front in June 1917, was wounded on 31 July and spent the rest of the war in hospital behind the lines; his time in the trenches is counted in weeks. The Weimar right made a great deal of this — a 1930 attack pamphlet asked whether Remarque had ever really lived, written under the pen name Mynona (the philosopher Salomo Friedlaender) — and the Nazis circulated the story that his real name was Kramer and that he was Jewish, which was false. The honest description is that this is not a war report: much of its detail came from two years of listening in a hospital ward. But the argument that an author is not entitled to write it does not survive contact with literature generally — by that standard very few books would be left.
  • The literary objections. The structure is episodic, one scene per chapter, with characters who do not develop but only get used up; the lyrical passages, especially the summaries about a generation, are full to the brim, and some readers feel Remarque is doing their feeling for them. The cast is functional: the farmer, the locksmith, the cobbler, the clear thinker, the boy with the textbooks — one label each, and little interior life outside Paul and Kat. It is conspicuously more readable than Storm of Steel, and that readability was itself suspected at the time: the publisher mounted an enormous campaign and the novel was the bestseller of the war-book boom.
  • Women barely exist as people. The mother and sister are objects of protection and concealment, and the book gives them not one judgement of their own; the three Frenchwomen across the canal, approached with bread and sausage, are handled lightly in a way that reads badly today; the wife who comes to the hospital is functionally a piece of warmth. The women here wait, nurse and comfort, and are rarely granted a situation of their own.
  • Political vagueness. It indicts the war without asking whose war it was, why it was fought or who profited; responsibility is assigned to "the war," a force with no subject, while the high command, the armaments industry and the Kaiser stay out of frame. Critics pointed out at the time that this lets the actually responsible off; the attack from the right was the exact opposite, that the book dishonoured the soldier. Being hit from both sides confirms that it stands in a place where no conclusion is drawn — which is both its honesty and its limit.
  • Do not mistake the adaptations for the book. A. W. Wheen's 1929 translation is the classic English text, but the first British and American editions cut or softened some of the coarser and more sexual passages. The famous ending of the 1930 Hollywood film — Paul reaching for a butterfly and being shot by a sniper — is the film's invention and is not in the novel; the 2022 German film adds a parallel armistice-negotiation plot and moves Paul's death to the last minutes before the ceasefire on 11 November, whereas the novel puts it on an unremarkable day in October with no dramatic timing whatsoever. That "he almost made it" pathos is precisely what Remarque refused.

Ten sentences to take away

1. A hundred and fifty men went up and eighty came back, and the surplus rations were the best news of the day: the book opens on a breakfast that left everyone stuffed.

2. The change does not begin with the first shot. It begins when shame goes, and they sit in a row on open latrine boxes playing cards.

3. The only boy in the class who said he did not want to go was the first to die. The sentence needs no commentary.

4. Müller eyeing a dying friend's boots is not callousness: whether an object reaches a friend's feet is the last thing these men still decide.

5. Those English boots go from Kemmerich to Müller to Paul and are booked for the next man — the object outlives its wearers by a wide margin.

6. The hand that knifed the Frenchman spends the following day giving him water and holding his wound shut. Remarque passes no judgement at all.

7. The damage is not that day in the shell hole; it is that afterwards he learned how to keep such days from troubling him.

8. The deadliest chapter contains no gunfire: he stands at his own bookshelf waiting for the books to speak to him, and nothing happens.

9. He carried Kat for kilometres and learned only at the dressing station that he had been carrying a dead man — chance making its last move.

10. You have just spent a whole book learning how specific this one life was, and are then told that from the outermost ring, nothing happened that day.
