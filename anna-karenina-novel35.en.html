Anna Karenina

Leo Tolstoy · serialized 1875–1877 / book 1878

A Moscow station, steam smearing the platform white. A woman steps down from her carriage; she has come to talk her brother out of a divorce. Ahead of her the crowd is pressing in around something: a watchman has been crushed by a train backing up. He'd been drinking, someone says. He never heard it coming, says someone else.
She goes back to the carriage and asks, quietly, whether something might be given to his family. Then she says it is a bad omen.
Eight hundred pages later she will walk onto a different stretch of track herself.

This page contains complete spoilers — how Anna dies, what Karenin says at her bedside, where Vronsky ends up, and the thing Levin decides not to tell anyone on the last page. The premise of this site is that everything worth discussing lives past the ending. If you want to keep the suspense, read the novel first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

A woman held firmly in place by a husband, a son and the whole of Petersburg society falls in love with an officer and then does the thing women of her time almost never did — she refuses to hide it, and says so out loud; society does not kill her, it simply and very politely edges her toward a place where there is no room to stand, until she walks onto the rails herself. Meanwhile, on a second track running alongside, a clumsy provincial landowner marries, farms, buries his brother, has a son, and finds a reason to go on living in a passing remark from a peasant — one book, one life going down and one going up, and Tolstoy explains nothing: he sets the two side by side and lets you look.

Where it sits

Leo Tolstoy (1828–1910) had finished War and Peace and meant to write a historical novel about the age of Peter the Great. It never came. Anna Karenina was begun in 1873 and serialized in The Russian Messenger from 1875 through 1877. The final part — Part Eight — was refused by the editor, Mikhail Katkov, because Tolstoy wrote with open coldness about the volunteer movement then sending Russians to fight for Serbia, a cause Katkov himself was promoting. Tolstoy published Part Eight separately as a pamphlet at his own expense; the complete novel appeared as a book in 1878.

Its place in literary history is easier to name than that of War and Peace: that one is an epic, this one is the novel's coming of age. It no longer needs Napoleon to hold the stage. It writes what happens inside one household — an affair, a divorce, who gets the child, whether the invitation arrives — and then makes those things weigh as much as a war.

The story

Act one · Someone comes to patch a marriage, and a proposal fails

Moscow. The Oblonsky house is in chaos. Stiva (Stepan Arkadyich) has been sleeping with the French governess, and his wife Dolly has found the letters. Stiva's reaction is the funniest and coldest stroke in the book: it isn't that he feels nothing, it's that he genuinely cannot see what he did wrong — he has always been good to his wife, he simply happened to fall for someone else, and what is a man supposed to do about that? He wakes on the study sofa and the first thing he remembers is a pleasant dream.

To clean up the mess he sends for his sister from Petersburg: Anna Arkadyevna Karenina. Anna is the kind of woman whose arrival changes the air in a room — about thirty, married to a senior government official, mother of an eight-year-old, universally liked because she has both tact and life in her. She has come to mediate, and she succeeds: she holds Dolly's hands, says a few sentences, and Dolly forgives her husband in tears. Fix that in your mind: the first thing that happens in this novel is Anna rescuing a marriage a husband has wrecked.

On the day she arrives, a watchman is crushed by a train backing up along the platform. Anna hears it, sees the crowd, turns and asks quietly whether something can be given to the man's family. A young officer who has come to meet his mother — Count Alexei Kirillovich Vronsky — hands over two hundred roubles without a word. It is the first gift he gives her, and he pays for it with a stranger's death. Before boarding, Anna says it was a bad omen.Seed ①

Another man is in Moscow that day. Konstantin Levin, a provincial landowner, awkward, blunt, with nowhere to put his hands in a drawing room, has come to propose to Kitty Shcherbatskaya, Dolly's eighteen-year-old sister. He proposes; she refuses him — not because she dislikes him, but because she believes Vronsky is about to speak. She is entirely wrong. At the ball she watches Anna, in black velvet, dance with Vronsky. Anna isn't flirting; she looks, if anything, frightened of herself. But Kitty reads the light coming off the two of them: what she sees is not a betrayal but something already happening that nobody can stop. Kitty collapses on the spot, falls ill, and is sent to a German spa. Levin goes home to his fields.

Anna flees too. She takes the night train back to Petersburg with a small lamp lit and an English novel open on her lap, and she cannot read it. Tolstoy is exact about why: the people in the book are falling in love, nursing the sick, making speeches in Parliament, and she does not want to read about other people living — she wants to live.Seed ② The train stops in a blizzard; she steps out for air, the snow whipping sideways, and Vronsky is standing under the lamp. He offers no explanation, only that he goes where he must be, which is where she is.

At Petersburg her husband, Karenin (Alexei Alexandrovich), is waiting on the platform. The first thing she notices about him is that his ears stick out. Eight years of marriage crack open on a pair of ears — Tolstoy doesn't write psychological analysis, he writes the moment you begin to be repelled by one specific physical part of a person, and can never again not see it. And note: her husband and her lover have the same first name. That is not a coincidence, it is the author's finger pressed to the back of your neck.

Act two · The steeplechase: a mare's back, and a sentence said out loud

The next year, in one line: Anna resists, fails, and begins to lie. She and Vronsky become lovers. Tolstoy writes the aftermath of their first night in no register of happiness at all — Vronsky stands looking at her the way a murderer looks at the thing he has just killed, a thing he had wanted. In the 1870s this was outrageous: novelists writing adultery wrote either sin or liberation, and Tolstoy wrote the scene of something living being destroyed.

Anna becomes pregnant. When she tells him, Vronsky's first thought is that the situation must be resolved, and his second is of duels and his career. He is not a bad man; he has simply never been required to give everything for anything — and no man in this book ever is.

Then the steeplechase. Vronsky rides his English mare Frou-Frou, an animal written more vividly than most of the people: lean, high-strung, in a kind of understanding with her rider. He rides beautifully until the last ditch, when he fails to follow her landing and settles back in the saddle a fraction too late. That one movement breaks her back. She lies in the grass thrashing, looking at him. He kicks her in the belly, and an officer comes to put her down. Tolstoy gives him a verdict disguised as a thought: for the first time in his life he knew the worst kind of misfortune — the kind he had caused himself, and could not undo.Seed ③

In the stands, Anna gives herself away the instant Vronsky goes down. She cries out in front of half of Petersburg, and she knows immediately that it is too late. In the carriage home Karenin begins to reason with her in his own manner — not angry, merely reminding her about appearances, about decorum, about not giving people something to laugh at. Listening to him, she suddenly stops performing altogether. I am his mistress, she says. I cannot bear you, I am afraid of you, I hate you. Throwing it at him is a release. The hinge of the whole novel is here, and it is not the adultery — it is the confession. Had she lied like Stiva, covered it up, apologized and carried on, society would not have lifted a finger against her. Her crime is not doing it. It is refusing to pretend.

Meanwhile, in the country, Levin is dealing with rejection by mowing — not symbolically, but out in the meadow with a gang of peasants from dawn to dusk, an entire day of it. At first he can't keep up, his arms ache, he is ashamed. Then at some point the scythe begins to swing itself, he stops thinking about anything at all, his arms rise and fall and the grass goes down in rows. At the break the peasants give him black bread and a jug of water tasting of grass. Walking home he feels emptied out.Seed ④ Nothing in the plot advances, and Tolstoy spends a whole chapter on it, because he is setting something in place: the only activity in this novel that never lies is work.

Act three · Forgiveness at a bedside, and a studio in Italy

Karenin's solution is a bureaucrat's: no divorce, no scandal, appearances maintained, on condition that the lover never enters his house. What he wants is order. What Anna wants is to be alive. The two demands sit in the same house for months and neither moves.

In this stretch Anna has a dream: a small old man with a matted beard, bent over a heap of iron, hammering at it and muttering in French that it must be beaten, crushed, kneaded. She wakes terrified and says the dream means she will die in childbirth.Seed ⑤ What makes it worse is Vronsky's response — he has had exactly the same dream. It is the one moment in the book that steps outside realism, and Tolstoy gives it the plainest possible function: it predicts no details. It simply waits.

The child is born, a daughter. Anna develops puerperal fever and is dying. What follows is Karenin's great scene and the hardest thing in the novel to hold steady: half-conscious, she calls him to her, says she is wicked, begs him to forgive her — and Karenin, a man who has spent his life writing memoranda and who has to consult procedure before he can be unhappy, is struck by something he does not recognize in himself. He kneels, weeps on her arm, forgives her entirely. He is even able to shake Vronsky's hand. For that hour he is a Christian, and the only person in the book who actually manages to love his enemy.

The trouble is that once forgiveness is accepted, the forgiven party is permanently the shorter of the two. Anna lives — and finds she cannot bear her husband's magnanimity. You can fight back against hatred; being forgiven leaves you nothing to do but stoop. Vronsky collapses too: humiliated by the forgiveness, he goes home and shoots himself in the chest, and survives. The two of them climb out of their separate wreckage, find each other, and leave for Italy. Karenin had agreed to a divorce; at the last moment Anna refuses it, unwilling to accept the charity from his hand. That refusal will kill her.

The Italian passage is beautifully judged: they are free, and then they are bored. Vronsky takes up painting and invites a real painter, Mikhailov, to look, and Mikhailov sees at a glance that what he is looking at is educated imitation. What a person does with the time left over after getting everything they wanted is this book's cruellest question about running away successfully.

On the other track, Levin and Kitty meet again at a dinner at Stiva's. The famous scene: the two of them write the first letters of words in chalk on the card table and finish an entire conversation across a room full of guests, incomprehensible to everyone but perfectly clear to them. When Tolstoy writes love at his best, he never writes the word. They marry. Levin nearly misses his own wedding because no one can find him a clean shirt.

Act four · Home again: a birthday, and one night at the opera

Back in Russia, society goes to work on her — and the way it works is by not doing anything.

First the son. On Seryozha's birthday Anna slips into Karenin's house at dawn, bribes the porter, climbs the staircase she climbed for eight years, and lets herself into the nursery. The boy is asleep, flushed. He wakes, asks nothing, throws his arms round her neck and cries. A few minutes later the manservant appears and she has to go. The toy she brought is still in her bag. (That scene is worth taking apart on its own; see the next section.)

Then the world. That same evening she insists on going to the opera — Vronsky begs her not to, and she goes precisely because she must prove she has not been expelled. She sits in her box, magnificently dressed. In the neighbouring box Madame Kartasova says something to her husband in front of the entire house, stands, and leaves, as though unwilling to breathe the same air. No one insults her, no one touches her: she is sentenced by a woman standing up. At home, Vronsky loses his temper with her for the first time, because the humiliation was his.

They retreat to his estate at Vozdvizhenskoye, and Dolly comes to visit — one of the finest pieces of social observation in the book. Dolly envies Anna's freedom the whole way there, arrives to find English luxury, a French steward, a brand-new hospital, every single thing correct, and then lies awake all night because the house feels like a performance for an audience that isn't there. In the dark Anna tells her the truth: she will not have more children, she knows how to prevent it. And Dolly — who has borne seven, buried some, been betrayed and forgiven her husband — feels the floor go out from under her. The freedom Anna won was paid for by handing over the future along with it.

Vronsky acquires his own occupations: local elections, agriculture, public affairs. He hasn't stopped loving her; he simply needs to leave the house — and Anna has no house to leave. Her world contracts to one person, so she begins interrogating, testing, reading his telegrams, and taking morphine to sleep. In fairness one thing must be said here: Tolstoy does not write a woman driven silly by love, he writes a person stripped of every other role — barred from being a mother, barred from being a wife, barred from every drawing room in the city, until the only identity left is the one who is loved. Of course she checks around the clock whether it is still there.

Act five · A candle goes out

In Part Seven the two tracks touch once, and only once. Levin is in Moscow while Kitty waits to give birth, and Stiva drags him off to meet Anna. He goes with his mind made up against her, sits with her for an evening, and is completely won over by her intelligence, her directness and the pity she cannot quite hide; at home he defends her and Kitty picks a fight about it. Tolstoy has the most honest man in the book demonstrate the point on the reader's behalf: nobody who actually meets her can go on treating her as a cautionary tale.

Kitty's child is born, a son. Levin, outside the door, hears his wife screaming and believes he is losing his mind; when the baby is brought out his first reaction is not joy but terror — a new, naked, entirely losable thing that is now his to keep alive. Tolstoy insists on this, because he refuses to hand out cheap wholeness.

Anna's last days accelerate downward. She and Vronsky quarrel over something small, he leaves for his mother's, she wires him and the reply is cool. She takes a carriage to Dolly's, and on the way her consciousness starts leaking: shop signs, a man selling kvass, two laughing girls, a beggar, a fat woman, all pouring in and all instantly translated into the same sentence — everyone hates everyone, everyone is pretending. She decides that what is between her and Vronsky is nothing better, and boards a train to go to him.

At the little station of Obiralovka she gets out. A goods train is coming in. Standing at the platform edge, she suddenly remembers the man crushed under the wheels on the day she first met Vronsky.Payoff ① ↑ This is not a tidy coincidence: Tolstoy showed her the manner of her own death in chapter one, and eight hundred pages later she recognizes it herself — the "bad omen" of her first appearance turns out to have been a ticket with the date already printed on it. She wants to punish Vronsky, to make him sorry. She picks her moment between the wheels of the second car, kneels, and goes under. In the last instant she thinks: where am I, what am I doing, why — and tries to rise, and cannot. She crosses herself. The little old man is there too, hammering and muttering in French, working away at the iron over her.Payoff ⑤ ↑ The dream never was a prophecy about childbirth; it was simply waiting here. Tolstoy's one use of the supernatural is cashed with extraordinary coldness — this is not fate speaking, it is a mind, in its last second, remembering everything it never digested.

Then he puts out the candle. He compares her life to a book read by a small flame, a book full of anxiety and deception and grief and cruelty; the flame flares up brighter than it has ever burned, lights up everything that had been dark, sputters, dims, and goes out for good.Payoff ② ↑ That is the lamp from the night train in act one — she couldn't get through the English novel because she didn't want to read about other people living. The book she has now finished is her own. Recognizing "her life" and "the book she couldn't read that night" as the same object is the finest piece of craft in the novel.

The book does not end there; there is a whole further part. Vronsky waits at a station with a raging toothache, his face collapsed, barely able to finish a sentence. He is taking a squadron he has paid for himself to Serbia to fight the Turks, and his mother tells an acquaintance that it is just as well — better he go and get killed than lose his mind at home. This is Tolstoy's sentence on him: not repentance, but a death that will mean nothing, which he knows in advance. And what is really settled in him is the mare.Payoff ③ ↑ At the steeplechase he was not brutal and he did not stop loving Frou-Frou; he was simply a beat behind at the one moment that mattered, and her back broke. He did the same thing to Anna — no betrayal, no malice, just an absence at the exact moments she needed him with her. What kills her is not faithlessness; it is one slightly mistimed movement, repeated for three years. Incidentally, that Serbian volunteer fever, which most of Russia was cheering as a holy war, Tolstoy writes cold and sour — which is exactly why his editor refused Part Eight.

Last comes Levin. He has everything — wife, son, land, harvest — and nearly kills himself. He hides the rope and stops carrying a gun, because he cannot find a single reason why any of it should mean anything, given that everyone dies and he is a speck that briefly occurred in infinite space. What saves him isn't philosophy; it is a peasant's offhand remark that some men live only for their own bellies, and others don't — old Platon, for instance, who lives for his soul and remembers God. Standing in the field, Levin feels something give way.Payoff ④ ↑ The answer had been handed to him on the day he mowed with the peasants — he just thought at the time that what he'd got was relief, and Tolstoy makes him wait four hundred pages to recognize it: meaning is not reasoned into existence, it is worked into existence; not proved, but lived.

And the greatness of the book is in its last half page: Levin does not become a saint. He knows he will lose his temper at Ivan the coachman tomorrow over an unharnessed trap, will argue with people, will say the wrong thing. He decides not to tell anyone about any of it, not even Kitty. What Tolstoy hands over is not the shout of conversion, but a man privately, quietly, without intending to share it, deciding to go on.

Put the five acts together and the fuse is not in Anna at all — it is in her brother. The catastrophe begins with a man committing adultery at no cost whatsoever, and his sister being summoned to glue his marriage back together. Anna met Vronsky in the course of running that errand. Stiva walks away untouched from beginning to end: at the close of the book he is still working his connections for a better-paid post, still borrowing money, still cheerful. The same act makes a man charming and drops a woman down a well; it is not morality that punishes Anna, it is that the bill is only sent to half the population. Tolstoy never raises his voice about this. He simply files both accounts in the same book and lets them face each other.

Three slow motions

1 · The mowing: how to write "it was a good day" without going soft

The mowing chapter contains approximately zero plot: a man works all day and goes home. Why is it one of the most famous scenes in the novel?

Because it is written as body, not as mood. Tolstoy gives nothing but action and sensation: the blade needs whetting, dew soaks his trouser legs, the grass is stiff uphill and soft down, the point at which his arms stop obeying, the old peasant who works faster and steadier and doesn't sweat. The word "happiness" never appears, and the reader finishes the chapter feeling well — because every gradation of physical tiredness is there, and bodies remember real tiredness. The danger in lyric writing is doing the reader's feeling for them; Tolstoy's method is to hand over the raw materials one at a time and then shut up.

It also does structural work: the chapter sits right beside the ones in which Anna starts lying. Honesty in a working body on one side, dishonesty in a drawing-room mouth on the other. Tolstoy's contrasts are never written into the sentences. They are written into the table of contents.

2 · The last carriage ride: the ancestor of stream of consciousness

Between leaving the house and going under the train, Anna has a carriage ride and a short rail journey. In 1877 there was no precedent for how these fifteen pages are written: the narration is handed almost entirely over to her eyes and her head, and the outside world arrives in fragments — shop signs, a kvass seller, two laughing girls, a beggar, a stout woman — each one translated on arrival into the same sentence. They are all pretending. Nobody loves anybody.

Technically this runs on free indirect discourse: no "she thought," just a character's inner speech written as third-person narration, so that the reader cannot separate the narrator's judgement from hers and is forced to look out through her eyes. The device grew into stream of consciousness in the hands of Joyce and Woolf; Tolstoy uses it with terrible control — the further in you go, the shorter the sentences and the faster the jumps, so that the rhythm itself is a readout of her mental state.

Crueller still is the distribution of information. The reader knows two things she doesn't: that Vronsky has not betrayed her, only tired; and that her judgement is failing. What those pages produce is therefore not sympathy but helpless watching — you can see more clearly than she can, and there is nothing you can do.

3 · The birthday: putting the worst pain in the shortest time

Anna's secret visit to Seryozha runs only a few pages, and its force comes from time pressure: from the moment she is inside, the reader is counting seconds with her — the manservant may come, Karenin may wake. The dread isn't described. It's scheduled.

The second piece of craft is one switch of viewpoint. Tolstoy isn't satisfied with letting us watch her holding the child; he goes over into the boy's side of it. This eight-year-old has been told his mother is dead and has never believed it, and has been waiting; now that she is actually here, he doesn't dare test it, in case asking makes her stop being there. A child's half-belief weighs ten times what the mother's tears weigh.

The third is the toy that never comes out of the bag — she brought a present, never found the moment, and was hurried away, and Tolstoy adds not one word of comment. Good details never explain themselves. They just sit there and start hurting after you close the book.

The machine at the centre: two lines that never meet, and an epigraph

This is not a novel of ideas in the science-fiction sense; its "idea" is its architecture. Three things hold it up.

One · Two plots set side by side, almost never crossing

The Anna material and the Levin material each take roughly half the book, and the two protagonists meet face to face exactly once (Part Seven), in a scene that advances no plot at all. Contemporaries complained that the book had no structure. Its method of argument is juxtaposition: it will not tell you which way of living is right, it puts two ways of living under one roof and lets them light each other. Both lines work the same set of problems — what a marriage is, what a child is, what to do about death, what entitles anyone to go on. Anna's answer is to load everything onto one person; Levin's is to spread himself across land, family and labour. Anna wants to be loved; Levin wants something to do. Tolstoy's preference is obvious, but he does not cheat: he makes Anna far more magnetic than Levin.

Two · The epigraph

"Vengeance is mine; I will repay."— epigraph to the novel (Romans 12:19)

That sentence turns the whole book into an unsolved problem. It supports at least three readings, and all three hold:

  • Anna gets what is coming to her — the plainest reading, and the one that survives scrutiny worst: an author out to punish her would not have written her so well, or so persuasively.
  • Vengeance belongs to God, so human beings have no standing to judge — which makes the line a rebuke to the women rising from their opera boxes, not a verdict on Anna.
  • Retribution is a mechanism, not a sentence — Anna is destroyed not by heaven but by the compounding interest on each of her own steps, and the epigraph merely warns you off appointing yourself the enforcer.

The three readings have been fighting for a hundred and fifty years. The epigraph's function isn't to answer; it's to warn you in advance that whatever you are about to do to this woman, someone in the book has already done it.

Three · Detail used as evidence

Tolstoy almost never writes abstract psychology. He writes visible things: the ears, the chalk letters, the broken back, the toy that stays in the bag, the candle. The method hands judgement back to the reader — you are not informed that Anna can no longer stand her husband, you stand there being repelled by those ears alongside her. Psychological description makes you know something; detail infects you with it.

What it makes you think about

Question one
Should Anna have gone — when the price of being honest is your son?

Take the case for going, at full strength. Her marriage was arranged by an aunt when she was seventeen, to a man twenty years older who processes emotion as administration. It isn't that she wouldn't do her duty; she did it for eight years. What she is finally asking for is pitifully small: to be allowed to say what is true. And the only option that society offers her is to keep performing — like Stiva, like half of Petersburg: respectable by day, whatever you like by night, so long as nothing is said aloud. What Anna refuses is not really her husband; it is the arrangement whereby you may do anything provided you lie about it. Can a person give up everything in order not to lie? If not, then everything we say about authenticity is decoration for good weather.

Now the other side, at full strength, and not with anything as cheap as "she broke the moral law." The hard objection is this: she paid for her honesty with someone else's money. Seryozha is eight. He got no vote. He was told his mother was dead, and then grew up alone in that house. Anna's freedom is real and Seryozha's loss is real, and the two do not cancel. Worse, there is the daughter — the book is quite clear that Anna is cold toward the baby, and she admits it herself. A person may stake everything of their own on their own honesty. May they also stake everything belonging to two people who cannot yet speak?

None of this has aged. The vocabulary has just changed. "Being true to yourself" is the least questionable value our generation has, and it is almost never asked the obvious question: who normally receives the invoice for your authenticity? A divorce, a resignation, a move to another city, a relationship ended — each one is partly paid for by somebody who was not consulted. Tolstoy discounts neither side. He will not let you call Anna selfish, and he will not let you call her innocent. He simply leaves Seryozha standing there.

Question two
Karenin's forgiveness: a saint's hour, or the most perfect revenge available?

At the bedside, Karenin's forgiveness is clean in motive. He astonishes himself, he weeps, he shakes his rival's hand. By the standards of any religious ethics this is full marks, and for a short while it makes him a happy man.

But look at its effects. The forgiveness finishes Anna off. Hatred is an equality — two people can trade blows. Forgiveness is a gradient: the forgiven party is in debt permanently, and can never pay it down. What drives her out of the house is not her husband's coldness but his nobility. Which raises a genuinely uncomfortable question: if the practical effect of a virtue is that the other person cannot breathe, is it still a virtue?

Both sides, at strength. For the defence: the morality of an act should not be decided by the reaction it provokes; Karenin calculated nothing in that hour, and it cost him his standing (society did in fact laugh at him). Rule that "forgiveness makes people uncomfortable, therefore don't forgive," and you have handed ethics over to the beneficiary's mood. For the prosecution: Karenin very soon subcontracts his forgiveness — to Countess Lidia Ivanovna, to church language, eventually to a French clairvoyant. Once his nobility acquires an audience and an apparatus, it starts to smell. The hardest distinction isn't between good and evil; it's between real goodness and a very well-executed performance of it.

This happens daily. A parent's "I don't blame you," a partner's "it's fine, I understand," a colleague's "I covered for you this time" — some of those are forgiveness and some are the quiet opening of an account. The difference isn't audible when it's said. It shows up six months later, in whether it gets mentioned again.

Question three
Who killed Anna — society, or Anna?

The "society killed her" reading has enormous textual support. Her crime is not adultery but being known to have committed it, and the punishment is a precise social death: invitations stop, people stand up in the next box, the divorce never comes through, the child is out of reach. In the very same drawing rooms, everybody knows about Stiva, everybody finds it funny, and his career is unaffected. The book's record of the double standard is so fine-grained that reading it a hundred and fifty years later still makes you flush.

But writing her as a pure victim badly underestimates Tolstoy. He gives Anna a great deal of agency: she refuses the divorce — her own decision, and the one that kills her; she uses morphine; in the final weeks she subjects Vronsky to an interrogation she herself knows is unfair; and what she is thinking as she goes under is let him be sorry. That is not the gesture of despair. It is an act of revenge. Handing all of it over to "oppression" cancels her standing as a whole person.

Both accounts are true, and the book only works if you hold them at once: society decided which exits were locked; she decided which of the remaining ones to take. Every argument about structural constraint today jams at the same point — emphasize structure and the person disappears; emphasize choice and the oppression gets laundered. Tolstoy's solution is to refuse the trade: he keeps both ledgers in full, and never reconciles them.

Question four
Does Levin's answer count — or is faith just a voluntary end to asking?

Levin's deliverance is suspiciously convenient: an educated man who has read a cabinet of philosophy and seriously contemplated suicide is rescued by a peasant's passing remark. You can quite reasonably call this an escape — he hasn't solved the problem, he has decided to stop asking it. He isn't even fully convinced himself, and he hides it from his wife, which looks like a guilty conscience. And his revelation depends heavily on his circumstances: a landowner with land, a wife, a son and no worries about dinner finds it comparatively easy to discover that living is good.

Now the case for, at strength. Two supports under his change matter. First, what he wanted was never a proposition, it was somewhere to stand — he discovers that before he knew any answer at all, he had already been living as though one lived "for the soul": nursing his dying brother, keeping several hundred people fed, mowing that day's grass. He did not believe and then act; he found he had been acting all along. Second, and decisively: he knows explicitly that this answer cannot be proved, and he has no intention of trying. What he wants is not title to the truth, but the ability to eat dinner without carrying the thought that none of it means anything.

Which turns into a question nobody gets to dodge: is meaning a proposition that has to be demonstrated, or a condition that can only be practised? If the former, Levin is a coward, because he abandoned the demonstration. If the latter, then everyone waiting to work it out before they start living is waiting for a train that is not coming. Tolstoy leaves this on the last half page — and then has Levin snap at the coachman about a carriage, so you understand that even if it is an answer, it does not make anyone better. It only makes it possible to continue.

The obsession Tolstoy kept returning to

Line his books up and the same fixation keeps coming back: under what conditions does a person stop performing?

In War and Peace, Pierre peels the drawing-room shell off himself layer by layer, on the battlefield and in the prisoners' shed, and survives on the strength of one peasant soldier's bearing — the prototype of Levin's move. The Death of Ivan Ilyich (1886) pushes it to the limit: a judge who has lived a supremely proper life discovers on his deathbed that the whole thing was somebody else's script, and that the only person good to him is an illiterate servant. In Resurrection (1899) a nobleman recognizes, from the jury box, the woman he ruined years earlier, and dismantles his entire respectability. His protagonists never choose between good and evil. They are all struggling with the same task: tearing the false part off themselves, which hurts.

The other half is his darkening view of marriage. Here, Levin and Kitty's marriage has hope in it. By The Kreutzer Sonata (1889) the same author produced a novella that comes close to rejecting sexual love and marriage wholesale, narrated by a husband who has killed his wife and believes the institution should be abolished. Between Kitty's labour and that knife lies Tolstoy's own crisis of faith (A Confession, 1882) — a crisis already leaking into the last part of this novel: the chapters in which Levin nearly kills himself are the author's own condition at the time.

Criticism and controversy

Without flattery, several real disputes attach to this book, and the criticism has weight:

  • Whose side is the author on. One camp holds that Tolstoy punishes Anna with that epigraph and that train; the other holds that he wrote her so well, and so persuasively, that he effectively betrayed his own moral intention. Both find plenty of textual support, and a century and a half has settled nothing — a novel that can sustain two flatly opposed readings has both a strength and an ambiguity, and they are the same fact.
  • The women. Feminist criticism has long noted that the book offers women two roads — Dolly and Kitty's childbearing endurance, or Anna's destruction — and that Anna's contraception and her coldness toward her infant daughter are often read as punitive writing aimed at a woman who declines motherhood. The defence points out that no book records the double standard of that society more thoroughly, and that Stiva's total impunity is the sharpest indictment in it.
  • How much Levin there is. A great many readers find the Levin sections slow and sermonizing, especially the agricultural reform and the religious argument of Part Eight; others hold that it is precisely that line which turns an adultery novel into a book about how to live.
  • The Part Eight affair. Katkov refused to print Part Eight because of Tolstoy's coldness toward the Serbian volunteer fever then sweeping Russia, and Tolstoy had to publish it himself. It is a documented case of an author's politics directly affecting how his text reached readers.
  • The author's later repudiation. In What Is Art? (1897) Tolstoy applied a severe religious-moral standard to literature and judged a great deal of it inadequate, including his own earlier novels. Readers need not accept an author's verdict on himself, but it is worth registering: the man who wrote this book and the man who later assessed it were not the same reader.
  • Translation. English readers meet very different books depending on the hands they pass through — Constance Garnett, the Maudes, Rosemary Edmonds, Pevear and Volokhonsky, Marian Schwartz and others. Tolstoy's Russian is long-breathed, deliberately repetitive, and pointedly unpolished, and those burrs are frequently smoothed out in translation. Whether he reads well to you is sometimes not his doing, and sometimes not his fault.

Ten lines to take away

1. The opening — "All happy families are alike; each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way" (Part One, first line) — reads like an aphorism but works like a table of contents: the book is the unhappy half, taken apart.

2. Anna's crime is not the adultery, it is the confession; her brother does the same thing, lies about it, and walks away untouched.

3. The fuse of the whole catastrophe is one man's affair — and the person summoned to clean it up is his sister.

4. Her husband and her lover share a first name. Tolstoy never mentions it. He just lets you notice.

5. Frou-Frou's back is broken by one clumsy movement from a man who loved her — the first time in his life he causes something he cannot undo, and not the last.

6. Karenin's forgiveness is the one act of pure goodness in the book and the last stone on Anna's chest; there is no contradiction between those two sentences.

7. Her final thought before going under is let him be sorry — not despair, but a last act of revenge, and a last demand to be seen.

8. The candle she read her life by flaring and going out is the most economical ending in nineteenth-century fiction: one metaphor closes a life and a book at the same time.

9. Levin's answer comes from a peasant's small talk, he isn't entirely convinced by it, and he tells no one — Tolstoy never hands out the shout of conversion.

10. What you remember will not be the train. It will be the toy that never came out of the bag.
