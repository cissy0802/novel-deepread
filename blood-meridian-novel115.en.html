Novels, Deep-Read · Cormac McCarthy · No.115

Blood Meridian

Blood Meridian, or The Evening Redness in the West · Cormac McCarthy · 1985 · twenty-three chapters and an epilogue

中文 →

Rain drums on a canvas tent packed with people who have come to hear a revival preacher, and he has just reached the part about hell.
The flap opens and in walks an enormous man, taller than anyone there, without a hair on his head or face, pale as a skinned stone.
He comes to the front and tells the crowd, calmly, almost regretfully, that this preacher is an impostor, wanted for violating a little girl and for congress with a goat.
For a moment the tent is silent. Then someone draws a pistol.
That night in a bar someone asks him how he knew. He laughs and says he had never laid eyes on the man before today.

This page contains complete spoilers — how a boy who ran away at fourteen falls in with a company paid by the scalp; how they go from killing Apaches to killing anyone with black hair; who dies at the river crossing and who gets away; why the judge pursues the boy across the desert; and what happens, twenty-eight years later, in the outhouse behind a Texas saloon — which the book does not show, so neither can we. We can only tell you what it does show.

In One Line

In 1849 the government of a northern Mexican state starts buying Indian scalps by the piece, and a band of Americans takes the contract and kills its way across the desert; a nameless boy rides with them to the end. This is not a book about how the West was won. It is about the piecework slaughter underneath the winning — and about a hairless giant who insists that the slaughter is what humankind really is.

Where It Sits

Cormac McCarthy (1933–2023) grew up in Knoxville, Tennessee, and his first four novels are about Tennessee's poor, its criminals and its outcasts. Around 1976 he moved to El Paso, learned Spanish, drove and walked the routes this book follows, and with a MacArthur Fellowship awarded in 1981 wrote his fifth novel and first Western.

Its skeleton is history. The Glanton gang was real: in 1849–50 they hunted scalps for the authorities in Chihuahua and Sonora, and were wiped out by the Yuma people at a ferry crossing on the Colorado River. McCarthy's chief source was My Confession, the memoir of a former soldier named Samuel Chamberlain, which describes a learned, bald and savage "Judge Holden." In literary history it occupies the slot of the anti-Western: it borrows the whole kit of the cowboy story — horses, guns, desert, campfire — and turns every piece over to show the blood underneath. It sold poorly on publication; twenty years later it was being mentioned in the same breath as Moby-Dick and Faulkner.

The Story

Act One · See the Child

Before the first chapter there are three epigraphs: a line from Paul Valéry, a passage from the German mystic Jacob Boehme, and — strangest of all — a short item from a 1982 Arizona newspaper reporting that anthropologists re-examining a 300,000-year-old fossil skull from Ethiopia had found evidence that it had been scalped. Setup ① Wedged between a poet and a mystic, the clipping looks like a stray bit of archaeological trivia.

The first sentence is a command: "See the child." (opening of chapter one). He is born in Tennessee in 1833, the year the stars fell in a great meteor shower. His mother dies giving birth; his father is a drunken former schoolmaster who quotes poets. The child is thin and pale and ragged; he can neither read nor write, Setup ② and the narrator tells us that already there broods in him a taste for mindless violence (paraphrased). At fourteen he runs away.

He drifts through Memphis and the New Orleans docks, fights, is shot in the back. Eventually he crosses into Texas, and in the rain at Nacogdoches he walks into that revival tent and sees the judge for the first time. In the mud outside he fights a man named Toadvine, who has no ears and whose forehead is branded with letters, a criminal's mark. They finish the fight as companions, and that night they burn down a hotel together.

From beginning to end the boy has no name. He is "the kid," and only in the last chapters does he become "the man."

Act Two · Captain White and the Costumed Horsemen

The Mexican War is over, and some Americans feel it ended too soon. A Captain White is recruiting an irregular company to march into Mexico and take Sonora for himself. He buys the kid a meal, explains that the Mexicans are a people unfit to govern the land they hold, and has him outfitted.

The company marches into the desert. Water runs short; the mules drop one by one. Then a vast war party of Comanches appears on the horizon and comes at them at a gallop, dressed in whatever they have stripped from the dead: one in a bridal veil, one in a stovepipe hat, one in the armor of a Spanish conquistador, others painted and naked. This cavalry from some nightmare masquerade slaughters nearly all of White's men.

The kid escapes with a wounded man named Sproule, and they stumble through the wilderness; at night a vampire bat settles on Sproule's neck to feed. The kid is eventually taken by Mexican soldiers and marched to Chihuahua City. There he sees Captain White again — just his head, floating in a glass jar of mescal. In prison he finds Toadvine, and by day they are chained and put to work in the streets.

Act Three · A Contract and a Company

A troop of mounted Americans rides into town, led by John Joel Glanton, a dark, close-mouthed man. They have signed a contract with the governor: they will clear Chihuahua of Apaches, and for every scalp they bring in they will be paid a bounty, with a special price on the head of the Apache chief Gómez. The company is short of men; Toadvine knows some of them, and he and the kid are let out of prison to join.

The company is as motley as anyone it kills: Tobin, an ex-priest who left before taking his vows; two men both named John Jackson, one black and one white, who loathe each other; a party of Delaware scouts; David Brown; and the judge, who is here too. A few days out, the white Jackson draws his pistol and orders the black Jackson away from the fire — this fire, he says, is not for him. The black Jackson walks off, comes back a little later, and takes the white Jackson's head off with one stroke of a knife. No one stands up. Glanton glances over and tells someone to drag the body away.

The judge is the most conspicuous of them. He is close to seven feet tall and hairless all over; he speaks a handful of languages, dances, and lectures the camp on geology, law and fossils. He carries a leather-bound ledger, and whenever he meets something new — a leaf, a bird, a fragment of old Spanish armor, a painting on a rock wall — he sketches it carefully and notes its dimensions. Once, having copied a set of rock paintings, he takes up a stone and scrapes one of them off the wall. Asked what all the recording is for, he says that whatever in creation exists without his knowledge exists without his consent (paraphrased). He means to write everything down, and what he has written down belongs to him.

Act Four · The Judge's Gunpowder and the Camp by the Water

One night Tobin tells the kid how Glanton's men first met the judge. Years before, a great band of Apaches was chasing them; they were out of powder and certain to die in the desert. Crossing a barren waste, they came upon a man sitting alone on a boulder, as if he had been waiting for them. It was the judge. He led them up a mountain, scraped bat guano from a cave to get niter, burned wood for charcoal, and climbed to the crater of a dead volcano for sulfur. Short of water to bind it, he had every man piss on the pile while he stirred it with his bare arms into a great black paste. When the Apaches caught up, they ambushed them with it and killed a great many. Tobin finishes by saying he still does not know why the judge was sitting on that rock.

The company finds a large camp of Gileño Apaches by the water. They ride in at dawn. The book describes the massacre in roughly the register it uses for a storm: women, old men, children, no distinction; the scalping knives rise and fall. When they return to Chihuahua City, the bells are rung for them, the governor gives a banquet, and the ball goes on into the night. Glanton's men drink and dance in the blazing hall.

Act Five · Hair Has No Nation

The money goes fast, and they ride out again. Apaches grow harder to find, and the bounty pays only for scalps — and a black-haired scalp cannot tell you whether it came from an Apache, a Mexican farmer, or a tribe that never harmed anyone. First they wipe out a village of Tiguas, known to be peaceful. Then they start killing Mexicans, drinking and brawling and murdering in Mexican towns. Chihuahua turns around and puts a price on Glanton's own head, and a Mexican army takes up the pursuit.

The company halts before a bush whose branches are hung with dead infants. Nobody speaks. In this book violence has no side: Comanches, Apaches, Mexican soldiers and Americans all do the same things.

In one town the judge buys two puppies from a Mexican boy and drops them in the river. Another time, he picks up a surviving Apache child on the trail and dandles him on his knee all afternoon; the next morning the child is found scalped at the edge of camp. No one asks who did it.

One night at the fire someone says war is a foolish thing men made up. The judge shakes his head. It makes no difference what men think of war, he says; war endures; before man was, war waited for him (paraphrased). War is the ultimate game, because the stakes are everything: the lives of the players, and who the world belongs to. His conclusion takes three words: "War is god." (paraphrased) Payoff ① ↑ Only now do we see that the clipping on the epigraph page was never trivia. McCarthy put that scalped 300,000-year-old skull at the very front of the book as exhibit A for this speech — you may disagree with the judge, but first you have to answer the bone. The beauty of the move is that it lets a madman's sermon stand, from page one, on an archaeological foundation.

Act Six · The Kid's One Soft Spot

Over this year the kid does everything the company does, and the book never excuses him. But there are a few details the narrator sets down without comment.

Once, David Brown takes an arrow in the thigh, the head lodged in the flesh. No one else will touch it; the kid takes a knife and gets it out. Tobin, watching, calls him a fool — God will not look after the likes of him forever, is the gist. Setup ③ Another time, with Mexican soldiers on their trail, a man named Shelby has a shattered hip and cannot ride. Glanton has the kid stay behind, meaning him to finish the wounded man and catch up. The kid stays, does not do it, leaves Shelby under a tree, and rides on.

Tobin tells the kid privately that the judge has his eye on him.

Act Seven · The Crossing

In early 1850 the company reaches the Yuma crossing of the Colorado River. The gold rush has begun; thousands need to get over to California, and the ferry belongs to a Dr. Lincoln. Glanton seizes the crossing — first charging fares, then robbing the passengers, then murdering them. He has had an understanding with the local Yumas and turns on them. He makes a trip to San Diego and comes back with liquor and supplies, and the camp is drunk for days. At the crossing the judge takes charge of an idiot whose brother has been carting him to California in a cage and exhibiting him; the judge bathes him and keeps him at his side from then on.

One night the Yumas come. Glanton is too drunk to rise, and an axe splits his head. Dr. Lincoln, Glanton's dog and most of the men die there. The kid, an arrow in his leg, escapes into the desert with Tobin; Toadvine gets away too.

Act Eight · Judgment in the Desert

The judge is not dead. With the idiot in tow, he finds them in the desert. The kid has a pistol and keeps hidden; Tobin whispers to him over and over: shoot, now, kill him. The kid does not shoot.

The judge stands in the open and addresses their hiding place as if passing sentence in court. Every man in the company, he says, gave himself wholly to the work — except the kid, who alone reserved in his soul some corner of clemency for the heathen (paraphrased); and that corner, he says, makes the kid a traitor to the enterprise. Payoff ③ ↑ Only now does the reader think back to the arrow pulled from Brown's leg and Shelby left alive under the tree — set down by the narrator without a word of comment, like passing trivia. The judge had entered each one in his ledger. And here is the cruelest turn: the only thing in the whole book that could be called goodness is named aloud for the first time at a trial, and read out as the charge. Tobin's warning comes due too: it is not God who has been watching the kid, but the judge.

They shake the judge off at last. The kid reaches San Diego and is thrown into jail; the judge comes to see him there and talks to him through the bars. Later, Toadvine and David Brown are hanged in Los Angeles.

Act Nine · A Book He Cannot Read, a Woman in a Niche

The kid survives and drifts through the West for years, working whatever jobs there are. He carries a Bible the whole time. Payoff ② ↑ The line from chapter one — he can neither read nor write — lands only here: he carries across half a continent a book he cannot read a word of. The judge reads everything and records everything; nothing counts until it is in his ledger. The one book the kid owns stays forever closed to him. From the first chapter the two men stand at opposite ends of the written word.

One year, in a canyon, he comes upon a party of penitent pilgrims, all of them murdered. In a small niche in the rock kneels an old woman in a faded shawl. The kid goes to her and talks — more than he has talked in his whole life. He tells her he is an American, that he can take her somewhere safe, that she cannot stay out here alone. He reaches out and touches her arm. She weighs almost nothing, a dry husk; she has been dead for years.

Act Ten · Fort Griffin, 1878

The kid is forty-five now, and the book calls him "the man." On the Texas plains the buffalo are gone; only the bone-pickers are left, hauling wagonloads of bleached skeletons to sell to the factories. A group of young bone-pickers around a fire ask him where he's from, and one boy, Elrod, keeps goading him. That night Elrod creeps back to kill him, and the man shoots him dead. Elrod is barely more than a boy, about the age the kid was when he ran away.

He walks into a crowded saloon at Fort Griffin. A bear in a crinoline is dancing for the crowd, and a drunk pulls a pistol and shoots it. The judge is there. Twenty-eight years have passed, and he has not changed at all.

The judge talks to him about the dance. Only the man who has given himself entirely to the blood of war, he says, is fit to dance it; everyone else is a false dancer (paraphrased). The man says: you ain't nothing. The judge says: you speak truer than you know.

The man goes with a woman, then out to the jakes behind the saloon. He opens the door and the judge is inside, naked. Smiling, the judge gathers him into his arms and drops the latch.

Later two men come out to use the jakes; one opens the door, looks in, says "Good God almighty," and backs away. The book never tells us what they saw.

Inside the saloon, the fiddlers saw away, boots pound the boards, and towering over everyone the judge is dancing naked, light on his small feet, huge and pale and hairless, like an enormous infant. "He says that he will never die." (end of chapter twenty-three)

The epilogue is a single page. At dawn on the plain a man moves along with some tool, digging a hole, then another, in a line, and with each blow striking from the rock the fire God has put there. Behind him come wanderers searching for bones. Most readers take him for a man setting fence posts: the open range is about to be carved into fenced squares.

The Fuse

The fuse is not the judge, and not Glanton. It is the contract: a government decides to pay by the scalp. A scalp is a commodity that cannot show where it came from, and the moment it becomes the unit of payment, everything after is arithmetic. Apaches are hard to find, so kill whoever is easy to find; Mexicans have black hair too, so kill Mexicans; when the state can no longer pay and puts a price on you instead, go and kill travelers at a ferry. The kid, Toadvine, Brown — put any fourteen-year-old runaway in their place and the outcome is much the same. What makes the judge terrifying is not what he does — the others do it all too — but that he is the only one who believes the business is the truth.

Three Slow-Motion Scenes

I · The Costumed Horsemen

For the Comanche charge McCarthy writes a sentence so long it barely lets you breathe: "and" after "and," bridal veil, stovepipe hat, armor, umbrella, painted horses, piled one on another with no pause and no hierarchy. The rhythm is the charge itself: the reader is swept forward by the sentence, and before the first rider comes into focus the twentieth is already on top of you. The costumes are the key. They were stripped from people who had been robbed, so each is evidence of an earlier massacre; an army comes at you wearing the relics of a whole colonial history, absurd and horrifying in the same frame, and you don't know whether to laugh or run. McCarthy explains nothing and enters no soldier's head. He gives you only the picture.

II · Tobin and the Gunpowder

This is a story inside the story: the narrator steps back and lets an ex-priest talk at a campfire. Change the teller, and the judge stops being a man and becomes a legend. Every step Tobin describes is sound chemistry — niter, charcoal, sulfur, and urine with its nitrogen compounds — yet the whole thing plays like a black mass: a ring of doomed men around a giant, pissing into a heap while he stirs it barehanded into a black paste that kills. Best of all is the boulder at the start, a man sitting in the middle of nowhere with no road in any direction, as if waiting for them. Tobin has no answer and neither do we. The more solid the chemistry, the less explicable the rock.

III · The Door of the Jakes

The book has given us hundreds of corpses, every wound described precisely — and at the last and most important death, the camera stops outside the door. We get a naked giant, an embrace, a latch, and then a stranger's "Good God almighty." A book that has never once looked away looks away here, and the looking away is worse than everything it showed. Then we cut into the saloon, where the judge is dancing, and the sentences turn light and circular, repeating that he dances, that he never sleeps, that he will never die. Twenty-odd chapters of weight come to rest on one door, and a dance flings them off.

The Core Device: A Narrator Who Enters No One's Head

The book's most fundamental choice is its refusal of psychology. The narrator almost never tells you what the kid thinks, fears or regrets. He writes only what can be seen: sky, horses, wounds, stones. One passage on the desert light says that in it rock, tree and man have no precedence over one another, and McCarthy's name for this is "optical democracy." That is the book's whole ethical posture: a man's death and a rock cracking in the sun are written in the same kind of sentence. No good guy is offered to stand behind; the reader has to judge alone whether those few moments of softness count.

The second device is a collision of two languages. The narration has the taste of the King James Bible, Melville and Faulkner — archaic, vast, chained together with "and," without quotation marks. The characters, when they open their mouths, are terse and rough, and their Spanish goes untranslated. Above, Genesis; below, illiterate men killing. Each chapter even opens with an old-fashioned summary heading, like the table of contents of a chronicle, listing the coming slaughter in a calm voice.

The third is the judge himself. He is the only person with a complete theory: war is god; whatever exists without his consent is an affront; moral law is an invention of mankind to strip the strong of their power in favor of the weak (paraphrased). He is learned and charming, never sleeps and never ages, and at the end declares he will never die. Read him as a man, and he is a serial killer with a historical original; read him as a symbol, and critics have called him war itself, or the evil demiurge of Gnostic myth who rules the material world. The historical Holden appears only in a memoir of doubtful reliability; "never sleeps" and "never dies" are the novel's needs, not facts. McCarthy keeps both readings standing on purpose: you can never be sure whether he is only a monster or a law of nature.

What It Makes You Think About

One · The state pays by the scalp, and hair cannot say whose it was. When the wrong people die, is the guilt with the man holding the knife, or with the one who set the price?

The case for the one who set the price: the government of Chihuahua knew perfectly well a scalp could not be traced, and made it the unit of payment anyway; it wanted results and closed its eyes to method. And who were Glanton's men? Fugitives, illiterates, leftover soldiers, fourteen-year-old runaways. A system that recruits the poorest and hardest men available and offers them a price that counts only quantity has already written the script of the massacre. Swap in a different set of men and the ending is the same — the book itself is the proof. Punish only the hand with the knife, and the author of the script stays behind the curtain forever.

The case for the man with the knife: the contract said Apaches. No clause told anyone to wipe out a Tigua village or scalp Mexican farmers; every one of those men chose it and did it with his own hands. In the very same company, the kid at least spared Shelby — proof that "the situation made me" is never the whole story; someone can always stop. If a big enough incentive excused the act, no one would ever be responsible for anything.

McCarthy takes no side; he just sets the two facts next to each other: the contract was real, and every killing was done by somebody's hands. Bring it home: a company that looks only at the numbers, and employees who start faking figures and fleecing customers; piecework pay, and corners getting cut. You can blame the person who looks only at the numbers, but the numbers won't sign your name for you.

Two · You spend a whole year doing terrible things with a crew, and hold back on two or three small occasions. Do those occasions count?

The case that they don't: the kid pulled an arrow, spared one wounded man — and then? He kept riding with them, kept taking his share, rode into the camp by the water with the rest. His softness saved no Apache child and stopped no massacre, and when he had his chance in the desert to kill the judge, he didn't fire. Mercy that never becomes action is a comfort for oneself, not anything given to the victims. To use it to set him apart is like someone who took part in everything saying "but I didn't want to in my heart." Who doesn't say that?

The case that they do: the judge knows better than anyone that they count — otherwise he would not hunt the kid across a desert or wait for him in a saloon twenty-eight years later. In a system where everyone has handed himself over, one man who has not handed himself over entirely is a flaw, proof that the system is not a law of nature but something that can be refused. For "war is god" to hold, there can be no exceptions. The kid's corner of clemency saved no one, but it leaves the judge's theory incomplete.

The author seems to sit at the coldest point between the two: he lets the mercy exist, be noticed, be convicted, and then be shut behind a door. Bring it home: you stay in an organization you don't believe in, now and then put in a word for someone or do a little less — is that conscience, or the painkiller that lets you keep staying? Perhaps it depends on whether that corner ever grows into the step that takes you out.

Three · If people were scalping each other 300,000 years ago, is civilization reshaping human nature — or just a coat of paint that can be stripped at any moment?

The case for the judge — he is the best talker in the book, so it isn't hard: the skull is real; every people in the novel does the same things, Comanche, Apache, Mexican soldier, American; Chihuahua City rings its bells and throws a ball for the butchers. Law, church and the governor's banquet did not stop the violence; they issued it an invoice. In this book civilization is never the opposite of violence but its employer. To say human nature is gentle and circumstances corrupt it is exactly the kind of comfort the judge would laugh at.

The case against: even if violence is ancient, that doesn't make it everything. The skull proves people could scalp; it doesn't prove scalping was all they could do — burial, care for the sick and wounded, sharing food are just as old. The old woman in her niche and the boy who pulled an arrow are part of human nature too. "This is how people are" is a description; the judge quietly turns it into a command: since it is so, so it should be. That leap is the real weak point in his theory.

Where does the author stand? The epilogue is the only clue, and it reads both ways: the fire struck from the rock might be order, law, a new beginning — or the man might simply be stringing the barbed wire that turns open country into private property. Bring it home: whenever a disaster or a riot shows us the other face of our neighbors, someone says "now you see what people really are." That may be true — but is it the whole face, or just the side we fear most?

Through-Line: A Writer Who Spent His Life on Violence

McCarthy keeps returning to one question: in a place where no God intervenes and no law arrives, what is left in a person? The early Child of God follows a mountain outcast who ends up a murderer and necrophile, yet the narrator calls him a child of God much like yourself perhaps — again refusing both to defend and to condemn.

After Blood Meridian his West grows lonelier. The young men of the Border Trilogy (All the Pretty Horses and its sequels) still believe in horses and promises and find that their world is already gone. In No Country for Old Men the killer Anton Chigurh decides people's fates with the toss of a coin, almost another incarnation of the judge: a man who treats chance as law and himself as fate's executor. That book ends with the old sheriff dreaming of his father riding ahead into the dark carrying fire in a horn; in The Road the father tells his son they are "carrying the fire." Look back at the epilogue of Blood Meridian — a man striking fire from rock — and McCarthy's later books seem to be asking whose fire that is, and whether it can still be passed on.

Controversies and Criticism

Indictment or consumption? The most common charge: so many pages, so many gorgeous sentences, spent on scalping and dead infants, until the reader is trained to admire slaughter as scenery. Harold Bloom later championed the book fiercely, but admitted that his first two attempts at it failed because the violence drove him off. Those who say it aestheticizes violence and those who say it forces us to look at violence are pointing to the same passages.

The Indians are only outlines. The Comanches, Apaches and Yumas are nearly all nameless, interior-less masses that come and go like weather; some critics see this as repeating the old Western clichés, just with the Americans painted black too. Defenders answer that the white characters have no interiority either — the kid doesn't even have a name — and that this evenhandedness is the method. Both have a point, but the asymmetry remains: the former were real peoples.

Women barely exist. The women in the book are victims, whores, a dead mother and a corpse in a niche. Whether that is the truth of that world or the author's blind spot is still argued.

Style and sources. Some critics find McCarthy's prose inflated and portentous; B. R. Myers made him one of the chief targets of "A Reader's Manifesto" in The Atlantic in 2001. As for sources, Chamberlain's memoir is itself a mix of fact and invention, and the judge grew from the seed of a dubious account; he is not to be read as history.

The author himself. In 2024 Vanity Fair published a long report that McCarthy, in his forties, began a long relationship with a sixteen-year-old girl, Augusta Britt, during years that overlap with the writing of this novel. McCarthy had died before it appeared and could not respond. It is a fact about the author; readers can decide for themselves how it bears on the work.

Crossovers

  • Economics · Goodhart's law: In 1975 Charles Goodhart observed that an indicator stops being reliable once it is made a target. A scalp was meant as evidence of Apaches killed; once it became the unit of payment, men began producing the evidence itself. The difference: Goodhart described an indicator decaying over time, whereas here everyone knew from the start that it couldn't tell true from false, and nobody cared.
  • Psychology · Bandura's moral disengagement: Albert Bandura described how people license cruelty — shifting responsibility to a superior or a contract, dehumanizing victims, renaming the act. Glanton's men use all three; the judge needs none of them.
  • Archaeology · Keeley's War Before Civilization: Lawrence Keeley's 1996 book argued that prehistoric societies had far higher rates of war death than modern states, the same direction as the epigraph's skull. The difference: his is a contested empirical estimate; the judge makes it theology.
  • Philosophy · Heraclitus: A surviving fragment calls war the father of all and king of all. The judge's "war is god" is nearly the same sentence; but Heraclitus's war is usually read as the tension between opposites, the judge's as literal killing.
