Novels, Deep-Read · Jin Yucheng · No.91

Blossoms

Jin Yucheng · 2012 · Prologue, thirty-one chapters, coda

中文 →

The Jing'an Temple food market. Hairy crabs lie trussed in straw in bamboo baskets, one on top of another, blowing tiny bubbles.
Tao Tao, who sells them, spots Husheng passing and hauls him behind the stall for tea. Voice lowered, he starts in on the things he sees and hears when he delivers crabs into people's homes: who is sleeping with whom, and the pair he caught in broad daylight just the other day. He is enjoying himself enormously.
Husheng holds his teacup and says nothing. Tao Tao finishes one story and starts another.
Husheng says he has somewhere to be, and leaves.

This piece contains complete spoilers: where Beidi went, why Xiao Mao cut off his two best friends for twenty years, what was written in Xiaoqin's notebook, who becomes a nun, who dies, and whose voice is on the phone at the very end. The novel has no plot suspense; its force lies in a thirty-year drop, and you cannot describe the drop without Xiao Mao's sickbed and that last phone call.

In one sentence

Three Shanghai boys from wildly different backgrounds (A Bao, grandson of a capitalist; Husheng, son of air-force officers; Xiao Mao, from a factory family) become friends in the 1960s, are torn apart in the 1970s, and by the 1990s are each seated at one banquet table after another. It is a novel built out of silence: in the loudest thirty years a city ever had, everyone talks, and nobody says the thing that matters.

Coordinates

Jin Yucheng was born in Shanghai in 1952 to a father from Suzhou. The Cultural Revolution caught him as a teenager, and he spent years as a sent-down youth on a farm in Heilongjiang. Back in the city he became a literary editor, working for decades at the magazine Shanghai Literature, and wrote a modest amount of short fiction that few people noticed. In May 2011, at nearly sixty, he began posting installments on a local Shanghai web forum under the handle "Alone Up to the Attic": a stretch each day, about old Shanghai people, told in the cadence of Shanghainese. He had a first draft within six months. It appeared in the literary journal Harvest in 2012, came out as a book in 2013, and won the Mao Dun Literature Prize, China's top award for the novel, in 2015.

Its place in literary history is easy to state: it is the first major novel in decades to think in Shanghainese and still be readable across the whole country. Plenty of writers have described Shanghai; this book lets the city do the talking. The 2023 television series directed by Wong Kar-wai shares the title, but it is close to a different story, with no Husheng, no Xiao Mao and none of the 1960s half. Everything below is about the novel.

The story

The novel runs on two alternating tracks: odd-numbered chapters follow the 1960s and 70s, starting when A Bao is about ten; even-numbered chapters follow the 1990s, when all three men are middle-aged. To keep cause and effect clear, I will tell the earlier track first, then the later, and bring them together at Xiao Mao's sickbed, which is also where the book joins them.

Act one · A rooftop on Sinan Road: A Bao, Beidi and a piano

Early 1960s. A Bao is ten and lives in a villa on Sinan Road, in the old French Concession: three storeys if you count the attic. From the roof he can see over half the district. His grandfather was a capitalist in old Shanghai, and his father had been an underground Communist before 1949. In those years that was an uneasy combination: the family still had the house, but no longer the confidence. A Bao collects stamps and likes to sit on the roof, doing nothing.

Next door lives a little girl called Beidi who plays the piano. She is being raised by an old woman from Shaoxing whom everyone calls Granny, a slow talker with an endless supply of old stories. Beidi, Granny and A Bao spend a lot of time together: one plays, one tells tales, one listens. Once, chatting about what people turn into when they die, Granny says she would rather become a fish and swim off, and be done with it. Beidi chimes in: then she will be a goldfish. Set-up ① It is an idle joke between a child and an old woman; nobody reading it stops to look.

Husheng comes from somewhere else. Both his parents are air-force cadres, and the family lives in an apartment block off Maoming Road, more secure than A Bao's household and more politically "correct". Xiao Mao lives in the lanes around Dazimingzhong, the "Big Clock" district in the industrial west of the city. His parents are workers, the house is crowded, several families share one staircase, and everyone can hear everyone else cough. Xiao Mao reads old books, trains with a boxing master, is solidly built and loyal. The three boys come from the top, the middle and the bottom of Shanghai, and in a few years when class had not yet fully torn the city apart, they became friends. They roam the streets together, swap books, talk; they also get to know Shuhua, a girl who reads poetry and has read a great deal of everything. Husheng is quietly in love with her.

Act two · 1966: the roof caves in

Then 1966 arrives. A Bao's home is raided and the family is "swept out of the door", the phrase of the time, and moved to Caoyang New Village, a 1950s estate built for factory workers: rows of plain blocks, several families to one kitchen. In a single summer A Bao drops from a Sinan Road rooftop into a shared workers' kitchen, from one world into another. He later ends up working in a neighbourhood workshop.

Husheng's family does not escape either. His parents are pulled into a political case and fall from the "correct" side. One day he and Shuhua watch a man throw himself under a vehicle in the street. The two teenagers stand at the edge of the crowd, unable to say a word. Shuhua has read a lot by now, and on a street like that, all those books suddenly weigh far too much and nothing at all.

Beidi's piano is carried off. Not long afterwards, Beidi and Granny are gone too: no death notice, no transfer order, just gone, never seen in the lane again. A story starts to circulate among the children. In the middle of the night, it goes, a wild cat carried a goldfish and a crucian carp in its mouth all the way to the Rihui Creek and slipped the two fish into the Huangpu River.

Payoff ① ↑ The idle joke from act one, about swimming away as a fish and Beidi being a goldfish, comes true here down to the species: one old, one young, a carp and a goldfish. Jin never tells us what actually happened to them. He hands the one loss a child cannot bear over to a fairy tale the children made up themselves. A Bao, hearing it, says this must be a story, a myth. He knows it is a story, and a story is all he can have. For years afterwards he goes from junk shop to junk shop looking for that piano, the one with a little fish marked on it, and never finds it. The novel never reveals where Beidi went. It is the cleanest silence in the book.

Shuhua is sent down to the countryside in Jilin, in the far northeast. She writes to Husheng, and then sends a letter breaking it off: she is going to marry there, and he should stop writing. It is a letter so calm it is almost cruel, a girl who has read so much poetry closing the door on her own girlhood in the plainest words she can find. Years later word comes back that after her third child, her mind gave way. The Shuhua who used to talk poetry with the boys in the street has been swallowed by the northeastern snow.

Act three · The staircase at the Big Clock: Yinfeng, the upstairs uncle and Chunxiang

In the 1970s A Bao falls in love. Xuezhi is a conductress on a city bus, and she tells him that on her bus he will never need to buy a ticket. Set-up ② They have a lovely stretch of time, in the bus, at the stops. But her family objects to his background: a capitalist's grandson, a family swept out of its house, a future that is visibly grey. A Bao writes the letter that ends it himself. He doesn't argue, doesn't plead, doesn't say anything. It is the first time we see the pattern of his whole life: when something truly hurts, he ends it with his own hand before anyone else can.

Xiao Mao gets a different kind of story. In his building lives a young wife called Yinfeng whose husband, Haide, is a merchant seaman away for months at a time. She is lonely, he is young, and one thing leads to another. The lane houses have paper-thin walls and only one staircase, and the uncle on the second floor listens hard and writes it all down: which day, what time, how long. When Haide comes home, the tally is placed in his hands.

There is no scene. Yinfeng simply goes cold and stops acknowledging Xiao Mao. Xiao Mao dies without ever learning who told; he believes, to the end, that she just stopped loving him. The reader knows. He doesn't. Jin leaves that gap open for decades and never once closes it on Xiao Mao's behalf.

After a scandal like that, Xiao Mao's mother hurries to find him a wife. The match is Chunxiang, older than him and divorced, her former husband having had some physical defect. Chunxiang is a Christian, and in her heart she does not want this marriage. Alone, she prays for an answer, and God says nothing. The novel's epigraph is her thought: "God says nothing, as if it were all up to me…" (epigraph) Set-up ③ She takes divine silence as the choice being handed back to her. So she marries him.

Before the wedding, in a barber's shop and in front of other people, Xiao Mao announces that he is getting married, and makes it clear that his friends need not keep in touch. Yinfeng is there. She says nothing; then she crouches down and starts to sob. The novel offers almost no explanation for the break. Was it shame over Yinfeng? Was it the class gap finally showing itself in adulthood, A Bao and Husheng being, after all, people from "above"? The book gives the scene, not the reasons. From that day, the three friends stop seeing each other.

Chunxiang is a good wife. She can tell there is an empty space in her husband and urges him to invite A Bao and Husheng back. He refuses. Then she dies in childbirth, and the baby with her. Before she goes, she tells Xiao Mao that a person must have friends. Set-up ④ At the time he does not do anything about it.

Act four · The banquets of Huanghe Road: the 1990s

The Shanghai of the 1990s is another city. A Bao is in business, and at dinner everyone calls him Mr. Bao; Husheng is a lawyer who often handles his friends' divorces. Around them circles a crowd: Li Li, whose restaurant Zhizhen Garden sits on Huanghe Road, a street of eateries lit all night; Lingzi, who runs a small place called Night Tokyo and has some connection to Japan, where the same old regulars always seem to be sitting; Miss Wang; Mei Rui, who works at the international trade centre, poaches Miss Wang's clients and sets her sights on A Bao; and Mr. Kang and Mr. Xu, men whose business is never quite clear but who are forever picking up the bill.

This half of the novel is very nearly one meal after another. Who invited whom, who sits where, who brought someone along, who left early, whose phone rang. Dishes arrive, toasts go round, men brag, women laugh, one topic is barely under way before another cuts in. Now and then someone tells a story from the past and the table goes quiet for a moment, then laughter rolls over it. In the 1990s people talk about everything except themselves.

Under the tablecloth, families are splitting. Miss Wang's husband, Hongqing, works for a state enterprise; they want a second child, so they arrange a sham divorce to get around the one-child rules. On a group outing to the town of Changshu, Miss Wang sleeps with Mr. Xu and comes back pregnant. Mr. Xu does not want the child and sends someone to persuade her to end it. The sham divorce hardens into a real one; there is no going back to Hongqing. Mei Rui's family is a mess too: her mother, suddenly fashionable in late middle age, has rekindled things with an old flame, a rich man's son who went to Hong Kong years ago, and plans to divorce Mei Rui's father. In an old house by Lake Tai, Mei Rui tells Mr. Kang the whole sorry business, and the more she tells, the less it sounds like someone else's story.

Tao Tao is still selling crabs: the same Tao Tao who dragged Husheng behind the stall in the opening pages and could not stop talking. His favourite subject is men and women: whose wife is seeing whom, how he stumbled on them, how he caught them in the act. Set-up ⑤ He considers himself an expert. He has a wife, Fangmei; a married woman from Hebei named Pan Jing pursues him so hard she goes straight to Fangmei and demands a divorce; then he falls for a young woman called Xiaoqin. Husheng steps in and handles the divorce from Fangmei. Tao Tao and Xiaoqin move into an old public flat on Yanqing Road, and he thinks the good life has begun.

One day the two of them are larking about on the balcony when the rusted railing gives way. Xiaoqin falls and cannot be saved. The police hand Tao Tao a notebook of hers and tell him to read it himself. He had always assumed it was her business ledger. When he opens it, it is about him: how she arranged things step by step, how she pushed him toward the divorce, how she hurried the marriage along.

Payoff ⑤ ↑ The man at the crab stall who spent the prologue tallying other people's affairs was himself an entry in someone's ledger. The first voice in the novel belongs to a man sure he has seen through men and women; in the end he is seen through by a notebook he never bothered to open. The bawdy stories of the prologue read at first like local colour, a way to set the tone; here they turn out to be a mirror held the wrong way round. And Xiaoqin's notebook rhymes, across twenty years, with the upstairs uncle's tally of Xiao Mao and Yinfeng. Two eras, and in each one somebody in the dark, writing other people's feelings down as figures.

Act five · The party breaks up

Li Li and A Bao circle each other for a long time. The proprietress of Zhizhen Garden is brilliant at working a room, but her private rooms are crammed with old dolls. One night she finally tells him her history: as a young woman she was lured away and ended up in the sex trade, and someone tattooed a bleeding rose and a few English words on her lower belly; a Mr. Zhou got her out, and she had the tattoo removed in Hong Kong. Having said all this, she goes back to her banquets, organising, laughing as before. Not long afterwards she invites an eighty-year-old Buddhist abbess, shaves her head and becomes a nun. The woman who was best at running the game in the 1990s is the one who walks out of it, more decisively than anyone.

Tests show that Miss Wang's baby is badly malformed. Everyone urges her not to go through with it, and she insists on having it. So that the child will have a legal father, after much manoeuvring she registers a marriage in name only, to Xiao Mao.

By then Xiao Mao is back among his friends. In the mid-1990s the three of them meet again; after twenty years apart, nobody mentions what was said in the barber's shop. Payoff ④ ↑ Chunxiang's dying words, that a person must have friends, went unheeded; twenty years later Xiao Mao spends his last years making good on them. In act three she begged him to bring A Bao and Husheng back and he refused; after her death the same request became the only will she left him. A woman who lives for just a few chapters ties the two halves of the novel together.

Then Xiao Mao falls ill: stomach cancer. A Bao and Husheng visit. From his bed he still jokes with them, teasing about the women in his life and laughing at himself for learning all that boxing only to have no use for it. A Bao says something afterwards to this effect: Xiao Mao wants to die and Miss Wang wants to give birth, and neither one is easy.

On his sickbed Xiao Mao says it: God says nothing, as if it were all up to me. Payoff ③ ↑ These were Chunxiang's words, her prayer: a woman who did not want to marry took God's silence for permission, and married. More than twenty years later, in her husband's mouth, the meaning flips completely: not "I can decide for myself", but "I can't decide anything, and not even God will step in." Placed at the very front of the book, the epigraph reads like a line about freedom; only at Xiao Mao's bedside do you see it is a line about loneliness. The same sentence, at one end a woman's wedding, at the other a man's death.

Xiao Mao dies. To the end he knows nothing of the upstairs uncle's tally. He asks A Bao and Husheng to look after the young French couple lodging in his flat, and they promise.

In the coda, A Bao and Husheng walk along the river, talking about Xiao Mao, about the women, about how many secrets a person can actually keep in a lifetime. A Bao's phone rings: a number he doesn't know. He answers, and hears Xuezhi's voice. The novel stops there.

Payoff ② ↑ The bus conductress from act three, who promised he'd never need a ticket, is the woman A Bao wrote out of his life with his own hand. Thirty years later her voice turns up from an unknown number, and what he says to her the novel does not tell us. The last voice in the book is the one A Bao most wanted to hear as a young man, and was the first to give up. Jin allows nobody a neat ending and lets nothing quite close: one phone call ties the 1970s to the 1990s at A Bao's ear, then cuts the line.

If you are looking for the fuse, it is not Yinfeng, not Xiaoqin, not any business deal. The fuse is the summer of 1966: the rooftop on Sinan Road caves in, the piano is carried off, an old woman and a girl turn into fish. The 1960s half of the novel is about a world being taken away; the 1990s half is about what people use to fill the hole: banquets, deals, one affair after another, endless talk. A Bao stays alone, Husheng ends one marriage after another for other people, Xiao Mao goes twenty years without his friends; each of them carries an empty space those years left behind. The upstairs uncle's tally and Xiaoqin's notebook are that fuse going off twice on a small scale: in the dark there is always someone writing it down, and in the light there is always someone saying nothing.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · The cat carrying fish: giving a disappearance to a fairy tale

Any other writer could have turned Beidi and Granny's disappearance into an indictment: the raid, the persecution, a specific death. Jin refuses. He lets the news travel among children, lets it grow into a tale of a wild cat padding through the night with two fish in its jaws, and then has A Bao himself say: it's a story, a myth.

Why does it work? Because the point of view stays fixed on a boy of about ten, and that is exactly how a child handles something unbearable. The information stops at the edge of what a child can take in; the reader, like A Bao, never learns what happened, only that a girl who played the piano is gone. That is harder to forget than any concrete horror, because the reader is forced to imagine ten thousand endings on her behalf. And with the goldfish line planted so lightly in act one, the fairy tale gains a terrible precision: she said she would turn into one, and she did.

Two · The barber's shop: a break without a single inner thought

Xiao Mao's announcement, that he is marrying and that his friends need not stay in touch, happens in a barber's shop. A few sentences; Yinfeng crouches down and weeps. No psychology, no narrator stepping in to explain why he does it, no line like "a sharp pain went through him".

This is the method of the whole novel in miniature: write only what people say and do; never go inside their heads. The old Chinese storyteller's tradition worked this way. The teller reports what you could see and leaves the rest for the listeners to weigh. Jin takes the approach as far as it will go. The effect is that readers have to assemble Xiao Mao's motive themselves. Shame? Pride? Class? Revenge on Yinfeng? Whatever they arrive at weighs more than anything the author could have supplied, because it is theirs.

Three · Xiaoqin's notebook: the hand shown only after death

Xiaoqin's fall from the balcony is told fast, in a few lines, like a news item. The real blow comes afterwards: a policeman hands over a notebook that Tao Tao took for her accounts.

It works because the information arrives only after the person is dead, and lands on the one man with no defences. With her gone, the notebook becomes a monologue nobody can ever answer; Tao Tao cannot even find someone to be angry at. Crueller still, Tao Tao entered the novel as its resident expert on adultery; his is the book's first voice. Jin lets the man who reads everyone else be read to the bone by a dead woman, adds no comment at all, and moves on to the next banquet.

The book's core devices

Blossoms is not a premise-driven novel; its devices lie in how it tells things. There are three.

The first is bu xiang. The Shanghainese phrase means not making a sound: not answering, not reacting, not taking a position. It appears more than a thousand times. Someone says a mouthful; the other person says nothing. A conversation reaches the crucial point; nothing. Someone is insulted; nothing. Someone is moved; nothing. The rule is that a character's feelings are never described, only marked by where the silences fall. The logic runs like this: no psychology, so dialogue has to carry everything; the dialogue is street chatter, so what really matters can only fall in the gaps; the gaps are the silences. What holds up is that this captures a real way of living. In a city where people live on top of each other, in an era when the wrong word could ruin you, silence is the safest and most dignified reply. Jin has said that an editor asked him why his characters had no inner life, and that he added a few lines near the end of the book version to answer, through the characters themselves. What is literary choice rather than life is the totality of it: real people do say what they feel, sometimes, and the novel cuts every such moment for the sake of its restraint.

The second is how the dialogue is set down. There are almost no quotation marks; the punctuation is mostly commas and full stops; one person speaks and another picks up, in long unbroken runs, like a whole table talking at once. Jin says this airless style arose by accident while he was posting daily on the forum, and that he kept it once he realised it resembled the old Chinese storytellers' prose. The effect is noise, especially in the 1990s chapters: you are reading not a story but a roomful of voices.

The third is the alternation. The 1960s track is childhood and loss: slow, visual, a little like a fairy tale. The 1990s track is banquets and appetite: choppy, almost all dialogue. They switch every chapter, so the reader keeps jumping between two Shanghais: one chapter a boy on a Sinan Road rooftop, the next Mr. Bao at a Huanghe Road table. The twenty years in between, from the late 1970s to the early 1990s, are nearly blank. Jin has said the alternation began simply because crossing two lines in daily posts made things "livelier", and that he was not thinking about contrast as he wrote. Yet the gap became the book's most powerful structure: you never see how those boys turned into these men; you see the start and the result, and must fill the middle yourself, the way a city never tells you where its wounds are.

A note on the language: Jin calls this a novel written with a Shanghainese mind, but "reformed": he dropped dialect characters outsiders cannot read, even the Shanghainese words for "you" and "he", and kept only the syntax and cadence. The book also carries twenty illustrations drawn by the author, many of them street maps.

What it forces you to think about

One · A dying man has misjudged someone all his life: tell him, or say nothing?

Xiao Mao dies believing Yinfeng simply stopped loving him, never knowing that the upstairs uncle kept a tally and handed it over. Suppose you were the friend who knew, sitting at his bedside. Would you tell him?

First, the strongest case for silence. What use is the truth to a dying man? Yinfeng left his life long ago; the upstairs uncle is probably old or dead; there is nobody to confront and nothing to repair. Telling him would only reopen a wound that has long since scarred, and what he would get is not relief but a fresh anger he has no time to digest: so the twenty-year break, the rushed marriage, Chunxiang's death all started with a bored old man listening at the stairs. People have a right, at the end, to leave with a version that lets them rest. Deciding that someone "has to know" is often about the teller being unable to carry the secret.

The other side is just as strong: he was deceived for a lifetime, and keeping it from him is one more failure of respect. Xiao Mao believes a woman dropped him, and that misunderstanding may have shaped everything he felt about women and love for the rest of his life; there may be some of that wound in the flippancy with which he jokes about women from his deathbed. The truth might let him see Yinfeng again: her coldness was not betrayal but coercion. Letting someone die inside a misunderstanding means deciding, on his behalf, what his life meant. And if silence becomes a habit even between friends, how real is the reconciliation of these three men at all?

Where does the author stand? The novel itself makes the silent choice: nobody tells Xiao Mao, not one word. Jin seems to accept the silence, but he lets the reader know the truth, and so its whole weight lands on the reader. Bring it home: an elderly relative still resents someone who was in fact wronged, and you know what really happened. Do you say so at their eightieth birthday?

Two · Can a cruel era be remembered as a beautiful childhood?

The 1960s half is written beautifully: the rooftop, the plane trees, the piano, the stamps, the friendship of boys. But this is the era of raids, suicides and disappearances. Is writing it so lovingly honest, or a dangerous kind of nostalgia?

First, the case that it is honest. A ten-year-old in 1966 did not see only violence. He also saw summer, a girl he liked, a friend's fists. Allowing only horror into memory, and never beauty, is another rewrite of the past: it turns everyone who lived through those years into a specimen of victimhood and erases what they truly had. Jin does not use beauty as a cover-up. Beidi vanishes, Shuhua loses her mind, Husheng's parents fall; the beautiful things are taken away one by one, and that is exactly why they are written so beautifully. You have to see how good it was to know what was lost.

The other side: nostalgia has a built-in bias toward the survivors' point of view. A Bao's fall from Sinan Road into Caoyang New Village is a tragedy; for people who lived in workers' housing all their lives, it was just Tuesday. Critics have pointed out that the book's longing for old Shanghai carries a class homesickness: the plane trees of the genteel districts turn into poetry more easily than the coal stoves of the poor ones. And for young readers who never lived through that time, such lovely memories may dilute the weight of the history itself: they remember the piano and the goldfish, and forget why the piano was carried away.

Where does the author stand? He answers ambiguously, through structure: the 1960s are beautiful and cruel, the 1990s loud and empty. He doesn't say which is better; he only shows that nothing people later used to fill the hole comes close to what was taken. Bring it home: the "best years" you look back on, were they the best years for the people around you too?

Three · Banquet after banquet: is this living, or hiding?

The characters of the 1990s do little but eat, flirt and talk about other people. No ideals, no great causes, no one discussing "meaning". Is a life like that wasted, or the only honest way left to live?

First, the strongest case for the banquet. People who lived through the 1960s and 70s saw "meaning" used as a weapon: grand slogans, correct class origins, demands that you give everything for some goal. This table of people in the 1990s can finally live for themselves: a good meal, a few friends, a love affair, some money. Noise itself is a kind of liberation, and refusing to talk about meaning is the most dignified reply to an era that talked about nothing else. And there is real feeling at the table too: Husheng handling a friend's divorce, A Bao tending Xiao Mao in his illness.

The other side: noise can also be an anaesthetic. A whole table talks all night and nobody says one true thing about themselves; Li Li tells her story and the next thing we know she has entered a convent. She could not stay at the table any longer, and only then could she speak. Miss Wang, Mei Rui, Tao Tao all make a mess of their lives, and nobody stops to ask what they are doing. If the people of a society can only fill the hollow inside with food, drink and affairs, the noise may just be the hollow's echo.

Where does the author stand? The title is one answer. Blossoms open full, and fall fast; Jin has said none of his women escape the old proverb that no flower stays red for a hundred days. He doesn't condemn the banquets; he just lets them break up, one table at a time. Bring it home: when did you last sit among a crowd of people and say something you truly meant?

Motifs: what Jin keeps writing about

Jin has not written much, but a few obsessions run through it. First, the history of ordinary people. He has said he wrote Blossoms to record what nameless people did: not heroes or celebrities, but people in the lanes, the markets, around the dinner table. Second, the crease where family meets politics. His non-fiction book Looking Back tells his parents' lives: a father who worked in the Communist underground and was persecuted after 1949. A Bao's family (a capitalist grandfather, an underground father) carries the shadow of that crease. Third, the grain of the city. The maps he draws are an attempt to give a city back its actual streets, house numbers and staircases.

Blossoms is where these strands first come together, and most completely: his father's generation's scars, his own adolescence and thirty years of a city, all handed over to silence to tell.

Controversy and criticism

Women and desire. This is where readers' criticism concentrates. The 1990s half parades one woman after another: Miss Wang, Mei Rui, Li Li, Lingzi, Xiaoqin. Some readers find them hard to tell apart, lined up as if in a man's line of sight, with sex written often and lightly. Others reply that each woman has her own fate and force: Li Li's vows and Miss Wang's insistence on giving birth are their own choices, while most of the men are passive. The text supports both readings.

No plot. Academic critics have called the structure "flat" and "networked": no central event, no protagonist's arc, a cast so large you need a list. Jin himself admits that when he was posting online, the 1960s chapters drew few replies while the 1990s banquets drew crowds.

Nostalgia. Some critics read the book's love of old Shanghai, especially its genteel districts, as "imagined nostalgia", the past patched up with present-day eyes. Others argue the opposite: it shows the emptiness beneath the glitter, and the 1990s half is not nostalgia at all but a cold stare.

The dialect barrier. The Shanghainese syntax shuts out many northern readers at first. Jin's answer was the "reformed" approach: drop obscure dialect characters, keep the cadence. Opinions differ. Some Shanghai readers find it not local enough; some readers elsewhere find it too local.

The TV confusion. The 2023 series was a huge hit, but it starts nearly from scratch, built around Mr. Bao's business battles in the 1990s, without Husheng, without Xiao Mao, without the 1960s half. Many people came to the book through the show and discovered they were two different things. That is not the novel's fault, but it has thrown many people's idea of Blossoms out of line with the original. An English edition was announced some years ago, with John Balcom as translator; how a voice this rooted in dialect will survive in English is a question still open.

Crossovers

  • Sociology · Simmel's reserve: In "The Metropolis and Mental Life" (1903), Georg Simmel argued that city dwellers, flooded with stimuli and strangers, develop a cool reserve to protect themselves. Bu xiang works like that urban armour. The difference: Simmel's reserve is indifference, while the novel's silences usually press down on the heaviest feelings.
  • Cognitive psychology · the reminiscence bump: Studies of autobiographical memory find that older people recall events from roughly ages ten to thirty more often and more vividly than any other period. The novel's 1960s track begins when A Bao is ten and is disproportionately rich in images; the 1990s half is almost all voices. The difference: the bump is a natural bias of recall, whereas the novel switches between the two periods chapter by chapter, forcing you to face the drop.
  • Sociology · Goffman's front and back stage: In The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (1959), Erving Goffman described social life as theatre: performance out front, make-up off backstage. The Huanghe Road banquets are the city's biggest front stage; Li Li's doll-filled rooms and Xiaoqin's notebook are the backstage. The difference: Goffman's backstage is where you relax; in the novel it tends to be lethal.
  • Buddhism · impermanence: Buddhist teaching holds that everything formed of conditions arises and passes away; nothing stays. The title and its proverb, that no flower stays red for a hundred days, have the same shape: the fuller the bloom, the nearer the fall. The difference: Buddhism moves from impermanence to letting go of attachment; in the novel only Li Li takes that road, and everyone else goes back to the table.
