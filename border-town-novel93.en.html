Novels, Deep-Read · Shen Congwen · No.93

Border Town

Shen Congwen · 1934 · Twenty-one chapters

中文 →

West Hunan, where the province runs up against Sichuan: a small hill town called Chadong. Outside the walls a creek runs into the river, and beside the creek stands a white pagoda. Below the pagoda lives a household of three: a ferryman of seventy, a girl, and a yellow dog.
When someone needs to cross, the old man hauls on a cable strung from bank to bank and draws the square-nosed boat over hand by hand. Passengers try to press coins on him. He refuses. When he can't refuse, he goes into town, spends the money on tea and tobacco, and hangs them in the boat for the next people who cross.
The girl sits on a rock by the water and watches the dog leap in and clamber out dripping. Her name is Cuicui. There is not a single villain in this story, and by the end she is the only one of the household left.

This piece contains complete spoilers: whom Cuicui loves, who sang for whom on the one night that mattered, which brother drowned on which rapid, the night the pagoda fell, and who is left waiting for whom. The book has almost no plot suspense. Its tragedy grows entirely in the things nobody says, and there is no explaining why nobody says them without telling you how it ends.

In one sentence

A ferryman's granddaughter grows up, and both sons of the town's boat-master fall in love with her. The brothers compete in the fairest way they know, the old man sounds out her heart in the gentlest way he knows, and the girl hides her feelings in the shyest way there is. Everyone means well and nobody says it plainly, and so one young man drowns, one leaves, one old man dies in a thunderstorm, and a girl is left at the crossing waiting for someone who may never come back.

Coordinates

Shen Congwen (1902–1988) came from Fenghuang in West Hunan, with Miao and Tujia blood in the family. He joined a local army at fourteen or fifteen, spent years drifting with troops up and down the Yuan River valley, and in his early twenties went alone to Beijing, where he taught himself to write, starting with a great many rejection slips. Border Town was serialized in the weekly Guowen zhoubao in 1934 and came out as a book the same year. He had just married Zhang Zhaohe and was living in Peiping, writing about a riverbank he had left more than a decade earlier.

Its place in literary history is easy to locate. While most of his contemporaries were writing about class, enlightenment and national salvation, Shen wrote a pastoral with almost no social conflict in it: a small, self-sufficient, warm-hearted world, and the kind of tragedy that happens inside such a world without anyone needing to be wicked. After 1949 he largely stopped writing fiction and turned to the history of Chinese costume. The book went cold on the mainland for thirty years; abroad, C. T. Hsia's A History of Modern Chinese Fiction (1961) championed it, and in the 1980s it came home to become the little town every Chinese reader seems to remember and none can return to. In English it is best known in Jeffrey C. Kinkley's 2009 translation.

The story

Act I · Under the pagoda: a crossing and an old debt

Chadong lives off the water. The river runs past the town and on down to Chenzhou and Dongting Lake. In town there is a boat-master named Shunshun who runs the docks and the boat crews. He was a soldier in his youth, made his money on the river, and is generous to anyone in trouble. A mile outside town, where the creek meets the river, is a spot called Bixi Ju, Green Creek Bluff, with a public ferry, a white pagoda,Setup ① and a small house. The old man has run the ferry there for fifty years. The boat belongs to the government and the government gives him rations, so in his view no one who crosses owes him a single coin.

He lives with his granddaughter, Cuicui, and her existence is the old debt this crossing carries. More than a decade earlier, the old man's only daughter fell in love with a soldier garrisoned in Chadong. They had courted in song, and she became pregnant. The soldier wanted to run off downriver with her, but running meant he would betray his duty as a soldier and she would abandon her lonely father. They could not leave and could not part, and the soldier reasoned that if they could not be together alive, they could at least be together in death. He took poison first. She could not bear to take the child with her, so she waited until the baby was born, then walked down to the creek, drank a great deal of cold water, and died.Setup ②

The old man raised the orphan. The house was ringed with green bamboo, so he named her Cuicui, "emerald." She grew up in wind and sun, tanned dark, clear-eyed, like a small wild creature of the hills: she would bolt from strangers, then, once satisfied they meant no harm, go calmly back to playing at the water's edge. The old man worried about nothing in the world except one thing: that when she grew up, she might go the way her mother went. He never said a word of this to Cuicui.

Act II · Three Dragon Boat Festivals: two brothers and one insult

The liveliest day of Chadong's year is the Dragon Boat Festival. Boats race on the river, the banks are packed, and the town's officials release a flock of ducks into the water: whoever catches one keeps it. The love story of the novel hangs on three of these festivals.

The first was two years earlier. Cuicui, thirteen or so, went into town with her grandfather to watch the races. He ran into friends, was pulled away for a drink, then went back to mind the ferry so another man could see the races, telling her to wait for him by the river. It got dark. The crowds thinned and went home. He did not come. Sitting alone above the darkening water, Cuicui had a sudden, frightening thought:Setup ③ what if Grandpa were dead?

Just then a young man came up out of the river with a duck in his hands. This was Shunshun's second son, Nuosong, known around town as Number Two. Seeing a girl alone in the dark, he told her to go wait upstairs at his house, and teased her that the big fish in the river would come and bite her. Cuicui thought he was trying to lure her into one of the stilt houses along the river street, where boatmen went for women, and she cursed him with the local equivalent of "drop dead." The dog barked at him too. He laughed it off and sent one of his men with a torch to walk her home. Only on the way did she learn that the man she had cursed was the Number Two the whole town admired. She said nothing about it to her grandfather, but from that night on there was someone in her heart.

The second festival was the year before. Number Two was away downriver with the boats and missed it. Cuicui and her grandfather went into town, got caught in the rain, sheltered at Shunshun's house, and met the elder son, Tianbao, known as Number One. He was as sturdy and open-handed as his brother, more like their father in temperament, and blunt. He made no secret of liking Cuicui, telling the old ferryman to his face that the girl was as lovely as a statue of Guanyin. Cuicui did not respond. Her mind was on the one who had not come home.

The third festival is this year's. Shunshun invited grandfather and granddaughter to watch the races from his own stilt house. Number Two was back, out on the river in one of the boats. Squeezed among the women upstairs, Cuicui overheard two strangers gossiping: a squire from the stockade up the hill wanted to marry his daughter to Number Two, and the dowry would be a new mill.Setup ④ In a town like this, a mill is a serious fortune. Cuicui found the idea strange without knowing why, and it put her in a sulk with everyone. The squire's daughter was there too, well dressed, sitting at ease. Cuicui said nothing, and carried the sulk all the way home.

Act III · The cart road and the horse road: the fairest contest

Number One was serious. He asked an old cavalryman in town, a man named Yang and a longtime friend of the ferryman, to sound the old man out. The old man's answer is the most important speech in the book. Roughly: there are two roads to a marriage here. One is the "cart road": you send a matchmaker, you talk to the family, you do it by the rules. The other is the "horse road": the young man stands on the cliff across the water and sings to the girl, for three years and six months if need be, until her heart gives. If it's the cart road, he as the elder would decide. If it's the horse road, it is up to Cuicui.

Number One took the cart road first and formally sent a matchmaker. The old man received him courteously and neither accepted nor refused: he would have to ask the child. Then he asked her, roundabout, testing her with jokes. Cuicui blushed, turned, ran, and said nothing. The old man could not read her and would not push her. What he feared was not that she would marry the wrong man, but that she would marry a man she did not want. So he let the matter sit, and waited.

Meanwhile the brothers finally learned each other's secret: they loved the same girl. Two young men raised together on the river did not fall out. They worked out a fair method in the Chadong way: by local custom they would take turns on moonlit nights, singing from the cliff across from Green Creek Bluff, and whomever Cuicui answered would have her. Number One knew he sang badly and his brother sang beautifully, and he suggested that on his turns his brother sing for him. Number Two refused: each should sing for himself, or it wasn't fair.

On the night itself they went together. Number One said that since he had already gone by the cart road, his brother should start. Number Two began to sing, and Number One knew at once that he was no match. He never opened his mouth. Every song that drifted across the creek that night was sung by Number Two alone.

Cuicui was asleep. In her dream she heard singing so lovely that it lifted her soul out of her body, and she floated, weightless, up over the pagoda, across the vegetable patch, and halfway up the cliff opposite, to pick a handful of the tiger-ear saxifrage she could never reach by day. When she woke she remembered the dream but knew nothing of the song. The old man had lain awake and heard it all, but he took the singer to be Number One. When he next saw Number One he happily praised him for taking the cart road and the horse road both. Some time passed before he learned from others that the singer had been Number Two.

Act IV · The rapids: one man goes under and everyone falls silent

Number One swallowed it. Unwilling to stay home and watch his brother and the girl, he went downriver on one of the family's boats. At a dangerous stretch of water called the Ci Rapids the boat came to grief. He went into the river and did not come out.

When the news reached Chadong, Shunshun's household blamed no one aloud, but the same thought sat on all of them: if that old ferryman hadn't been so evasive, neither saying yes nor saying no, Number One might never have left. Number Two took it hardest. His brother had gone away to make way for him. He went downriver to search for the body and found nothing. When he came back he was cold to the old ferryman at the crossing, answering half of whatever he was asked.

The old man took that coldness as his own guilt. Not knowing what else to do, he did the only thing he knew how to do: he sounded people out, more carefully than ever. Once, when Number Two was crossing, he worked the question into small talk: the mill or the ferry, which did he really want? Number Two let it show. He wanted the ferry. And after that, he left anyway. The old man went into town to see Shunshun, steeled himself, and raised the matter outright, hoping to get Cuicui a straight answer. Shunshun was polite, poured him a drink, and gently closed the subject. Number Two had gone downriver to Taoyuan some days before.

Payoff ④ ↑Back in Act II the mill was a scrap of gossip among the women, a thing that put Cuicui in a sulk and that the reader took for the entrance of a rival. Here it turns out to have been a choice. A mill is a fortune you can see. The ferry is nothing at all: a government boat, a poor old man, a girl who won't talk. Number Two chose the ferry. It is the closest thing to a declaration of love in the entire book, and it was made to the grandfather, not the girl, and then he left. Shen puts the most valuable thing in town beside the least valuable, lets a young man choose right, and still does not let him cross over.

Act V · The thunderstorm: the pagoda falls

Summer came on and the days grew heavier. The old man came back from town with something on his mind and said nothing about it to Cuicui. She sensed something, clung to him more than ever, and could not bring herself to ask. One night thunder rolled in over the hills and rain fell all night long. The creek rose. In the house the two of them listened to the rain and went to sleep, each with their own worries.

At daybreak Cuicui got up and saw that the white pagoda behind the house had collapsed and the ferry had been carried off by the flood. She ran back in and called her grandfather. He did not answer. Sometime as the storm was dying, the old man had died.

Payoff ③ ↑On that festival evening in Act II, waiting by the river for a grandfather who didn't come, Cuicui had thought: what if Grandpa were dead? At the time it was just a child's panic at being left too long, wiped away a moment later by Number Two's torch, and the reader forgot it along with her. Shen hid the book's only rehearsal of death inside the same night as the girl's first stirring of love. The two most important men in her life were, on that one evening, one present and one absent. Two years later the thought came true, and the one who had been present was no longer in Chadong.

Payoff ① ↑The pagoda was standing on the first page, like the household's house number, and the reader passed it by. In Cuicui's dream, the first thing the song lifted her over was the pagoda. Now, on this night, the pagoda goes down together with the old man who had kept the crossing beneath it for fifty years. It is not scenery that happens to collapse. It is the old world itself: while the old man stood, the tower stood; once he was gone, it could not. Later the people of Chadong took up a collection and rebuilt it. A pagoda can be rebuilt. A man cannot.

Shunshun came, people came from town, and they buried the old man on the ridge behind the house. The one who stayed to keep vigil with Cuicui was old Yang the cavalryman, the same man who had once carried Number One's question to the ferry. One by one he told her the things she hadn't known: why Number One left, who had sung that night, why Number Two would not come back, how much her grandfather had worried over her these last months. Only then did Cuicui understand the whole of it, and she cried all night.

Payoff ② ↑The old debt from Act I finally comes due here. As a young groom, Yang had once led his horses to Green Creek Bluff and sung to Cuicui's mother, and she had paid him no attention; it was the soldier she wanted. More than a decade later, the singer she never answered has become her daughter's only support in the world, and her daughter is waiting for another singer. Only here, too, does the reader fully understand the old man's endless evasions. He had watched a girl decide on a man by the sound of his voice and end at the creek drinking cold water. He would not choose for Cuicui because he was afraid of driving another one to her death; he would not say yes because he was afraid she was choosing wrong. That fear runs under every page from the first to the last.

Shunshun offered to take Cuicui into his house to wait for Number Two, as his son's bride in all but name. She refused. She would stay at the crossing and wait. Yang stayed on with her, and since the ferry still needed a keeper, Cuicui took over her grandfather's work. By winter the pagoda had been rebuilt. The young man who had sung under the moon and lifted her soul in her sleep had not come back. The novel's last sentence says, in effect: perhaps this man will never come back, perhaps he will come back "tomorrow." (Chapter 21, the closing line.)

If you want the fuse, it isn't Number One's death, or the mill, or the misunderstanding on the night of the song. The fuse is that long drink of cold water more than a decade earlier. Cuicui's mother's death taught the old man one lesson: love can walk a girl into the creek. So all his love for Cuicui turned into not daring: not daring to ask straight, not daring to accept, not daring to refuse. His vagueness misled Number One; Number One's leaving filled Number Two with guilt; Number Two's guilt shut Shunshun's family up; and the silence of everyone together finally crushed the old man. A tragedy with no villain: one father's grief, passed down over the years, until it became the silence of an entire town.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · Dusk by the river: a love that begins with a curse

Run it again. It's nearly dark, the race crowds have gone, and a girl of thirteen sits by the river waiting for an old man who isn't coming, the thought "what if he's dead" still fresh in her. A young man climbs out of the water with a duck, says a few kind and slightly teasing things. She mishears him and curses him. The dog barks. He smiles and sends someone with a torch to see her home. She finds out who he is on the way.

Why does it work? First, it is a love that begins with a misunderstanding, and the misunderstanding runs in one direction: mistaking a good man for a bad one. Every later misunderstanding in the book has this shape; they just get more lethal. Second, the information comes very late. When she curses him, she doesn't know who he is, and neither do we; his name arrives in torchlight, on the walk home. Her embarrassment and the first flutter of feeling are compressed into that small gap in time. Third, Shen never writes a sentence saying she fell for him. He only says that when she got home she told her grandfather nothing. In this book, silence is the shape that love takes. At the beginning that is sweet. Later it turns to poison.

Two · The moonlit song and the saxifrage: she was asleep, so she missed it

Run it again. On the cliff, one man sings while another stands beside him, growing more speechless with every verse. In the little house across the creek, an old man lies awake listening, delighted, and gets the singer wrong. A girl sleeps, and in her dream the song lifts her up the cliff to pick the tiger-ear saxifrage she can't reach by day: the fleshy-leaved plant that grows on the rock face fifteen or twenty feet up, which she looks up at every day while hauling the ferry.

Why does it work? Because the most important declaration in the book reaches none of the three people present correctly. The singer assumes she heard. The listener is asleep. The one awake picks the wrong man. And a fourth swallows his own love back down. The point of view is chopped fine here: Shen is on the cliff, then in the house, then inside Cuicui's dream, and the reader is the only one who knows everything. The handful of saxifrage is Shen's single consolation for the miss: the song did reach her, but it reached her in a dream, somewhere she couldn't describe when she woke. She received the gift without knowing who gave it, which is exactly where she ends up.

Three · After the storm: she wakes, and the world is missing two things

Run it again. Thunder and rain all night. At dawn the rain stops. The creek is brown and swollen, the ferry is gone, the pagoda is a heap of rubble. The girl goes back in and calls her grandfather, calls again, and no one answers.

Why does it work? Shen writes death as an inventory. He doesn't show how the old man died or whether he had any last words. He just lets Cuicui discover things one by one: no pagoda, no boat, no grandfather. The death happens in the narrative's blind spot, "as the storm was dying," like a job quietly finished while everyone slept. In terms of rhythm, the chapters before this have grown steadily more airless: heavy weather, a heavy-hearted old man, fewer and fewer words. Then the storm releases everything that had been pressed down, followed by a morning of terrible quiet. There is no violence anywhere in the book. Its single act of violence is the weather.

The book's core devices

Border Town isn't a novel of invented worlds, but it does have two devices: the two roads, and the blank space.

The first: the cart road and the horse road, two grammars for one world. The two paths to marriage that the old man mentions in passing are really the structure of the book. The cart road means matchmakers, negotiations, property; it belongs with the mill, the boat-master's standing, the squire's dowry, a world that runs on contracts and assets. The horse road means song, moonlight, the heart's choice; it belongs with the ferry, the pagoda, the handful of saxifrage, a world that runs on feeling and instinct. Chadong lives by both grammars at once, and both are kindly meant. The tragedy follows its logic like this: Number One takes the cart road; Cuicui recognizes only the horse road; the old man stands between them and dares not answer for her on either. Then when Number Two takes the horse road, the old man credits it to Number One. Each road works fine by itself. Where they cross, someone dies. What holds up is how precisely this captures a society in transition between arranged marriage and free choice, where the old rules are no longer firm enough and the new ones not yet clear. What is literary necessity: real West Hunan was nowhere near this gentle. Shen pushed the bandits, the warlord armies and the opium out of the frame, leaving this world with only one kind of conflict: misunderstanding between good people.

The second: blank space, writing the most important things by not writing them. The most important sentences in this book are never spoken. Cuicui never once says "I love Number Two." Number Two never says a tender word to her. Number One tells no one why he leaves. The old man dies without ever telling Cuicui what he feared. Shen writes landscapes, weather, ducks at the festival, stilt houses, and whenever he reaches the thing that matters most to a character, he has them blush, walk off, go haul the ferry. This isn't laziness. It is a technical choice: it puts the reader in the gap between the characters, watching exactly how the words fail to get said. The cost is that some readers find the characters too faint, too much like figures in a painting. But if they had said what they meant, the book would hold one misunderstanding instead of a whole world.

What it forces you to think about

One · The ferryman: if the child won't speak, do you speak for her, or wait?

You have raised a girl from infancy, and her mother died for love. Now a good family comes asking for her, and all she does is blush and run. You can tell there is someone on her mind, but you can't tell who. What do you do?

First, the strongest case for deciding on her behalf. A girl of fifteen or sixteen who has hardly seen the world, in a place where courtship rules are this intricate, simply does not know how to say what she wants. Her silence is not a choice; it is waiting for someone to take the first step for her. The old man is her only family, and he had words available. He could have asked, "Is it Number Two?" or told Number One plainly, "No." Every time he hedged, three young people had one more layer to guess through. "Respecting her wishes" can sometimes mean leaving the pain of the decision to the person least equipped to bear it. Number One's death is, at least in part, the interest on that respect.

The other side is just as strong. No one knows better than the old man what happens when a girl's fate gets decided for her. Her mother was crushed by "you can't have both": the soldier's duty, the father's loneliness, burdens other people loaded on her. He has sworn that nothing will weigh on Cuicui like that, himself included. Choose Number One for her, and what if her heart is set on Number Two? Turn Number One away, and what if she regrets it later? By what right does anyone gamble with another person's whole life? He waits because, as he sees it, a wrong marriage lasts a lifetime and a late answer costs only a few months. What he didn't reckon with is that some answers, given late, also cost a lifetime.

Where does the author stand? Shen loves the old man, but he lets Shunshun's family lay the blame at his door, and lets the old man die carrying it. He doesn't condemn him, and he doesn't acquit him either. Bring it home: when a child of yours is choosing a college, a career, a partner, and saying nothing, are you the one who asks directly or the one who waits for them to speak? And while you wait, what are they waiting for?

Two · Number Two: your brother died over her. Can you still marry her?

You and your brother loved the same person. You agreed to compete fairly, and you won. Your brother left home heartbroken and drowned on the way. She is still at the crossing, and it may well be you she wants. Do you go back?

First, the strongest case for going back. The contest was fair: your brother proposed it, conceded it, and chose to leave. His death was a boating accident, not anyone's plot. Cuicui did nothing wrong; she doesn't even know who sang that night. If Number Two stays away out of guilt, he is making a living, innocent girl pay a dead man's bill. If his brother could see, would he want both of them to ruin their lives on his account? Staying away looks like loyalty to the dead, but in effect it turns his own grief into her punishment.

The other side: some things don't untangle by logic. Every time he stood at that crossing he would remember that his brother died on a boat going downriver, and that his brother got on the boat because he heard Number Two sing. Marrying her would mean eating and sleeping beside that memory every day. And his family, his father, have already laid the blame on the household at the ferry. This marriage isn't just between two people; it asks a family that has just lost its eldest son to welcome, smiling, the very reason he died. Some happiness is within reach, but if you reach for it you are never again the person you were. Leaving isn't coldness. It is a young man trying to find a way to live with himself.

Where does the author stand? He gives Number Two not one word in his own defense, and doesn't bring him back. The last line, perhaps never, perhaps tomorrow, hands the dilemma back to the reader untouched. Bring it home: when something you get happens to be the price of someone else's loss (a job, a position, a person), do you accept it with a clear conscience, or feel forever in someone's debt?

Three · Cuicui's waiting: fidelity, or a girl trapped by a town?

At the end Cuicui refuses to move into Shunshun's house. She stays at the crossing, takes over her grandfather's boat, and waits for a man who never said he would return.

First, the strongest case that this is something beautiful. No one arranged this for her. For the first time she makes her own decision: not to go live in another family's house as a bride in name only, but to keep to the water she knows, her own boat, her own heart. Taking over the ferry, she takes over a way of living: take no money, expect no return, be good to everyone who crosses. Her waiting isn't passivity. In a world where everyone says half of what they mean, she has finally settled on one thing and will not give it up. In a world where everyone is weighing the odds, someone willing to hold on for something uncertain is exactly what this book exists to protect.

The other side: she is still in her teens. The man she waits for never said a word to her; he sang one night while she slept. Every relative she had is dead, and staying at the crossing may not be a choice at all. Perhaps the crossing is the only thing she knows how to do. To the reader, the ending is poetry. For a real girl, it is a lifetime tending a boat. It is very easy to admire someone else's predicament as a virtue, especially when they bear it quietly, without tears or complaint. The more beautifully Shen writes it, the sharper that question gets.

Where does the author stand? He ends on an open hope, and puts "tomorrow" in quotation marks. Those quotation marks are Shen's own hesitation: he wants to believe, and doesn't dare believe on her behalf. Bring it home: is there someone in your life who has been waiting for something that might come "tomorrow"? Do you urge them to move on, or wait with them?

Motifs: what Shen kept writing about

Shen spent his life writing around one river system: the Yuan and its tributaries, and the soldiers, boatmen, boat prostitutes, ferry crossings and village girls along its banks. A few obsessions run through all of it. First, the natural decency of "country people" set against the respectability of the city. In the story "Husband," a farmer goes into town to visit his wife, who works as a prostitute on a riverboat, and slowly wakes up inside his humiliation; in Shen's hands, country roughness holds an honesty city people have lost. Second, girls by the water and the lives that fate pushes them through. "Xiaoxiao" is about a girl married at twelve to a husband of three, who is muddled through her disgrace and forgiven by the indifference of village custom. "Sansan" is about a girl raised at a mill who feels a vague tenderness for a sick young man from the city, and he dies. A mill, a wait, a feeling never spoken: in Border Town these parts finally come together into a complete shape. Third, this world is disappearing. In his later novel Long River he wrote about how modern things (new campaigns, local security forces, outside money, outside rules) were seeping into West Hunan.

He once said that he wanted to build, in words, a small Greek temple, and that what it would enshrine was human nature (preface to a 1936 selection of his work, paraphrased). Border Town is that temple in its most complete form: a world without villains, where good people still push one another into the river.

Controversy and criticism

Pastoral as escapism. This is the most common criticism and the one with the most history. In the 1930s and 40s, leftist critics accused Shen of dodging class conflict and prettifying a backward countryside, writing idylls while the nation was in mortal danger. In 1948 Guo Moruo published "Denouncing Reactionary Literature and Art," naming Shen as a "pink" writer. Around 1949 Shen suffered a breakdown and attempted suicide, and afterward set fiction aside. That history kept Border Town labelled "out of step" for decades; its defenders today say that precisely because it refused to follow the times, it survived them.

An idealized West Hunan. The West Hunan Shen actually knew had bandits, warlord armies, beheadings and opium; his own autobiography describes killings he witnessed as a young soldier. Border Town cuts almost all of that out. Critics call it a dishonest pastoral fantasy. Others point out that when Shen went home in 1934 he saw for himself that the region was already changing, and that Long River was his attempt to fill in that missing half.

Cuicui as a figure. Cuicui barely speaks. She blushes, runs, falls silent, and is likened to a small wild animal. Feminist readings see her as a gazed-upon, naturalized "pure maiden" who carries a male writer's nostalgia for home and innocence rather than a will of her own. The rebuttal is that at the end she turns down Shunshun's arrangement, the first time she truly decides for herself.

Characters who are too good. Nearly everyone is kind, generous and courteous. Some readers find this makes them thin, with all the conflict driven by chance and misunderstanding, and the tragedy lighter for it.

The Nobel story. The most widely repeated claim comes from the Swedish sinologist Göran Malmqvist, a member of the Swedish Academy, who said more than once afterward that the Swedish Academy had been ready to give Shen the 1988 prize, and that Shen died that May. It rests on one academician's later account; the deliberations stay sealed for fifty years, so it cannot be checked.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · The illusion of transparency: Gilovich, Savitsky and Medvec (1998) found that people consistently overestimate how much of their inner state others can see. Nearly everyone in Chadong lives inside this illusion: Cuicui thinks her feelings are obvious, the old man thinks his hints are plain enough. Where it differs: the experiments measured momentary nerves, while the town's silence is held up by a whole system of custom.
  • Anthropology · Mauss's The Gift: In The Gift (1925), Marcel Mauss argued that every gift carries an obligation to return it, binding people into a web of debt. The ferryman turning forced coins into tea and tobacco for the next passenger looks like a deliberate refusal to owe anyone. Yet Number One's death leaves a debt between the two households that no one can ever repay. Where it differs: Mauss described institutional exchange and status rivalry; Chadong's gifts genuinely expect nothing back.
  • Sociology · Tönnies on community and society: In 1887 Ferdinand Tönnies distinguished Gemeinschaft, bound by kinship and custom, from Gesellschaft, bound by contract and interest. The ferry and the mill stand at the two poles: one takes no money, the other has a price. Where it differs: Tönnies described a whole society's historical shift; Shen sets it inside one young man's single answer.
  • Chinese philosophy · Laozi's "small state, few people": Chapter 80 of the Daodejing pictures a tiny realm of few people and simple tools, content with its food and customs. Chadong is much like it: contented, uncompetitive, keeping its local ways. Where it differs: Laozi's villagers never visit the next state, while Chadong is a river port living on trade, and the mill and the downriver boats have long since let the outside in.

Ten lines to take with you

① A story with no villain can still carry off a whole family and leave one girl at the crossing.

② Cuicui's love began with a curse: she took a good man for a bad one, the first of the book's misunderstandings.

③ The cart road is a matchmaker; the horse road is a song. Both are kindly meant, and where they cross, someone dies.

④ That night the singer thought she heard, the listener was asleep, and the one awake got the singer wrong.

⑤ In a dream she picked the saxifrage she could never reach by day: she received it without knowing who gave it.

⑥ A mill is a fortune you can see; a ferry is nothing at all. Number Two chose right and still never crossed.

⑦ The old man's evasions weren't cunning but fear: he had watched a girl follow a song down to the creek and drink cold water.

⑧ Respecting someone's choice can mean leaving the pain of deciding to the person least able to bear it.

⑨ A pagoda can be rebuilt. A man can't.

⑩ Perhaps he'll never come back; perhaps he'll come back "tomorrow." The quotation marks are the author's own hesitation.
