Brave New World

Aldous Huxley · 1932

中文 →

A temperature-controlled hall, the light cold. On a conveyor, a line of glass bottles creeps forward thirty-three centimetres an hour for two hundred and sixty-seven days, and then the bottles are opened and the people inside are taken out.
A man in a white coat is explaining to a party of students: this batch is destined for the tropics, so it is being heated now — the body learns to love warmth before it is quite a body. That batch will work in mines, so it is rocked as it grows; the better rocked, the less sick later.
The students take notes. Nobody winces. This is not a punishment, it is a benefit: when the time comes, these people will genuinely want the work they get.
The whole book asks a single question: if everyone gets what they want, what exactly are you missing?

In one sentence

In a world that manufactures people on an assembly line, teaches morality by whispering slogans into sleeping children, and digests every discomfort with a side-effect-free pill, a few faintly uneasy citizens bring back from a primitive reservation a young man raised on Shakespeare; he is first a sensation, then a nuisance, and finally discovers that this world has no intention of crushing him — it simply has no use for him — so he retreats to a disused lighthouse to punish himself, is turned into the next entertainment, and after one night of frenzy hangs himself. The horror of this book is not that anyone is oppressed. It is that nobody is unhappy.

Coordinates

Aldous Huxley (1894–1963) came from an almost unfair intellectual family: his grandfather was T. H. Huxley, the biologist who fought Darwin's public battles; his brother Julian was a distinguished biologist; his half-brother Andrew later won a Nobel Prize in physiology. An eye disease in adolescence nearly blinded him and closed off medicine, so he wrote instead. Brave New World, his fifth novel, appeared in 1932; before it came the clever, acid social satires of Crome Yellow and Point Counter Point.

The title is Miranda's line from The Tempest — a girl who has seen only her father and a monster, meeting a crowd of strangers for the first time and marvelling at the brave new world that has such people in it. Hanging that piece of pure innocence over a dystopia is the book's central technique: it does not scold, it makes you see the beauty first.

Its place in literary history is settled: with Zamyatin's We (1924) and Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949) it forms the standard dystopian trio. The difference is method — Orwell governs by fear, Huxley by satisfaction. One is a boot stamping on a face; the other is a sweet dissolving in the mouth.

The story

Act one · A guided tour: how people are made

The year is A.F. 632 — After Ford. This world worships Henry Ford; the man who invented the assembly line has replaced God, and people sign themselves not with a cross but with a T, for the Model T. The planet is unified under ten World Controllers, and the state motto is carved over the door: Community, Identity, Stability. The novel opens with a tour of the Central London Hatchery and Conditioning Centre, the Director walking a class of students down the line and explaining as he goes. What he explains is the world's foundation: people are not born, they are manufactured — and manufactured to order.

First, Bokanovsky's Process: a fertilised egg is arrested, forced to bud and re-bud, until it yields as many as ninety-six genetically identical embryos — the same face repeated down a workshop, working the same machine. The Director calls it one of the major instruments of stability: standard people doing standard work. Then predestination: five castes, Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, Epsilon. Caste is not earned afterwards, it is arranged in the bottle — the lower the caste, the less oxygen, the less brain, until Epsilons are grown as semi-morons. This is not cruelty, it is fitting: make a person physiologically suited to the life he is going to lead, and he will never experience that life as a hardship.

Then the coldest passage in the book, a lesson in the infant nurseries. A batch of Delta babies is set down on the floor in front of bowls of roses and bright picture books. They crawl over, grab at the pages, gurgle. An alarm shrieks, the floor is electrified, the babies convulse and scream. Two hundred repetitions later, this batch will feel instinctive revulsion at books and flowers for life. The reasoning is stated plainly: books might let somebody read the wrong thing, and people who love flowers go off to the countryside, and the countryside consumes nothing — a citizen who does not consume is an economic waste. Plant ①

Finally hypnopaedia, sleep-teaching: a speaker beside the pillow repeating short sentences, a hundred and twenty times a night, for years. The novel is careful about its limits — the method is useless for teaching science, the child can answer nothing next morning; but for teaching what is good, which caste I belong to, what I ought to want, it works terrifyingly well. So every head carries a set of sentences it never thought and always obeys: everyone belongs to everyone else; ending is better than mending. In 1932 Huxley had already written down the real principle of propaganda: it does not change your judgement, it changes the preference you judge from.

Three pressure valves complete the machine: sex is entirely free and more or less compulsory, and sticking with one person is the disreputable thing; the great majority of female embryos are sterilised in the bottle; and, above all, soma — a drug that delivers pleasure at once, with no muddle, no hangover, and, at a large enough dose, a holiday of several days. Everyone collects a ration at the end of the working day.

Act two · Three people who don't quite fit

The world is nearly perfect. A few components are loose.

Bernard Marx is an Alpha-Plus psychologist who is a head shorter than his caste should be — rumour says alcohol got into his blood-surrogate while he was still in the bottle. He is permanently awkward: unsure of himself with lower castes, sick of hearing people discuss their sex lives the way others discuss weather. He mistakes this awkwardness for a philosophy.

Helmholtz Watson has the opposite problem: he is too good. He lectures at the College of Emotional Engineering, writes advertising copy, hypnopaedic rhymes, feely scenarios, all of it excellent. And he has a surplus he cannot spend — a sense that there is a power in him meant to say something important, and he knows neither what it is nor what words could carry it. Plant ②

Lenina Crowne is a Beta who vaccinates embryos in the Hatchery. She is not a rebel; she is this world's healthiest product — pretty, good-tempered, punctual about changing men, and reaching for soma whenever anything stings. Her single irregularity is that she has been seeing the same man for four months, which her friends already find close to indecent.

Bernard invites her on a holiday to the Savage Reservation in New Mexico. Getting the permit signed, he finds the Director unexpectedly talkative: as a young man the Director went to that same reservation with a girl; there was a thunderstorm, she fell into a gully and was never found, and he came back alone. The moment he has finished he is mortified and furious — he should not have shown a subordinate a useless emotion like that. To recover, he threatens Bernard on the spot: behave, or you go to Iceland. Plant ③

Act three · The Reservation: another way to live

The Reservation is a patch of the world deliberately left alone: an electrified fence around the New Mexico desert, inside which people still give birth, grow old, sicken, worship, have parents and husbands and wives. The World State leaves it because civilising it would cost more than it is worth. Bernard and Lenina walk into a village called Malpais and Lenina nearly breaks down: dust, dogs, flies, old men — she has never seen a wrinkle, since in the civilised world people look twenty until they die at sixty. A woman is nursing a baby, an image her education has made close to obscene. She reaches for her soma and finds she left it at the rest-house.

A ceremony is under way in the plaza. Drums, meal scattered on the ground, snakes; a boy walking a circle while a masked man whips him, once round, twice round, blood on the dust, the crowd sounding at each stroke. Lenina covers her face. Afterwards a fair-haired young man comes over and speaks to them — in English — and says: it should have been me, I should have been the one, they would not let me do it. Plant ④

This is John. His mother Linda is the girl lost in the gully twenty-odd years ago: she hit her head, was taken in by the villagers, and was pregnant — which nobody from the civilised world would have thought of, pregnancy there being an almost impossible accident. She had John.

Their life in the village is wretched, and what makes it wretched is precisely the civilisation Linda carries. She goes with the village men because everyone belongs to everyone else, and the men's wives corner her indoors and beat her; she cannot mend clothes, having been taught that ending is better than mending; she drinks mescal brought by the men in place of soma until she is insensible. John, as her son, is shut out by every other boy and barred from the initiation ceremony.

He learned to read from Linda's old work manual — a child whose primer was The Chemical and Bacteriological Conditioning of the Embryo, which is Huxley's cruellest and saddest single stroke. Later one of the village men produced an old book: the complete Shakespeare. From then on John had a language. All his pain, love, shame and vengefulness comes out in four-hundred-year-old English — when he hates his mother's lover, what sounds in his head is Hamlet at his mother's bedside. Plant ⑤ He grew up hearing Linda describe the Other Place; when Bernard offers to take him to London, what he blurts out is Miranda's line about the brave new world.

Act four · The Savage comes to London

Bernard gets a permit from the Controller, Mustapha Mond, and brings John and Linda back. He has a calculation in mind: the Director is about to deal with him.

On the day of his return the Director assembles the whole Centre to announce Bernard's exile to Iceland and make a public lesson of it. Bernard asks leave to introduce two people. Linda comes through the door — swollen, toothless, her face ruined by alcohol and soma — and lunges at the Director calling him by the pet name she used decades ago. Dead silence, then howling laughter. Then John walks in, kneels, and says the one unsayable word in this world: my father. Payoff ③ ↑

Look back at that odd confession in act two and it all resolves: the Director telling the thunderstorm story was not small talk, it was the one leak of nostalgia this world permits itself, and the rage he turned on Bernard immediately afterwards was not bureaucratic bullying but a man frightened by himself. Huxley hides the detonator of the whole novel inside a passage that sounds like an awkward digression. "Mother" here is obscene; "father" is merely a joke. The Director resigns on the spot and vanishes from the book.

Bernard becomes famous overnight. The man with a Savage is a social lion, women queue for him, and he discovers that he likes this world very much — his rebellion, it turns out, was only the resentment of someone not invited. But John will not perform: one evening, with the whole fashionable world assembled and the Arch-Community-Songster of Canterbury in attendance, he locks himself in his room. Bernard's fame evaporates in an hour, and the guests turn on him harder than before.

The one real connection is Helmholtz. John reads him Shakespeare, and at first Helmholtz cannot stop laughing — words like father and mother are irresistibly funny — but at certain passages he goes quiet. The surplus he could never spend turns out to be this: some things can only be written from inside pain, lack and impossibility, and his world has abolished pain, lack and impossibility. Payoff ② ↑ A man who writes advertising copy will never write those lines, not because he is stupid, but because his world denies him the material.

Between John and Lenina there is only mismatch. She really does fall in love, in the way she was taught: she wants him, so she walks over and takes off her clothes. He wants something else entirely — to do something difficult for her, to be tested, and then to have her properly. Terrified of her and of his own desire, he swears at her in Othello's language and strikes at her; she locks herself in the bathroom, bewildered, having done everything exactly right. There is no villain in the scene. Two people follow the scripts installed in them, and the scripts are incompatible.

Then Linda dies. Since London she has never really woken: she asked for a massive soma ration, was told it would cost her months of life, and took it anyway, with nobody objecting. When John reaches the Park Lane Hospital for the Dying, a group of Delta twins is there for death conditioning — children brought regularly among the dying and given sweets, so that death and fear never form a pair. A crowd of identical children rings his mother's bed, remarking with interest on how hideous the fat old woman is. John hits one of them. The nurse arrives shaking with anger — not for the child, but because that slap might have damaged their conditioning. Payoff ① ↑

Read that against act one. The babies who crawled toward roses and picture books and were shocked into convulsions are exactly these twins, grown. The novel never puts a villain on stage; it simply lets act one's process deliver its product in act four — those two hundred shocks are working for this world right now, and not one of them has failed. The name Linda calls at the end is not her son's; it is that of the man in the Reservation who brought her drink.

John runs out and finds the hospital's soma ration being handed to a crowd of Deltas. Don't you want to be free men, he shouts, and throws the whole box out of the window. The crowd freezes, then riots — not for freedom, for the pills. Helmholtz arrives and joins in with delight; Bernard hovers at the edge, wanting to and not daring. The police come with soma vapour, water pistols loaded with soma solution, and a recorded Synthetic Anti-Riot Speech playing at moderate strength, gently urging everyone to love one another. Minutes later they are all weeping in each other's arms. It is the funniest and most hopeless ten minutes in the book: a revolution dissolved by a smell in the air.

Act five · The conversation behind closed doors, and the whip at the lighthouse

The three are taken to Mustapha Mond, Resident Controller for Western Europe. He is no tyrant; he is the best interlocutor in the book. As a young physicist he was too good, was found out, and was offered a choice: exile to an island, or give up the research and join the government. He took the second, and has spent his life suppressing what he loved. The Bible and a shelf of forbidden books sit in his safe, all of them read.

He opens the ledger for John, and the logic is airtight. After the Nine Years' War came the great collapse; anthrax bombs and economic ruin left humanity a choice between world control and extinction. The first move was not killing, it was the campaign against the past — museums closed, monuments dynamited, every book published before A.F. 150 suppressed. The second was to dismantle each source of instability in turn: family, love, religion, the fear of ageing and death, and the two most awkward of all — high art and free science.

But Shakespeare, John says. Mond answers: you can't write it any more; tragedy needs social instability, and we have none. You can't have stability and Othello both. And science? Mond smiles: science is dangerous too, a wild animal we keep on a chain — truth and happiness cannot both be maximised, and we chose happiness. He offers two experiments: Cyprus was cleared and stocked with Alphas only, the cleverest people governing themselves, and produced a civil war in which nineteen of twenty-two thousand died, the survivors petitioning to be governed again; Ireland was put on a four-hour working day, and the result was not happiness but restlessness and a steep rise in soma consumption. His conclusion: people can bear neither too much freedom nor too much leisure.

Then the sharpest line of all: soma is Christianity without tears — every consolation religion offers, and none of the price it charges. John's reply is the hinge of the book. He does not want comfort; he wants God, poetry, real danger, freedom, goodness, sin. Mond compresses the list into one phrase: you are claiming the right to be unhappy. John pauses, and says:

"All right then, I'm claiming the right to be unhappy."— John, chapter 17 (quoted)

Mond then completes the inventory for him: the right to grow old and ugly and impotent, to have syphilis and cancer, to be short of food, to be lousy, to lie awake dreading tomorrow. John says he will take the lot.

The verdict is exile. Helmholtz asks for the Falkland Islands — he picks a thoroughly bad climate deliberately, convinced that foul weather is good for writing. Bernard hears the word Iceland and collapses, on his knees, sobbing for mercy, and is carried out with every idea he ever had gone out of him. At the door Mond says something almost human: the islands hold the most interesting people in the world, and the two of them are being sent somewhere they can be themselves. John asks to go with them and is refused: I want to go on with the experiment.

John runs. He finds a disused air-lighthouse on the Surrey heath and settles there, purging himself first with mustard and warm water to wash the civilisation out, then digging, planting, making a bow. But he cannot stop punishing himself — he believes he betrayed his mother at the hospital, and that his thoughts about Lenina are filth. So he whips himself. Payoff ④ ↑

The whip goes back to the plaza at Malpais and the boy he envied for being beaten. There, the whip was liturgy — a community's suffering carried by one chosen member. John transplants the identical action to an English lighthouse, where there is no community, no god and no election. The same act is religion in the Reservation and self-mutilation in the civilised world. He has no other language available, exactly as he has no words but Shakespeare's. Payoff ⑤ ↑ Miranda's line about the brave new world was first pure wonder; the second time, at the hospital, seeing a swarm of identical twins, it came out as a curse; by the end there is nobody left to say it to.

He is found, of course. Some Deltas passing by see him at it; a feely producer films him for days with a telescopic camera and releases The Savage of Surrey across Europe. Soon the helicopters come down in swarms, thousands of people crowd the lighthouse and chant for the whip. Lenina climbs out of one of them and walks toward him, crying. He goes at her with the whip and at himself; the crowd goes mad, the singing starts, soma goes round, thousands sway in time with his arm, and the performance becomes an orgy in which he loses himself. Waking the next afternoon, he remembers.

Toward evening, sightseers push open the lighthouse door and find him hanging under the arch. His feet turn slowly, like a pair of compass needles: south, south-south-west, south, south-south-east, east. The novel ends there. The last sentence contains no adjective, only bearings.

Put the five acts together and the fuse is a very small thing: a thunderstorm twenty-odd years back, and a woman who fell into a gully and was not recovered. Because she was left behind there exists a person born outside civilisation and raised on Shakespeare; because that person exists, the Director falls, Bernard is famous and finished within a month, Helmholtz learns what he was missing, and Mond is obliged to read the ledger aloud. What lets the fuse burn at all is an older decision: a world that permits no other way of living keeps one reservation anyway — kept only because governing it would cost too much, and thereby kept as a control group. Every comparison starts with a control group.

Two slow-motion replays

One · Chapter three: three voices cut together

The most radical passage technically is chapter three. Mond lectures students in the garden on history — on monogamy, on the stench of family life, on how people once went mad over love; at the same moment Lenina and Fanny gossip about men and soma in the changing room; at the same moment Bernard stands to one side listening to people discuss Lenina like a cut of meat, shaking with hatred.

Huxley slices the three lines into shorter and shorter fragments, alternating faster and faster, with hypnopaedic slogans wedged between them; by the end each utterance is half a line and the page flickers: a scrap of history, a scrap of gossip, a slogan, a spasm of resentment. The editing is itself the content. It imitates exactly how this society runs — everyone talking at once, every sentence a ready-made someone else installed, no thought belonging whole to anybody. If it makes you dizzy, that is because this is the sound of that world's mind.

Two · The last paragraph: pull the camera back and say nothing

One page earlier there are thousands of people, music, drumming, bodies, a rhythm out of control; turn the page and everything stops: a door opens, a pair of feet, turning slowly. Huxley does three things here. First, he does not describe a death, he gives bearings — south, south-south-west, south — a corpse's rotation reported in compass points, cold as an instrument reading. Second, he lets nobody comment: no mourning, no authorial summary. Third, the vantage point is the party of sightseers at the door — our last sight of John comes through the eyes of people who arrived to watch a show.

The whole novel argues that this world converts everything into consumption, and the final sentence performs the argument in its form: even his death is a stop on the tour. Any lyrical line in its place would halve the force.

The machine explained: which parts are real

Brave New World does not rest on one idea; it is a set of interlocking institutions. Taken apart, some components are real science, some are elegant invention, and some were invention in 1932 and are news today.

What is real

Moral education by conditioned reflex: real. Shocking infants into hating books and roses is Pavlov plus the behaviourism of John B. Watson, who in 1920 conditioned an infant to fear a white rat. Naming a character Helmholtz Watson is not an accident. That human likes and dislikes can be installed from outside has never been fantasy.

The principle of propaganda: real, and stated more precisely than in most political science. The novel says outright that sleep-teaching cannot convey science, only evaluations. That is its most prophetic line: effective manipulation does not argue with you; it arrives before your preferences form and supplies them. A recommendation engine does not persuade you either — it decides what you encounter, and therefore what you come to like.

Consumption as civic duty: real. "Ending is better than mending" is aimed straight at 1920s America, which Huxley visited in 1926; Ford's line, the advertising trade and Hollywood supplied his material. Planned obsolescence and consumption-as-employment-policy were live economic arguments of the period.

What is invented

Soma: pharmacologically a fairy tale. A drug that gives pleasure without impairing work, without addiction, without hangover, and that can put you away for several days on schedule, does not exist — every known euphoriant collects its debt in tolerance, dependence or damage. (The name comes from the ritual drink of the Vedas.) Its function in the book is structural: it replaces all the violence this society would otherwise need. With soma, no secret police are required.

Bokanovsky's Process: partly real, wholly implausible. One embryo becoming several identical individuals happens in nature, and splitting early embryos is done in livestock, but reliably making ninety-six people out of one egg is fantasy. What matters more is the purpose: the book treats it as an instrument of stability because identical people do not envy one another — a sociological hypothesis, not a biological result.

Hypnopaedia: since disproved. Sleep-learning was a fashionable idea in the 1920s and 30s and still looked like science when Huxley used it. Later work largely ruled out acquiring factual content while asleep, while leaving room for simple association and conditioning during sleep. The curious thing is that the novel's version lands on exactly the half science still allows — it insists sleep cannot teach knowledge, only preference.

The real device: abolishing the reason to rebel

If one sentence has to carry this book's invention, it is: it does not repress desire, it manufactures desire and then satisfies it completely. The other half follows: it does not fit the position to the person, it fits the person to the position — first there is dirty work somebody must do, then people are made who enjoy it.

The Party in Nineteen Eighty-Four knows it is not loved, and needs surveillance, torture and rewritten history. This world does not need love at all: it knows what people will want before they do, because it put the wanting there. So rebellion has no fulcrum. You cannot raise a revolution among people who are not suffering, and when John throws the soma out of the window the Deltas answer with fury, not gratitude. The word freedom can only be understood by someone who has first felt unfree.

Which is why the book's three heretics were not produced by oppression: Bernard by a physical defect, Helmholtz by surplus ability, Mond by having read too much. The machine only malfunctions outside its own tolerances, never among the people it grinds.

What it makes you think about

QUESTION ONE
If happiness has been installed, is it still happiness — and why should "real" outrank "feels good"?

Take Mond's side first, since it is usually taken too weakly. The world he administers has no war, no famine, no plague, no unemployment, nobody dying alone. That is not a promise, it is the novel's given fact. He has solved, at a stroke, the overwhelming majority of human suffering, and every objection to him comes down to a preference: we want our pleasures to have the right provenance. Ranking provenance above the non-suffering of billions is a position that needs an argument, not a self-evident premise. Sharper still: everyone who sides with John is speaking after dinner.

Now take John's side at full strength. His claim is not the cheap one that suffering is valuable. It is colder: if the desire being satisfied was installed by somebody else, the one being satisfied is not you. Decide in advance what a person should want, then supply it in abundance, and the sentence "he got what he wanted" has lost its subject; calling someone happy requires that there be a someone, and here the someone is a product specification. In 1974 the philosopher Robert Nozick proposed a thought experiment: a machine that could give you any experience you liked, indistinguishable from the real thing — would you plug in for life? Most people say no. So we want more than experience; we want the things to be happening, and to be the ones doing them.

None of which is abstract now. Antidepressants, recommendation feeds, the reward schedules engineered into games, tastes cultivated by algorithms — each improves the experience, and each helps arrange what you want. The question was never whether to use them, but this: when you say "this is what I like", can you tell whether the liking grew or was installed? Huxley's answer is cold — the person it was installed in cannot tell from the inside.

QUESTION TWO
"The right to be unhappy" — human dignity, or a pose only the fortunate can afford?

John's inventory — ageing, disease, dread, hunger, lice — has been quoted for ninety years as the book's victory speech. Its holes are worth opening up.

The strongest case against him: he is demanding suffering on other people's behalf. He has known exclusion and hunger, but he has never raised five children or lost a family to an epidemic. People who have actually had cancer do not describe it as a right. Suffering preserved in the name of ennoblement has, historically, almost always been preserved by those exempt from it.

The strongest case for him needs restating to work: what he wants is not misery but the standing not to be decided for. Choosing to carry something and being relieved of it by someone else are different in kind; a person may choose the harder thing — a child, an unpromising love, work in a rougher place — and once that choice is removed for their own good, the comfort left over is husbandry. Mond's translation of the demand into "the right to be unhappy" is an elegant substitution: render "let me choose" as "he wants to suffer" and it becomes absurd — a translation performed daily in ordinary life. You always have some easier option available, and something more troublesome you cannot explain wanting; defending the second is hopeless work, because the reward lives inside the process, and a sufficiently clever system can always prove you chose wrong.

QUESTION THREE
Orwell's boot or Huxley's sweet — which should frighten us more today?

In 1985 Neil Postman set the two books side by side in Amusing Ourselves to Death: Orwell feared those who would ban books, Huxley feared there would be no one who wanted to read one; Orwell feared truth concealed, Huxley feared truth drowned in irrelevance. The formulation is so quotable it has become a way of not thinking. An honest version takes both sides apart.

The strongest case for Orwell: the boot never left. Coercive states are not in short supply, and the cost of surveillance keeps falling, while Huxley's model carries a demanding precondition — it must be rich first. Without general affluence and cheap pleasure the machinery cannot run at all, which makes "amusing ourselves to death" a luxury worry for a large share of the world.

The strongest case for Huxley: the two are not alternatives, they stack, and when they stack the sweet does the heavy lifting. Sweets for the many, boots for the few who refuse the sweets — a combination far cheaper and far more stable than force alone. Worse, it offers no identifiable perpetrator: censorship can be opposed, but who exactly do you oppose in a system that keeps you scrolling voluntarily until three in the morning? Oppression at least leaves you something to resist; satisfaction does not even provide an object. So the better question is not which book is more accurate but: in your own day, is it fear or comfort that makes you drop the thing more worth doing?

Motifs: what Huxley kept writing about

Line his books up and the same problem is assembled and disassembled repeatedly: can a person be lucid and happy at the same time? He gave several different answers across a lifetime, and was not afraid to contradict himself.

The early novels — Crome Yellow, Point Counter Point — put clever people in country houses saying brilliant things at one another, none of them saving anybody; he doubted early that intelligence solves anything by itself. Brave New World pushes the doubt to the scale of a society: a world designed by extremely clever people can be technically flawless and humanly empty.

The second half of his life turned elsewhere. He moved to California in 1937; The Perennial Philosophy (1945) traced the mystical experience common to the great religious traditions; The Doors of Perception (1954) recorded his mescaline experiment and argued that some drugs might open perception rather than close it. The man who invented soma later defended psychedelics — the most honest and most mockable episode in his life. In his defence, the distinction is clear enough: soma exists so that people will stop looking, mescaline (on his account) so that they see more. Against him, it is equally easy: how would anyone know they were not rationalising their own soma?

In 1958 he published Brave New World Revisited, a non-fiction reckoning whose conclusion was that his timetable had been too conservative — population growth, over-organisation, propaganda technique and drugs were moving the world toward his fiction faster than he had guessed. In 1962, the year before his death, came his last novel, Island: a genuinely good society that also uses drugs and psychological technique, but to keep people lucid and looking after one another — the mirror image of Brave New World, identical components, opposite direction. It ends with the island seized by force from outside.

And one thing he conceded himself. In a foreword written for the 1946 reissue he called the book's great defect the fact that John is offered only two options — the lunatic utopia or the superstition of the pueblo — and said that if he rewrote it he would supply a third: a sane community, neither savage nor insane. Island is that missing third. In the same foreword he left the judgement that has been quoted ever since: the genuinely efficient total state needs no coercion, because its people "love their servitude."

Criticism and controversy

Without flattery: the book has real defects, and most of the criticism lands.

  • The characters are carriers for positions. Bernard, Helmholtz and Mond each hold a stance; Lenina has almost no interior (even her most human moment, falling in love, is shown only from outside); John is a moral machine that quotes Shakespeare. Huxley had already mocked the novel of ideas in an earlier book — the writer of ideas can only depict people who talk in ideas. Every symptom is present here.
  • The women. Women in the novel are presented chiefly as bodies and compliance; Lenina's desire is arranged for her and laughed at, and after she is struck the book loses interest in what becomes of her. The defence is that this is precisely the object of the satire, and a society that treats women as public utilities ought to read as ugly. The defence has limits: the male characters at least get ideas and change.
  • The shadow of eugenics. Huxley had a documented interest in eugenics; he was a member of the British Eugenics Society and delivered its Galton Lecture late in life. The novel satirises caste, yet many of its premises — that intelligence is gradable, that population quality is manageable — share a vocabulary with the eugenic discourse of the period. Those caste passages are hard to read as pure satire today.
  • The Reservation. The word "savage", the use of Native American ritual as an exhibit of primitiveness, and the decision that the one spiritually deep person on that land is a fair-haired white boy: the arrangement carries obvious period limits and a certain arrogance about the other as moral instrument. The feely playing in London likewise hinges on a black character abducting a blonde Beta; even if the target is popular taste, the racial imagination is of its time.
  • The relationship to We. Zamyatin's novel came eight years earlier and also gives us numbered people and abolished privacy. Reviewing We in 1946, Orwell judged that Huxley's book must have been partly derived from it; Huxley denied it, saying he learned of the book only afterwards. The case is unresolved, and perhaps the fair conclusion is that several such books appearing at once says something about what they were all answering.
  • The prophecy scorecard. It called consumerism, the entertainment industry, mood management by drug and technocratic government. It also got a good deal wrong: it assumed a single world government and a unified production system, and barely anticipated information technology or the individualisation of media. Read as prophecy it will disappoint from both directions: it is a satire published in 1932, aimed at Ford, Hollywood, the planning enthusiasms of the day and Britain's own class system.
  • Banned-book history. The novel has appeared for decades on challenge and banned-book lists in Britain and the United States, usually for sex, drugs and irreligion — a verifiable irony, given that its thesis is that no one need ban books nobody wants to read.

Ten sentences to take away

① The terror here is not that people suffer but that nobody on earth reports being unhappy — the victim list is empty and the invoice is not.

② The invention is one sentence long: don't repress desire, manufacture it and then satisfy it fully; after that, rebellion has no fulcrum.

③ Sleep-teaching can't convey science, only evaluations — the most prophetic line in the book, and the line every recommendation engine works on.

④ The babies shocked into fearing books and roses grow into the twins remarking on a corpse; there are no villains here, only a process and its output.

⑤ Mond is no tyrant; he is the best-read man in the book, and what he keeps locked in his safe is exactly what he forbids everyone else.

⑥ "You can't have stability and Othello both" — tragedy needs instability, and that is the most honest price this world ever quotes.

⑦ "I'm claiming the right to be unhappy" sounds heroic, but what it really claims is the standing not to be decided for; Mond's translation is what makes it absurd.

⑧ One whip: liturgy in the Reservation, self-mutilation in London, live entertainment at the end — an act means whatever the world around it allows it to mean.

⑨ John's tragedy is not that he is crushed but that he is consumed: this world need not kill him, only film him.

⑩ What stays is not the assembly line but the slowly turning feet, and an ending with no adjective in it.
