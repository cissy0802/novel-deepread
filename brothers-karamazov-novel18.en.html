The Brothers Karamazov

Fyodor Dostoevsky · serialized in The Russian Messenger, 1879–1880 · book edition 1880

中文 →

A September night. A man climbs into his father's garden and crouches behind the soaking bushes. The house is lit; the old man leans out of the window and peers into the dark, waiting for a woman. In the son's hand is a brass pestle.
Then he straightens up, turns, and runs.
The old man dies anyway, his skull broken open, the envelope that held the money lying empty on the floor. For the next six hundred pages this novel asks one question: who went in through that door?

This page contains complete spoilers — who actually kills Fyodor Pavlovich, why Smerdyakov hangs himself, what Katerina Ivanovna produces in court, how many years Dmitri gets, and what a crowd of boys shouts at a child's funeral in the last line. This site works on one conviction: everything worth discussing sits on the far side of the ending, and without the ending you can only discuss atmosphere. If you want to keep the suspense, go read the novel first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

An old landowner who has never raised a single one of his children is ringed by his sons: the eldest threatens to kill him over an inheritance and a woman, the second argues that without immortality everything is permitted, the illegitimate one understands that sentence and actually does it, and the youngest is in a monastery learning to love everybody; the night the old man dies three thousand roubles vanish, and the court sends to Siberia the one son least able to defend himself — a murder everyone had a hand in, one man committed, and a third man was convicted of.

Coordinates

Fyodor Dostoevsky (1821–1881) was sentenced to death at twenty-eight for belonging to a circle that read banned books, reprieved on the scaffold itself, and sent to four years of hard labour in Siberia. He lived with epilepsy, gambling debts and advances against unwritten pages, and wrote Crime and Punishment, The Idiot and Demons.

The Brothers Karamazov is his last novel, serialized through 1879–1880 and published as a book at the end of 1880; he died two or three months later. The line in his own preface — that this is only the first novel, and the main one is the second — is not a rhetorical flourish: he really did intend to write the grown-up Alyosha, and ran out of time. Its place in literary history is easy to name: it takes a domestic murder case and turns it into a public trial of God, freedom and responsibility — and hires the best available lawyer for every side.

The story

Act one · A scene at the monastery

A provincial Russian town. Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov, fifty-five, has scraped together money from a tavern, from moneylending, and from swallowing the dowries of two wives. He is lecherous, greedy, and above all a specialist in public disgrace — the more humiliating he makes himself, the more he enjoys it, because embarrassment leaves everyone at a loss, and people at a loss cannot touch him.

He has three sons and has raised none of them. The eldest, Dmitri (Mitya), is the child of the first wife; when she ran off, the old man simply forgot the boy in the yard, and the servant Grigory picked him up and brought him up. Ivan and Alyosha are the second wife's sons; she died young and they too were passed between relatives and strangers. There is a fourth man in the house: the cook Smerdyakov, whom the whole town believes to be Fyodor Pavlovich's bastard by a deaf-mute vagrant girl known as Stinking Lizaveta — and who was also raised by Grigory. One servant brought up two of this father's sons.

The book opens with the family assembling at a monastery outside town to have Elder Zosima settle a dispute about money. (The eldership, in Russian starets: a monk surrenders his own will entirely to an elder and gets, in return, someone qualified to judge for him.) Dmitri believes his father has swallowed his late mother's estate; his father says it was paid out long ago. The mediation fails within a minute: the old man plays the clown in front of the icons and the monks, tells filthy stories, mocks his own son; Dmitri storms in shouting; and the landowner Miusov casually repeats a proposition of Ivan's — that if men do not believe in the immortality of the soul, morality has no foundation and everything is permitted, cannibalism included. setup ① Ivan is present. He does not deny it.

In the middle of this embarrassment Zosima suddenly rises, walks over to Dmitri, kneels, and touches his forehead to the floor in a full bow. setup ② The room is stunned. The elder explains nothing and goes back to his cell.

Act two · Three thousand roubles and an amulet round a neck

Dmitri is twenty-eight, a discharged army officer, capable of dropping from the vilest thing imaginable to the most generous and back within an hour. He is engaged to Katerina Ivanovna (Katya): her father had embezzled public funds and faced ruin, and Dmitri was the one who put up the money — but first he made the proud girl come to his room alone, so she would have to bow her head. He never touched her; she bowed and left. Later she came into an inheritance, became his fiancée in turn, and treated him as benefactor and obligation. From day one this was not love but a contest over who is nobler, and the loser pays.

Then he met Grushenka — a woman who had been thrown over once, had saved a little money lending it out, and had no illusions left about men. She dangles father and son simultaneously and watches them tear at each other. The old man has three thousand roubles ready in an envelope, tied with a red ribbon and marked with her name: if she comes, the money is hers.

Here is Dmitri's disgrace. Katya once entrusted him with three thousand roubles to send to a relative, and he spent it. But he insists, over and over, that he spent only half. The other fifteen hundred he sewed into a little cloth bag and wore round his neck against his skin, so that he could prove he was a thief but not a scoundrel. setup ③ To anyone else the distinction is meaningless; to him it is the last standing column of his personality. He runs all over the district trying to raise the three thousand, because until the debt is paid he has no right to go after Grushenka.

Act three · Rebellion, and the Grand Inquisitor

Meanwhile the second son drags the novel up to another altitude. Ivan meets Alyosha in a tavern and begins by clarifying his position: it is not that he denies God — he accepts God; what he refuses to accept is the world God made. Then come the examples, all collected from newspapers and court records, all of them about children: a little girl locked out all night in a freezing privy by her own parents, beating her chest and praying to "dear kind God" to help her; a general who has an eight-year-old serf boy torn to pieces by hounds in front of the boy's mother because a stone the child threw hurt the paw of a favourite dog.

The argument runs like this. Perhaps at the end there really will be a final harmony, and on that day the victim will forgive the torturer and every tear will be explained as worth it. But nobody has standing to forgive on that child's behalf. The mother may forgive her own suffering; she has no right to forgive her son's. If the ticket to universal harmony is priced at one child's tears, the ticket costs too much — so Ivan does not deny God, he most respectfully returns the ticket.

Then he recites a "poem" of his own invention, The Grand Inquisitor. Sixteenth-century Seville; the Inquisition burned nearly a hundred heretics the day before. Christ returns. People recognize him, follow him, and he restores a blind man's sight. The ninety-year-old Cardinal Inquisitor passes by, lifts a finger, and the guards take him to a dungeon.

At night the old man comes in with a lamp and talks for the whole night. His indictment: out in the wilderness you rejected the devil's three temptations — you would not turn stones into bread, you would not throw yourself from the temple to prove yourself by miracle, you would not take the kingdoms of the earth — because you wanted men to believe in you freely, not to buy their faith. But you overestimated them. Freedom is too heavy. What a man wants is someone to kneel to, someone to hand his conscience over to, someone to tell him what is right. So for fifteen hundred years we have done the job for you: we took the devil's three gifts — bread, mystery, authority — and with them we let hundreds of millions live quietly like children and die happily. We have lied, we carry the lie, and it is we who love mankind. You have come back only to ruin the one thing that was ever done for them. So tomorrow I shall burn you.

Christ says nothing at all. When the old man has finished, he goes to him and kisses him on his bloodless lips. The Inquisitor shudders, opens the door of the cell and says:

"Go, and come no more."— the Grand Inquisitor to his prisoner (Book V, sense of the line)

Alyosha has no answer at all. He gets up and kisses Ivan — handing his brother's own gesture straight back to him.

All this time Ivan is also talking to somebody else: the cook Smerdyakov. Epileptic, sullen, self-taught, contemptuous of everyone and worshipful of Ivan, he keeps circling the same subject — the old man has money, Dmitri has lost his mind, anything could happen. Once he advises Ivan not to go to Moscow but to attend to some business nearby, at Chermashnya. The next day Ivan does leave — for Chermashnya. setup ④ Climbing into the carriage, he feels a disgust with himself he cannot name.

Act four · The elder dies, and the body begins to stink

On another line, Zosima finishes telling his life and dies. That biography is the novel's affirmative answer. As a young officer he was arrogant enough to challenge a man to a duel over a woman; the night before, he remembered his brother Markel, who died as a boy and who in his last days became strangely luminous and said a thing that comes to roughly this: every one of us is guilty before all, and he most of all. setup ⑤ Next morning Zosima let his opponent fire first, threw his own pistol into the trees, asked forgiveness, resigned his commission and entered a monastery.

When the elder dies the whole town comes for a miracle: by popular expectation the body of a saint should not decay, it should give off a sweet odour. Within a day it stinks powerfully instead. Father Ferapont, the ascetic rival, comes out to announce a judgment of God, and yesterday's saint is today's joke. Alyosha's faith collapses that day — not because he doubts God, but because he feels God has been unjust to the man he loves most.

Rakitin, a careerist seminarian, walks him over to Grushenka's, expecting to enjoy the spectacle of a saint's fall. But when Grushenka hears the elder is dead she climbs off Alyosha's lap and crosses herself, and tells him the tale of the wicked woman and the onion: in her whole life she gave away a single onion, so an angel held that onion down into the fire to pull her out; she was almost free when the other sinners caught at her legs, and she kicked at them shouting that the onion was hers — the stem snapped and she fell back. Two people who came to damage each other end up saving each other a little. That night Alyosha dreams of the wedding at Cana; Zosima crosses the room to him and says, come, you have been invited too. He wakes, runs into the yard, throws himself down and holds the earth and weeps — and gets up a settled man.

Act five · That night: a pestle, a wall, and a carriage to Mokroye

This is the day Dmitri goes out of his mind. He must have three thousand roubles or he is a thief; he begs everywhere and gets nothing. In the evening, convinced Grushenka has gone to his father, he snatches up a brass pestle and climbs into the garden.

He sees the old man at the window — neck craned, peering, waiting for a woman, the man he hates most on earth. He said afterwards, again and again, that he stood there gripping the pestle meaning to bring it down and something stopped him; he called it God watching over him. He drops from the window ledge and runs. Old Grigory catches him by the leg shouting "parricide!", and Dmitri swings the pestle down on the old servant's head and Grigory goes down, his face covered in blood. Dmitri jumps the wall, then comes back, wipes the blood with his handkerchief and feels for breath — then runs.

He learns that Grushenka has gone to the Polish officer who abandoned her five years ago, at the village of Mokroye, some twenty miles off. He decides to look at her one last time and then shoot himself, and sets out at night with a fistful of banknotes and crates of champagne. At Mokroye the Pole shows what he is: he offers, for three thousand roubles, to hand Grushenka over. She sees in one moment what she waited five years for, turns to Dmitri and tells him he is the one she loves. What follows is the most dissolute and most moving night in the book: two people, neither of them clean, deciding before dawn to begin their lives again together.

At daybreak the police walk in. Fyodor Pavlovich was found in his study with his skull broken; the three thousand roubles are gone and the red-ribboned envelope lies empty on the floor. Every piece of evidence points at Dmitri: he had threatened to kill his father, he was in the garden that night, he wounded the servant at the gate, he was penniless the day before, and tonight he is scattering money in Mokroye.

The preliminary investigation is one of the cruellest comedies in the novel. They make him undress to be searched, and he stands barefoot in front of a room of officials, mortified first by his dirty socks and then, loudly, by a deformed big toenail — a man who has just lost his father, his freedom and his one good night. He answers everything honestly except where the money came from. He would rather be taken for a murderer than admit the bag round his neck: confessing that he sewed up Katya's fifteen hundred and carried it about for a month strikes him as more shameful than killing. By the time he does say it, not one person believes him.

Act six · Who killed him: three conversations and a trial

Ivan comes back from Chermashnya and visits Smerdyakov three times. Twice the man lies half-propped in bed, prodding him with hints, watching him come apart. The third time he pulls a bundle of notes out of his stocking and lays it on the table: three thousand roubles, all of it.

He describes the whole thing. The epileptic fit was faked, so that he would be safely unconscious when it happened. He had already taught Dmitri the signal knocks — three, a pause, two — meaning Grushenka has come, which would bring the old man to the door. When Dmitri went back over the wall, Smerdyakov got up, knocked the signal, was let in by Fyodor Pavlovich himself, killed him with a cast-iron paperweight, took the money and dropped the envelope on the floor — a torn envelope left at the scene looks far more like Dmitri than a simple absence of cash.

Then he says the sentence Ivan will never get out from under: you taught me this. You said everything is permitted. payoff ① ↑ The line that was passed around a monastery reception room as a salon paradox, which Ivan himself never quite meant, has been picked up by a cook and used as a set of working drawings. Ivan is the theorist; Smerdyakov is the only person who ever took his theory seriously. And besides, Smerdyakov says, you knew what was coming and you left anyway; your going to Chermashnya payoff ④ ↑ was your consent. What looked like a last-minute change of travel plans turns out to be the one act of authorization in the book — and because it was never put into words, it can never be tried.

Ivan takes the money and says he will tell the whole court tomorrow. That night he runs a fever and spends hours talking to a shabby gentleman in an old frock coat with chilblains on his fingers — a devil who knows exactly where to press: you want to confess, do you? What you actually want is to be called a man of noble character tomorrow. You are not even sure you disbelieve in me. Ivan throws a glass at him. There is a knock: Alyosha, with the news that Smerdyakov has just hanged himself. The murderer is dead, the money is in Ivan's hands, and the only witness is gone.

The whole trial turns on one piece of arithmetic: Dmitri scattered nearly three thousand roubles at Mokroye and had nothing the day before, so the money can only have come from his father's envelope. His answer is that it was not three thousand but fifteen hundred, out of the bag round his neck — Katya's money. payoff ③ ↑ To protect a distinction nobody but himself cared about — thief, yes, but not scoundrel — he concealed that bag for a month, and so dismantled with his own hands the only chain of evidence that could have saved him. Nobody ever saw the bag; nobody can testify to it. The scrap of dignity he cared about most is precisely the thing that sends him to the mines.

The prosecutor presents him as the inevitable product of Karamazov animality. Fetyukovich, the celebrated defence counsel brought in from Petersburg, takes the physical evidence apart item by item, shows that it fits innocence just as well, and leaves behind a famous line about psychology being a stick with two ends. Then he plays a dangerous card and asks the jury: did the man lying in that blood deserve to be called a father? Where does a son's obligation come from, to a man who begot him and never raised him?

Ivan drags himself into court with a fever, throws the money down, says the murderer was Smerdyakov and that he himself incited it, and adds that the devil was sitting beside him a moment ago — the judge declares the witness deranged and has him carried out. Then Katya rises. Her testimony until then had been protecting Dmitri; but seeing Ivan destroy himself for his brother, she produces a letter Dmitri wrote her two days earlier while drunk, saying that if he could not borrow the money he would kill his father and take the three thousand from under his pillow. She saves the man she loves and ruins the man who owes her a bow. That drunken scrawl becomes the most convincing evidence in the case. The peasant jurors deliberate under an hour: guilty, twenty years of hard labour. The book that contains the verdict is titled "A Judicial Error" — the title is itself the judgment.

One more line closes at the same time. Ilyusha, the son of a poor retired captain whom Dmitri once dragged out of a tavern by the beard in public, has been tormented by his schoolfellows over it and falls fatally ill; Alyosha and the schoolboy Kolya keep a circle of boys around his bed. Ilyusha dies. After the funeral Alyosha gathers the boys by a stone and tells them: one day you will be grown men, you will do bad things, you will become hard; but if you keep one good memory from childhood, even one, it may be the thing that saves you. The boys shout hurrah, and Kolya shouts:

"Hurrah for Karamazov!"— the last line of the novel (Epilogue, at Ilyusha's funeral; sense of the line)

And in prison Dmitri has a dream. He is driving past a village burnt to the ground; at the roadside stands a peasant woman with a baby in her arms, the child blue with cold and crying without stopping. In the dream he wants desperately to know — why is the babe crying? why are people poor? why does nobody pick that child up? He wakes calm, and says he is willing to go for the babe; that everyone is guilty before everyone, and somebody has to go for all. payoff ⑤ ↑ The doctrine that came out of Markel's mouth and was passed on by Zosima — the one that sounded like monastery decoration — is said here for the first time by a man who does not go to church, drinks, and has beaten people. It is not argued into him; it is forced out of him by a dream. The novel's answer to Ivan does not finally live in the elder's biography. It lives in his brother's dream.

Zosima's bow is paid off here too: he told Alyosha afterwards that in touching his forehead to the floor before Dmitri he was bowing to the enormous suffering that was coming. payoff ② ↑ The verdict of the whole novel is placed in Book II and written as a gesture nobody can read — the first time through it looks like an eccentricity; the second time it is a spoiler. Nothing ends cleanly: the family raises money to get Dmitri off the convoy and away to America, and he agrees and is tormented by agreeing, because a man who escapes is not suffering for everyone, only escaping; Ivan lies in a fever nobody can promise he will survive; Katya comes to the prison and she and Dmitri ask each other's forgiveness, both knowing the forgiveness is false. The book settles no one's account.

Put the six acts together and there is only one fuse, and it is not the pestle: a man fathered four sons and raised none of them. What Dmitri fights his father for was never three thousand roubles but proof that he was owed something; Ivan's atheism grows in an empty house with no father in it and therefore no authority worth trusting; Smerdyakov's resentment comes of being simultaneously this family's blood and this family's servant; Alyosha spends the novel looking for a father to kneel to, and finds Zosima. Four characters are four ways of having been abandoned. Which is why the murder becomes a problem everybody has a share in — one hand did it; four hearts wanted it done.

Two slow-motion replays

1 · The girl in the privy: compressing theology into one specific sound of crying

The reason nobody has really refuted Ivan's rebellion in a century and a half is that he does not argue about evil in general — he argues only about children. Adults have eaten the apple and have their own accounts to settle; you can at least claim they deserve what they get. A child cannot. Ivan shrinks the whole field of theodicy — the question of why an all-good, all-powerful God permits evil — down to one five-year-old, where no defence can stand up. What makes it deadlier still is his material: case files and newspaper clippings, read out one after another like a prosecutor entering evidence. Dostoevsky refuses to let him be lyrical, because lyricism would give the reader somewhere to breathe. The most terrifying indictments are always delivered in the flattest voice.

2 · The kiss: how silence beat a whole night of eloquence

The chapter is grotesquely lopsided: one man talks all night, the other never says a word, and by ordinary logic that should be a rout. But Dostoevsky has seen one thing clearly — the Inquisitor has prepared for every argument and has not prepared for tenderness. The kiss refuses to enter his language (to start arguing would concede that this is a sum that can be worked out); it converts him from a position back into a man — an old man who has carried a lie for humanity his whole life and has never once been pitied; and it hands the initiative back to him, so that he opens the door and lets the prisoner go, undoing his own night of reasoning with a single action. The echo comes a page later, when Alyosha, with no rebuttal in him, gets up and kisses his brother. Faced with someone you cannot out-argue, the only move is not to argue, and not to leave.

The machine inside the book

1 · Polyphony: the author gives the best lawyer to the other side

The Russian scholar Mikhail Bakhtin named Dostoevsky's method polyphony: not a single author narrating a cast of characters, but several equal consciousnesses, none able to overpower the others, speaking at once, with the author declining to stand above them as judge. There is an easy test. Publish The Grand Inquisitor on its own and it reads as a nearly irrefutable anti-Christian manifesto — written by a devout Orthodox believer. He handed the thing he valued most to the strongest possible opponent and forbade the opponent to go easy. Which is why both camps still quote the book: atheists take Ivan for a programme, believers take Zosima for a doctrine.

2 · A detective novel's chassis: the reader knows, the court doesn't

Structurally this is a strict crime novel — murder, inheritance, a love triangle, a manufactured alibi, a trial. Dostoevsky borrowed the most popular commercial form of his day and turned the mechanism around: an ordinary detective story has the reader hunt the killer alongside the detective; this one tells the reader who did it early, then forces him to watch a complete judicial process arrive, by impeccable logic at every step, at the wrong conclusion. Nothing the prosecutor argues is absurd, and nothing the defence argues is absurd either; the drunken letter, the empty envelope, the money of unexplained origin all point honestly at the same innocent man. The novel's deepest suspicion of justice is not that judges are wicked, but that everything can be done correctly and come out wrong.

What it makes you think about

Question one
If the ticket to "it was all worth it" has to be bought with one child's crying, do you return it?

Take Ivan's side at its strongest first. His claim is not that God does not exist; it is a claim about cost and authorization. Any calculation that cancels present suffering against future good requires the sufferer's own signature, and the girl frozen in that outhouse can never sign. When somebody else pronounces it worth it, he is forgiving on her behalf — spending money that is not his. The objection is frighteningly clean: it denies no metaphysics, it insists on one rule only. You cannot settle a victim's account for her.

The hardest reply is not "have faith". It is: you cannot afford to return the ticket either. What Ivan hands back is not merely God's world but every future that costs anything — and he goes on eating, writing, loving a woman, and admits himself that he loves life more than the meaning of it. A man who makes "I do not accept" his final position has turned himself into a spectator, and Smerdyakov read consent in exactly that spectatorship. Refusing to sign for the world does not get you out of it; it only gets you out of carrying any of it. Zosima's side offers not a counter-argument but a change of role: stop being the auditor, be the debtor.

Bring it home. Every "that cost is acceptable" — a road that takes out a row of houses, a policy that must sacrifice some group, a restructuring that lets go of everyone over forty-five — uses the arithmetic Ivan is indicting, and a society that refuses to do the arithmetic at all cannot function. The question is not whether to calculate, but whether, once you have, you allow yourself never to think of the person you counted again.

Question two
The Inquisitor says people do not want freedom, only bread and someone to decide for them. Is he wrong?

State his case better than his supporters do. He is not a tyrant; he is a man who believes he has given up heaven and stayed on earth to carry a lie on other people's behalf. His evidence is empirical: for fifteen centuries human beings have handed over freedom for certainty, again and again — to a church, to a doctrine, to a leader, to a clear set of rules. And he is exactly right about the mechanism: the pain of freedom is not choosing, it is being answerable afterwards for what you chose. So his programme is that millions live happily as children while the few tens of thousands of us carry the cost. It is a quantifiable kind of mercy: the suffering it removes can be measured, and what it takes away cannot.

The other side has to be just as hard. First, his premise is self-fulfilling: keep people as children for fifteen hundred years and then cite their childishness as proof they must be kept. Second, he swaps a word — what someone relieved of choice receives is not happiness but peace, and he never separates the two. Third, and this is what the kiss answers: a love that cannot be refused is not love. If a man cannot get it wrong, his getting it right is worth nothing.

Today the question does not wear a cassock. It looks like the feed that decides what you see, the default that has already been selected for you, the interface that reduces a complicated matter to two buttons. All of them mean well, and all of them genuinely make you more comfortable. The Inquisitor never went away; he simply changed clothes. His current phrasing is that we have optimized your experience.

Question three
You did nothing, you only wanted it to happen, and then you left town. Is that murder?

Legally the answer is clear: no. Ivan struck no blow, gave no order, paid nothing, supplied no weapon, made no explicit verbal agreement, and is not even certain he truly wanted his father dead. Criminalizing intention is the first step of every tyranny — once you may be tried for what passed through your head, no one is innocent. And Smerdyakov was an adult who chose, himself, to pick up that paperweight; charging his choice to Ivan's account is precisely a refusal to treat him as a person.

But at its strongest, the other side names what Ivan did in a very modern way: he created the conditions and preserved his deniability. He knew there was powder in that house, he knew who was holding the match, and on the decisive day he left the town — at the suggestion of the man holding the match. He never said "do it", and everything he did was more effective than saying it, because words can be testified to and a business trip cannot. Smerdyakov's sharpest line is simply: you knew, then. Ivan cannot answer it, because he did know, and knowing and choosing to be absent is a form of participation.

Almost everyone has met this at a smaller scale: the manager who never says it outright and only keeps mentioning that someone is difficult to work with; the person who merely laughs while a group piles on; the relative whose position is that he said nothing, the other one decided for himself. The law can only handle what was said out loud. Conscience handles what was deliberately left unsaid. Ivan ends up sentenced by himself — and sentenced harder than the court sentenced his brother.

The obsession Dostoevsky keeps returning to

Line his novels up and the same fixation recurs: what happens to a man once an idea has taken possession of him. In Crime and Punishment Raskolnikov has the thought — that an extraordinary man may step over morality — before he has the axe; in Demons a whole generation is driven to destruction by abstractions. Here, at last, the idea and its holder come apart: the man who thinks it does not act, and the man who acts does not think. He spent a career running one experiment: let a thought out of the study and see what it breaks when it lands.

The other half of the obsession is public humiliation and public confession. His characters love to blurt out the most shameful thing about themselves in the least suitable room: Prince Myshkin in The Idiot, too purely good to survive in the world, tears the mask off every place he enters; and in this novel the same nerve runs from Fyodor Pavlovich clowning in a monastery to Dmitri going to pieces over a pair of dirty socks in front of the investigators. He believed that a man is most truly himself not when he is thinking but when he is disgraced. And none of it is written from the spectators' benches: his last book sends an innocent man to Siberia, and he had walked that road himself.

Criticism, honestly

  • The answer is weaker than the question. The classic complaint: Book V, Ivan's rebellion and the Grand Inquisitor, is written at overwhelming strength, while Book VI, Zosima's life, is mild, homiletic and sentimental — they are not in the same weight class. Dostoevsky did design Book VI as the reply to Book V, but many readers, including very sympathetic ones, come away feeling that the eloquence was outlived rather than answered.
  • The women. Katya and Grushenka are both written as emotional extremes, swinging between the exalted and the vicious, and their reversals usually serve some man's inner development; the sickly, sadistic fantasies given to fourteen-year-old Lise are genuinely disturbing to read now. The defence is that his men are equally vessels for ideas; but the women exist almost exclusively inside their relations to men, and that criticism holds.
  • The author's own positions. Dostoevsky's journalism, above all A Writer's Diary, contains explicit antisemitic writing and a Russian messianism that is on the record; and the few lines in this novel where Lise raises the blood-libel story about Jews crucifying children and Alyosha answers that he does not know have long been considered the most disquieting passage in the book. The two Poles at Mokroye are written as figures of fun. Stated as fact, without a verdict attached.
  • Digression and sentimentality. The Ilyusha strand strikes many readers as too sweet, particularly the speech at the stone; the interpolations, detours and enormous monologues look like loss of control to anyone who values structure. Nabokov, in Lectures on Russian Literature, dismissed Dostoevsky almost entirely as an overrated writer of cheap thrillers — an extreme judgment, but a real one, and representative of a whole line of style-first readers.
  • It is half a book. The preface states that this volume is only the first part of Alyosha's story and that the real novel is the second — which was never written. The hero the book keeps promising never actually takes the lead in it. There is also a widely repeated claim that in the sequel Alyosha would have become a revolutionary, even a regicide; it comes from a friend's recollections, cannot be verified, and should not be taken as fact.
  • Translation. English readers argue between Constance Garnett's old version and the Pevear and Volokhonsky one — the former fluent and elegant but smoothing out the roughness and repetition, the latter deliberately preserving the stumble. Dostoevsky's Russian was never beautiful Russian: it is hurried, repetitive, marked by serial deadlines — and that is his voice. A translation that reads too smoothly is a distortion.

Ten lines to take away

1. This is not a story about who killed the father. It is a story about how much of him each of four sons killed — one hand, four hearts.

2. The fuse is a father who never raised anyone: what Dmitri fights him for is not three thousand roubles but proof that he was owed something.

3. Ivan never denies God. He returns the ticket — the least refutable act of non-cooperation in modern literature.

4. The Grand Inquisitor's argument has one hole: keep people as children for fifteen centuries, then cite their childishness as the reason to keep them.

5. Christ says nothing and kisses him. Against someone you cannot out-argue, the only move is not to argue, and not to leave.

6. Smerdyakov is the one person who took Ivan's theory seriously; an idea's real weight shows only when it lands on the person with the least protection.

7. Going to Chermashnya is the single act of authorization in the book — and because it had no words, it can never be tried.

8. Dmitri can bear being taken for a murderer but not for a cheapskate; the fatal concealment was settled the moment he raged about his dirty socks.

9. The reply to Ivan is not in the elder's biography but in Dmitri's dream: the man who cannot do arithmetic goes, for a baby blue with cold.

10. What stays with you is not the trial. It is a crowd of boys at a stone, shouting hurrah for the surname of a man just sentenced to twenty years.
