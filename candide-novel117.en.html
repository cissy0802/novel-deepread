Candide

Candide, ou l'Optimisme · Voltaire · 1759 · Thirty chapters

The ship is breaking up within sight of Lisbon harbour. A kind Anabaptist has just hauled a sailor back from the rail and been pitched overboard himself; the sailor watches him thrash in the waves and walks away.
A young man moves to jump in after him. His tutor holds him back and, without hurrying, demonstrates that the harbour of Lisbon was formed expressly so that this good man could drown in it.
The proof is not yet finished when the ground beneath them turns over.

This piece contains complete spoilers: who dies and doesn't stay dead, what becomes of the great beauty, and what the last line says. The whole meaning of the book sits in its final chapter: a man taught that "all is for the best" travels through the worst places on earth, and in the end neither refutes the sentence nor accepts it. He goes and works a plot of land. Without that plot of land, there is nothing to discuss.

In one sentence

A guileless young man, schooled to believe that this is the best of all possible worlds, is kicked out of a castle for one kiss and then flung through war, earthquake, the Inquisition, pirates, a slave plantation and the con artists of Paris; after every disaster his tutor's sentence has to be proved all over again, until at last he finds a way of living that no longer needs to argue about it.

Coordinates

Voltaire (born François-Marie Arouet, 1694–1778) was the loudest pen of the French Enlightenment. Candide, or Optimism came out anonymously in 1759, posing as a translation "from the German of Doctor Ralph"; candide in French means innocent, frank, unworldly. It was banned in Geneva and Paris almost at once and reprinted again and again within the year.

Its place on the shelf is the philosophical tale (conte philosophique): short, fast, with characters moved like chess pieces, the whole book one long argument. The target is specific. In his Theodicy (1710) the German philosopher Leibniz argued that God chose the best from among all possible worlds, so that even evil is a necessary part of the optimal whole; Alexander Pope's Essay on Man had put it in a line: "Whatever is, is right." What pushed Voltaire past patience was the Lisbon earthquake of 1 November 1755.

The story

Act I · The castle: the best of all possible castles

In Westphalia stands the castle of the Baron of Thunder-ten-tronckh, which counts as magnificent in those parts: it has a door, and its windows have glass. The Baroness weighs some three hundred and fifty pounds and is respected accordingly. Living there is a gentle, simple-hearted young man called Candide. The old servants suspect he is the son of the Baron's sister and a decent local gentleman whom she always refused to marry, Setup ①because he could prove only seventy-one quarterings of nobility, the rest of his family tree having been lost to time.

The household tutor, Pangloss (roughly "all tongue" in Greek), teaches a subject Voltaire names "metaphysico-theologo-cosmolonigology", with a "nigaud", a dunce, buried in the middle. His great skill is proof: there is no effect without a cause, and in this best of all possible worlds the Baron's castle is the best of castles; noses were made to carry spectacles, and so we have spectacles; legs were plainly designed for breeches, and so we have breeches. Pangloss doesn't explain the world. He issues it a certificate of approval after the fact. Candide believes him with his whole heart.

The Baron's daughter Cunégonde is seventeen, rosy and appetising. One day, in a little wood known as "the park", she spots Dr Pangloss in the bushes giving a lesson in experimental physics to her mother's chambermaid Paquette, Setup ②a pretty brunette and a very willing pupil. Cunégonde grasps the chain of cause and effect and goes home thinking that she and Candide might be each other's sufficient reason. Next day, behind a screen, she drops her handkerchief, he picks it up, hands touch, lips touch. The Baron happens by and drives Candide out of the castle with great kicks to the backside. Voltaire's phrase for it is that he has been expelled from an "earthly paradise". Setup ③The scene is deliberately cut on the pattern of Genesis: a garden that thinks itself perfect, a forbidden kiss, an exile.

Act II · The world's first lesson: war, charity and a rotting nose

Half-starved, Candide trudges through the snow to a small town, where two men in blue buy him dinner and ask whether he loves the King of the Bulgars. Of course, he says, and with that he is a Bulgar soldier (the Bulgars stand in, roughly, for the Prussians; the Seven Years' War is on). He takes it into his head to go for a walk, and under military law must run the gauntlet between two thousand men. After two runs his back is raw to the bone; the King, passing by, learns that this is a metaphysician ignorant of the world and pardons him.

Then comes a great battle, and Voltaire narrates it like a calculating machine of terrifying calm: the cannon first lay low about six thousand men on each side, then the musketry rids the best of worlds of nine or ten thousand rogues who were infesting its surface. He calls it "heroic butchery". Candide hides among the corpses and escapes to Holland.

There, an orator fresh from an hour-long speech on charity sends him packing because he can't say whether the Pope is the Antichrist, and the orator's wife empties a chamber pot on his head from the window. The man who saves him is Jacques, an Anabaptist, the first truly good person in the book, and Voltaire makes him a member of a sect the established churches despised.

Soon a beggar latches on to Candide in the street: covered in sores, the tip of his nose eaten away, coughing up a tooth with every spasm. It is Pangloss. His news: the castle was razed, the Baron's family butchered, Cunégonde raped and disembowelled. As for his own condition, it is syphilis, caught from Paquette, and traced back link by link to one of Columbus's companions. Pangloss judges this necessary too: had Columbus not brought the disease back from America, we would have neither chocolate nor cochineal.

Jacques pays for the cure, which costs Pangloss only an eye and an ear, and the three of them take ship for Lisbon.

Act III · Lisbon: the earthquake, the noose, and a woman come back to life

That is the opening scene. Jacques drowns; the sailor he saved swims ashore. They have barely entered the city when the earth shakes and thirty thousand inhabitants are crushed beneath the ruins. Candide lies in the rubble begging for a little wine and oil while Pangloss proves to him that the earthquake was inevitable. The sailor goes through the dead for money, gets drunk and buys a girl.

The wise men of Portugal hit on the most effective means of preventing a repeat: an auto-da-fé, the public burning of a few people over a slow fire. The catch includes a man who married his godmother, two Portuguese who removed the bacon from their chicken (meaning secret Jews), Pangloss for having spoken, and Candide for having listened with an air of approval. The burnings are carried out, Pangloss is hanged, and Candide is flogged in time with the chanting. That same day the earth shakes again, with a tremendous crash.

Bleeding, Candide asks the book's question for the first time: if this is the best of all possible worlds, what are the others like? An old woman leads him away and dresses his wounds, and a few days later brings him to a veiled lady. It is Cunégonde.

She was indeed raped by a Bulgar and stabbed in the side, but she didn't die; a captain kept her and later sold her to a Jew, Don Issachar. The Grand Inquisitor wanted her too, and the two men agreed to share: the Jew gets Mondays, Wednesdays and the Sabbath, the Inquisitor the rest of the week. Issachar walks in and Candide runs him through; an hour later the Inquisitor arrives and Candide runs him through as well. The old woman takes charge: grab the jewels, saddle the horses, go.

Act IV · The old woman: half a buttock and a hundred wishes to die

In Cádiz, thanks to the drill he learned among the Bulgars, Candide is made a captain and ships out with a force sent to fight the Jesuits in Paraguay. On board, Cunégonde complains that she is the unhappiest woman alive, and the old woman, who has heard enough, tells her own story.

She was the daughter of a pope and a princess, beautiful enough to drive men mad, and her fiancé was poisoned on their wedding day. After that: pirates, rape, a civil war in Morocco in which her mother was torn to pieces, plague in Algiers, and sale after sale until she reached Constantinople. When the Janissaries holding Azov were besieged by the Russians and ran out of food, they decided not to eat the women outright, only to cut one buttock from each. That is how she lost half her backside.

Then she says the most clear-eyed thing in the book, roughly this: a hundred times I wanted to kill myself, and still I loved life. That ridiculous weakness may be our most fatal inclination, to cling to the very thing that is devouring us. And she adds a dare: Setup ④go and ask every passenger on this ship, and if you find a single one who has not cursed their life, throw me into the sea head first.

They ask. There are no exceptions.

Act V · The New World: an old Baron and a country of gold

In Buenos Aires the governor takes one look at Cunégonde and wants her, and at the same moment the officers hunting the Inquisitor's killer reach the port. The old woman's advice is the practical kind: the lady stays and survives; you, run. Candide flees with his valet Cacambo, a quick-witted man of mixed blood who has been choirboy, sailor, monk and soldier, exactly the worldliness Candide lacks.

They go to offer their services to the Jesuits of Paraguay, a state in which, as Cacambo puts it, the Fathers have everything and the people nothing. The commanding Father who receives them turns out to be Cunégonde's brother, the young Baron, left for dead by the Bulgars and nursed back to life by the Jesuits. The two men embrace in tears.

Until Candide says he means to marry the Baron's sister. The Baron's face changes and he strikes Candide with the flat of his sword: how dare you aspire to a woman of seventy-two quarterings! Payoff ① ↑In Act I, Candide's mother refused his father for having only seventy-one, and here the joke grows teeth: pedigree is the reason Candide's birth is a whispered rumour, and now it stands between him and Cunégonde. The missing generation is, precisely, his father. Candide runs the Baron through and he falls in his blood. Candide weeps: I am the best man in the world, and I have already killed three men, two of them priests.

Wearing Jesuit robes, they flee into the forest, where a local tribe nearly cooks them as Jesuits; Cacambo saves them by explaining that his master has just killed one.

With food gone and horses dead, they let a canoe carry them down a river that runs under a mountain, and come out into a plain walled in by cliffs: Eldorado. The village children play quoits with discs of gold, emerald and ruby, and leave them lying in the road when school starts. When the travellers pay for dinner with gold they picked up, the innkeeper laughs till he can barely stand: that's pebbles from the road, and travellers are fed at the government's expense.

A man of a hundred and seventy-two explains that here everyone is a priest: they thank God every morning and never ask Him for anything, since they already have all they need. There are no law courts and no prisons, but there is a palace of science; the King receives guests with an embrace and a kiss on each cheek. It is the only place in the book where all really is well, and it happens not to be on any map.

After a month Candide grows restless and does the arithmetic. If we stay, we're just like everyone else; if we go back with a dozen sheep loaded with these pebbles, we'll be richer than all the kings put together, and we can buy Cunégonde back. The King tells him his country is nothing much, Setup ⑤but when one is reasonably well off somewhere, one should stay there (in substance). He also says that keeping strangers against their will would be tyranny, and he won't do it. Three thousand physicists build a machine that hoists the pair, along with a hundred-odd great red sheep loaded with gold and jewels, over the cliffs.

Act VI · The price of sugar: Candide's first "no"

Outside Eldorado the red sheep die one after another, and by the time they reach the Dutch colony of Surinam only two are left. By the roadside outside the town lies a Black man wearing nothing but blue linen drawers, missing his left leg and his right hand. He explains, quite calmly: when you work in the sugar mill and the millstone catches your finger, they cut off the hand; when you try to run away, they cut off a leg. Both things happened to him. In substance: "This is the price at which you eat sugar in Europe" (chapter 19). His mother sold him for ten Spanish crowns and told him it was an honour to be the slave of white masters.

Candide weeps. He gives optimism a definition of his own: the mania of insisting that all is well when things are going badly. It is his first break with Pangloss, and no argument brought it about. A leg and a hand did.

He sends Cacambo with diamonds to buy back Cunégonde, arranging to meet in Venice, then hires a ship and is robbed of his sheep by its Dutch captain, Vanderdendur; when he complains, the judge fines him. He advertises for the unhappiest honest man in the province to travel with him and chooses a poor scholar, Martin, robbed by his wife, beaten by his son, persecuted by the local preachers as a heretic. Martin calls himself a Manichaean: the world is fought over by good and evil, and evil is well ahead.

At sea, they watch Vanderdendur's ship sunk in battle, and one red sheep, Candide's own, comes floating towards them. See, says Candide, crime is sometimes punished. And the passengers who went down with him, says Martin, what had they done? From here on Martin takes over Pangloss's seat, with the sign reversed: one says all is well, the other all is ill, and each can pocket any fact the world produces.

Act VII · The European tour: everything, and nobody happy

In Paris, Candide is fleeced in turn by salons, card sharps and a woman pretending to be Cunégonde. When their ship puts in at Portsmouth, they arrive just in time to see an admiral kneeling on deck and being shot, for not having got close enough to the enemy; in this country, the locals explain, it is good to kill an admiral from time to time "to encourage the others" (chapter 23). It really happened: Admiral John Byng was executed in 1757, and Voltaire had tried to intervene on his behalf.

In Venice there is no Cacambo. Candide sees a monk strolling arm in arm with a girl, both of them radiant, and bets Martin that here at least are two happy people. He invites them to dinner and the girl recognises him: Payoff ② ↑she is Paquette. The lesson in the bushes in chapter 1 and that link in the syphilis chain in chapter 4 turn out to have been the start of her life story: thrown out of the castle, kept by a doctor, beaten by his wife, and finally on the streets. The monk, Brother Giroflée, was forced into the cloister by his family and hates it. They smile because smiling is their trade. A joke that looked in chapter 1 like nothing more than a smutty aside about a tutor comes back in chapter 24 to collect.

The Venetian senator Pococurante (Italian for "caring little") owns Raphael, Homer and a fine garden and can't stand any of it. Candide decides he must be the happiest man alive. No, says Martin, he is sick of everything.

On a carnival night Cacambo reappears, now a slave of a deposed sultan. He brings news: Cunégonde is washing dishes on the shore of the Sea of Marmara, near Constantinople, and she has become horribly ugly.

Act VIII · The garden: "That is well said, but…"

On a galley bound for Constantinople, two oarsmen being whipped for slowness are Pangloss and the young Baron. Pangloss didn't die on the gallows: the executioner was used to burning people, not hanging them, and the rope was wet; when a surgeon who had bought the body made his first cut, Pangloss screamed so loudly the surgeon fled. The Baron didn't die either, and was later sent to the galleys for bathing naked with a young Muslim. Candide buys them both free.

Hanged, dissected, chained to an oar: does he still think all is for the best? Pangloss says he holds to his first opinion; a philosopher must not recant, and Leibniz cannot be wrong (in substance).

On the shore of the Marmara they find Cunégonde. Her skin is weathered, her eyes bloodshot, her neck withered, her arms rough and red, and Candide steps back three paces in horror: he has crossed the world for that face, and the face is gone. He no longer has the slightest wish to marry her, but he keeps his word. The Baron flies into a rage once more: a man with no pedigree, marry my sister? Payoff ① ↑Everyone has had enough of him and he is shipped back to the galleys. The seventy-one and seventy-two quarterings end their run here: pedigree fails to stop the marriage, and only sends its owner back to his oar.

With what money remains they buy a small farm. Then the money runs out. Cunégonde grows uglier and sharper-tongued by the day, Cacambo wears himself out growing vegetables and hauling them to market and curses his lot, and Pangloss mopes because he can't shine at some German university. The disasters have stopped, and what replaces them is harder to bear: boredom.

One day the old woman puts a question: Payoff ④ ↑which is worse, to be raped a hundred times by pirates, have a buttock cut off, be flogged, hanged, dissected and chained to an oar, in short to suffer everything each of us has suffered, or to sit here doing nothing? In Act IV she made every passenger admit to cursing their life; now the question is turned inside out. Can a life without misfortune be harder to endure? That, says Candide, is a big question. Martin says that man was born to live either in the convulsions of anxiety or in the lethargy of boredom.

Paquette and Giroflée turn up penniless, and Martin is proved right again. Pangloss leads everyone to consult the most famous dervish in the district: why was man created, and why is there so much evil? What business is it of yours? says the dervish. When His Highness sends a ship to Egypt, does he worry whether the mice on board are comfortable? Pangloss wants to move on to cause and effect and pre-established harmony, and the dervish shuts the door in his face.

On the way home they meet an old Turk taking the air under his orange trees. Two viziers and the mufti have just been strangled in the city; does he know? The old man says he has never known the names of any mufti or vizier and never asks what goes on in town. He just takes what he grows in his garden to market. He invites them in, and his sons and daughters bring sherbets, candied fruit and coffee. He has only twenty acres, which he farms with his children; work, he says, keeps three great evils away: boredom, vice and need.

Candide goes home and thinks it over for a long time. Payoff ⑤ ↑The King of Eldorado told him that when you are reasonably well off you should stay. He couldn't bear living where no one knew how rich he was, and left with his sheep. Only now, with the sheep dead and the treasure gone, on a patch of ground ten thousand times poorer than Eldorado, does he take the advice in. This time it comes not from a king but from an old farmer who doesn't know the names of his own ministers.

The little company sets to work, each at what they do best. Cunégonde is truly ugly, but she becomes an excellent pastry cook; Paquette embroiders; the old woman looks after the linen; Giroflée becomes a good carpenter, and even an honest man. Martin says: let's work without arguing; it's the only way to make life bearable.

Pangloss still can't help himself. All events are linked, he tells Candide: if you hadn't been kicked out of the castle, fallen into the Inquisition's hands, crossed America on foot, run the Baron through and lost your Eldorado sheep, you wouldn't be here eating candied citrons and pistachios.

Candide answers: "That is well said, but we must cultivate our garden" (chapter 30). Payoff ③ ↑The "earthly paradise" of Act I was ready-made, an Eden nobody had to work for, and a single kiss lost it. Thirty chapters later the garden is back, now the kind you have to dig yourself. The book begins with an expulsion from paradise and ends with people breaking ground by hand. Voltaire lets no one back into the castle.

The fuse

It looks as if the fuse is the kiss, and that is how Pangloss himself tallies things on the last page. But the fuse lies further back. It is the method of reasoning Pangloss taught Candide in chapter 1: whatever happens has a sufficient reason, and is therefore good. That method is why, when Jacques goes under and the city falls, Candide knows how to explain and not what to do. The book's catastrophes test the method more than they test his luck: can the explanation swallow the next thing too? On the final page Pangloss is still using it, stringing thirty chapters of suffering into a single causal chain that ends in a dish of pistachios. Candide's "well said, but" doesn't refute him. It just means Candide no longer needs him.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion I · A harbour built to drown a good man

The force of the Lisbon chapter lies in a mismatch of speed and tone. The sentences go by like a ledger: storm, hull splits, Jacques overboard, sailor walks away, Pangloss holds Candide back, earthquake, thirty thousand crushed. Each disaster is bigger than the last and every one is reported in the same flat voice. Then, into all that speed, Voltaire drops an unhurried proof.

He doesn't comment once. He just has one man in the ruins ask for wine and oil and the other answer with a syllogism, and the reader trips over the absurdity unaided. When the explanation comes as fast as the catastrophe, the explanation becomes part of the catastrophe. The cruellest stroke in the book is Pangloss holding Candide back: here optimism is more than a mistaken belief. It is the concrete reason one man didn't try to save another.

Slow motion II · The roadside in Surinam: the one time the book doesn't laugh

Candide laughs from start to finish: the lopped buttock, the philosopher who can't be hanged, the beauty shared out between two men on a weekly rota, all told in the same mocking voice. Only once does the narrator stop. The man in Surinam gets a few lines: he is lying down, this is what he's wearing, this is what he's missing. Then he explains himself in a voice as level as a weather report, and the book's greatest irony is that this passage contains none.

Look at the order of information. First the maimed body, then the causes (the millstone, the escape), then the connection to the reader's own table (sugar in Europe), and finally the last cut: his mother thought it an honour. From one man's wounds to the economy of the world, and back to the lie a mother told her son. Here, for the first time, Candide frames a definition of his own instead of repeating his teacher's.

Slow motion III · A door that shuts and a door that opens

The last chapter has two doors. At the dervish's, Pangloss arrives with the biggest questions in the book and receives a metaphor and a door shut in his face. At the old Turk's, nobody's questions get answered; he simply opens his house and feeds them what he grows.

Voltaire hands the answer to two men who refuse to reason: one won't answer the question, the other has never heard it. "Well said" is a sincere concession that Pangloss's chain is airtight. What follows "but" is no counter-argument. It is a change of subject. For thirty chapters the book has debated whether the world is good or bad, and its solution is to stop trying to win on that level. The line has been quoted for more than two centuries because it doesn't look like a conclusion and lands with the full weight of one.

The core device: an argument machine that runs on catastrophe

The central device of Candide is an experimental structure: state a thesis, then feed it counterexamples in sequence. The thesis is Leibniz as Pangloss tells it. Everything has a sufficient reason (the principle of sufficient reason: for any fact, there is a reason why it is so and not otherwise); God chose the best of all possible worlds; therefore every evil has its place in the optimal whole.

The machine runs in three steps: (1) something bad happens; (2) find some "good" thing downstream of it (syphilis → chocolate; being kicked out of the castle → the pistachios at the end); (3) declare the first a necessary condition of the second, and therefore good. What Voltaire saw is that this machine never jams. As long as the world is connected, something good can always be found downstream of anything bad; Pangloss is hanged, dissected and sent to the galleys, and the machine keeps turning.

The part that holds up is the diagnosis. A system that explains everything explains nothing: it only stamps approval on outcomes after the fact, and teaches people to start proving things when they should be reaching out a hand. Martin's pessimism is the same machine running in reverse, so Voltaire doesn't let him win either: he is right every single time, and being right never makes anyone's life better.

The part that has to be flagged honestly: Pangloss is not Leibniz. Leibniz never claimed that each thing, taken by itself, is good. His argument was that among all the combinations of things that could logically coexist (his term was "compossible"), this world is the best overall, and it contains evil because removing it would cost a greater good. Pangloss shrinks an argument about the whole into a sophistry that hands out prizes item by item. Voltaire is attacking the argument as it sounded from the pulpit, in the salon and in consolations offered after a disaster, not the strongest philosophical version of it. This is satire, not refutation.

What it forces you to think about

One · When someone has just lost everything, should you tell them it all happens for a reason?

We laugh at Pangloss. Change the scene, though: at a funeral someone murmurs that there's a plan behind it all; when you're laid off, someone says it may be a blessing in disguise. Are those words cruel?

The strongest case for Pangloss is not that he's right. It is that people need to believe their suffering isn't for nothing, or many of them won't hold up. After reading Voltaire's poem on the Lisbon disaster, Rousseau wrote back, in substance: you have taken the last consolation away from those who suffer and given them nothing in return. And so many died, he added, partly because people crowded six- and seven-storey houses together, which nature never asked them to do. In the book itself, Pangloss is hanged and chained to an oar and stays gentle and kind; optimism kept him from turning bad. Martin, who sees through everything, has never saved anyone either.

The other side is just as hard. The cost of the belief is paid by other people. Pangloss believes the harbour was made for Jacques, so Jacques goes unsaved, and a Surinam planter can believe just as sincerely that slavery has its place in the optimal whole. A system that can justify anything is most convenient of all for those who do harm. The sentence that consoles a victim is the same sentence that lets a bystander off.

Voltaire is clearly on the second side, but he never makes Pangloss a villain. That is the margin he leaves. Next time you are about to tell someone in pain that it happens for a reason, you might ask yourself who the sentence is for: to ease their pain, or to spare yourself from having to do anything?

Two · Two ministers have just been strangled in the capital. Should you tend only your own garden?

The old Turk doesn't know the ministers' names and never asks. Stop reasoning, work: it is the only prescription the book writes.

The strongest case for the garden: Candide tried caring about the whole world, and every grand narrative in it took its turn using him. The King's army, the Inquisition, the Jesuit state, every one of them took lives somewhere. Holding on to a plot of ground where you can see what your labour produces is no escape. It may be the only action that doesn't lie. And it genuinely cures this little company; even Giroflée turns honest. Today's version is familiar: a whole evening scrolling through news from far away, nothing changed, and the next day you can't even do your own job properly.

The strongest case against can be written with Voltaire himself: three years after Candide, he left the garden. In 1762 Jean Calas, a Protestant merchant in Toulouse, was wrongly convicted of murdering his son and broken on the wheel. Voltaire campaigned for three years and wrote his Treatise on Tolerance, until the verdict was overturned. Had he, too, never asked what went on in town, the Calas family would have remained a murderer's family for ever. Nor will the man in Surinam grow back a leg because someone planted a vegetable patch. In a world with slavery in it, is tending only your own garden a peace that only those who can afford it get to enjoy?

Voltaire gave the last line to the garden and spent the rest of his life writing a footnote to it. Perhaps the garden needn't mean "ignore the world" and could mean "start with something whose results you can actually see". The question is how you'd know whether your garden right now is where you sharpen your tools or where you hide.

Three · Would you stay somewhere perfect where nobody knows who you are?

Eldorado wants for nothing, and Candide leaves after a month. His reasons are candid: here we'd be like everyone else, back home we'd be richer than any king, and besides, Cunégonde isn't here.

The strongest case for leaving: a paradise that doesn't need you isn't your paradise. Eldorado's happiness is ready-made and leaves Candide nothing to do, and the woman he loves is suffering somewhere outside it; staying would mean abandoning her. Wanting to be seen, wanting to do something only you can do, isn't pure vanity. And how much of a perfect society sealed off by cliffs owes its perfection to the walls?

The case for staying is strong too. Not one sheep survives, Cunégonde becomes someone else, and he pays a dozen chapters of misery for the privilege of being seen. What he wanted was never happiness. It was being happier than other people.

Voltaire's answer is in the structure: the plot of land at the end is far poorer than Eldorado, but they dug it themselves. The everyday version: a steady, respectable job where nobody knows your name, against a risky chance to become somebody. The last time you left somewhere that was "reasonably well", was it for a better life, or for a bigger name?

Motifs: what Voltaire kept writing about

All his life Voltaire fought two things: religious fanaticism (his battle cry was écrasez l'infâme, "crush the infamous thing") and the spirit of system, meaning any grand doctrine that claims to have the whole world accounted for. Nearly all his philosophical tales send an innocent to collide with one or both of them.

In Zadig (1747), a sage whose good deeds keep bringing him bad luck is finally told by an angel that evil has its uses; at that point Voltaire was still half inclined to believe in Providence. L'Ingénu (1767) drops a young man raised among the Huron of North America into France and lets him run into prisons and the Church. Candide is the darkest and fastest of the line: it travels from "Providence may have its reasons" to "stop asking, go and work", and between the two lies the Lisbon earthquake.

When he wrote it, he was living on an estate outside Geneva; soon afterwards he bought Ferney, just across the French border, where for nearly twenty years he planted trees, set up workshops and ran his farms. For him, cultivating the garden was more than a figure of speech.

Controversy and criticism

A straw man? Pangloss is a caricature of Leibniz (see above). Defenders reply that Voltaire was attacking the doctrine as it actually circulated, which was in its caricatured form.

Antisemitism. Don Issachar is drawn as a greedy buyer, and the book has several offhand anti-Jewish jokes. Voltaire's writings elsewhere, including the Philosophical Dictionary, contain a great deal of contempt for Jews; that much is not seriously disputed. The real argument is over how to weigh it against his campaigns against religious persecution.

Race and empire. The Surinam passage is among the most famous anti-slavery pages of the eighteenth century, yet the same book has two South American Indigenous women take monkeys as lovers, played as a bestiality joke. In other works Voltaire was a polygenist (he held that the human races had separate origins), and today's scholars count him among the sources of modern racial thinking. Sympathy for the slave and that theory of race were two things in one man.

Women's bodies as punchlines. The three main female characters are moved along the plot almost entirely by being raped, sold and carved up, and the narrator tells it in the same jaunty voice he uses for war. Some readers see this as the satire's force: telling everyone's suffering with the same indifference makes us notice the indifference. Others note that the mockery falls noticeably harder on the women, whose endings are "turned ugly" and "became a pastry cook".

Too easy an ending? Critics call the garden a retreat only the comfortable can choose: Candide can buy his farm only because some Eldorado money is left. It is often read as political quietism, and the most forceful critic of that reading is Voltaire himself, through what he did next.

Crossovers

  • Philosophy of science · Popper's falsifiability: Popper held that a theory able to accommodate any possible observation isn't science, and Pangloss's machine accommodates earthquakes, nooses and syphilis alike. The difference: Popper was drawing a boundary and never called metaphysics meaningless; Voltaire simply makes it ridiculous.
  • Social psychology · Lerner's belief in a just world: in experiments in the 1960s, Melvin Lerner found that people who watch an innocent person suffer, and can't help, tend to derogate the victim in order to keep believing the world is fair. Pangloss is a close relative: to save the system, suffering must be made reasonable. The difference: he never blames the victims, and praises his own suffering along with theirs. The reputation he protects is the universe's.
  • Seismology · the Lisbon questionnaire: after the quake, Portugal's chief minister, the Marquis of Pombal, had every parish answer the same questions: how long did the shaking last, how many aftershocks were there, what happened to the wells. Those returns are often called the beginning of modern seismology. In the same disaster, Pangloss looked for a purpose while others started looking for causes. The difference: a questionnaire offers no consolation.

Ten lines to take with you

① Noses were made for spectacles: Pangloss doesn't explain the world, he certifies it.

② What Candide is kicked out of isn't a castle but a ready-made paradise where nobody works.

③ The book's first good man is an unbaptised one, drowned in a harbour while a philosopher blocked the rescue.

④ They burned people to prevent earthquakes, and the earth shook again that same day.

⑤ The woman with half a buttock says: a hundred times I wanted to die, and still I loved life.

⑥ The only place where all is well isn't on the map, and he left it after a month.

⑦ The price of sugar is a hand and a leg, and Candide's optimism stays behind on that roadside.

⑧ One says all is well, one says all is ill; neither is ever wrong, and neither ever helps anyone.

⑨ When the disasters stop, something harder arrives: boredom.

⑩ "Well said, but…": not a refutation, just the end of needing one.
