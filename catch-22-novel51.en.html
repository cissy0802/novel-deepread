Catch-22

Joseph Heller · 1961

中文 →

At the edge of a cemetery on a small Mediterranean island, a captain is sitting up in a tree with no clothes on. Below him a burial is under way: the chaplain reads, the spades go in and out. He is not boycotting the funeral. Days earlier, in the belly of a bomber, he held a nineteen-year-old gunner whose blood soaked his uniform through, and he has refused to wear clothes since.
Then the squadron's mess officer climbs up, settles on the next branch, produces a ball of cotton dipped in chocolate and presses him to taste it — he has just bought the entire Egyptian cotton crop and badly needs an outlet.
The grave is filled in. The cotton is still being held out.

In one sentence

In 1944, in an American bomber squadron on a small Mediterranean island, a bombardier who only wants to go home alive discovers that the number of missions required is raised by five every time anyone is about to finish it — and that the sole exemption clause reads: a madman can be grounded, but he has to ask, and anyone who wants to save his own life in the face of real danger is obviously not mad. So he works every loophole he can find, and after his friends are killed off one by one and his superiors offer him a hero's ticket home in exchange for his silence, he runs.

Coordinates

Joseph Heller (1923–1999), a Brooklyn son of Jewish immigrants, flew sixty missions as a B-25 bombardier with the Army Air Forces, out of Corsica. He later said most of those sixty were uneventful; on one run over Avignon a gunner aboard his plane was badly wounded — that single flight is the seed of the whole book.

Catch-22 was published in 1961, his first novel. It was to be called Catch-18, but Leon Uris brought out Mila 18 the same year and the publisher feared a collision, so 18 became 22. American reviews were split and hardcover sales modest; it became a bestseller in Britain first. What turned it into a generation's common language was the sixties — once Vietnam started, this novel about the Second World War was read as a novel about now. Today catch-22 is an ordinary dictionary noun: a bind in which every choice loses, and whose small print also proves you deserved to lose.

In literary history it belongs to the black-humor line: it doesn't reach for pathos, it makes you laugh first and then shows you what you were laughing at. Heller's own note at the front points out that Pianosa, south of Elba, is far too small to hold the units the book describes — this is not a war history, it is an absurdity machine assembled out of army parts.

The story

Act one · A liver that knows better than to flare up

The novel opens in a hospital ward. Captain Yossarian, a bombardier, is lying in the air-force hospital on Pianosa with a pain in his liver. The pain is calibrated with great precision: a little worse and it would be jaundice, which doctors can cure, after which he would have to fly again; a little better and it would be nothing at all, and he would have to fly again. He is parked exactly in between — a condition that can neither be treated nor discharged is the most perfect state he has been able to invent.

In the next bed, Dunbar's life plan is to do the most boring things available, on the theory that boredom slows time and slow time means a longer life. In the corner lies a soldier in white, encased head to foot in plaster and gauze, one tube going in and one coming out; Dunbar's conclusion is that there is nobody inside.

Yossarian is not afraid of the war. He has made a specific discovery: someone is trying to kill him. Clevinger has argued the point with him — nobody is trying to kill you, they're shooting at everyone.

"And what difference does that make?"— Yossarian to Clevinger, early chapters

That question is the door into the book. Once you grant that "shooting at everyone" and "trying to kill me" come to the same thing, the grand narrative of the war collapses and what remains is one specific man being specifically frightened by specific flak.

Somewhere in this act a name surfaces for the first time, with no introduction, like a joke: Snowden. seed ①Yossarian produces it at an education session, asking where the Snowdens of yesteryear have gone — an old line of poetry deliberately mangled. Nobody understands; he doesn't explain. Every few dozen pages another fragment of that name drops out, as if someone were dealing cards slowly.

Act two · What the rule actually says

Colonel Cathcart's entire ambition is to be a general and to have his photograph in The Saturday Evening Post. To look keen in front of his own superiors he keeps volunteering his men for dangerous missions and keeps raising the number of missions required to go home — whenever someone is close to finishing, he adds another five, all the way up to eighty. That number is the only timeline in the book that moves forward: everything else circles, only the count climbs.

So everyone looks for a way out. Doc Daneeka explains the one legal door, which is Catch-22: a man who is crazy doesn't have to fly, and all he has to do is ask; but wanting to preserve your life in the face of real and immediate danger is the process of a rational mind — so the moment he asks, he has proved he is sane, and he has to fly.

"That's some catch, that Catch-22." / "It's the best there is."— Yossarian and Doc Daneeka

What makes it terrible is not its severity but that it converts the act of applying for an exemption into the evidence that disqualifies you from one. The more clearly you want to live, the more firmly it proves you must go and die. The squadron's most reliably insane member is Orr, Yossarian's tent-mate: small, buck-toothed, forever taking apart the little gasoline stove in their tent, and forever bringing planes back half-wrecked — belly landings, wheels-up landings, ditchings in the sea. By the letter of the rule he could be grounded any time he asked. He never asks. seed ②

The other exits close one by one. Major Major Major Major — named that way by a father with a sense of humor, then promoted by a machine said to have one too — rules on taking command that nobody may come in to see him in his office while he is in it, only while he is out. So the men who want to apply for grounding can never find him. This is how the army's logic stacks up: every rule is reasonable, and together they make a room with no door.

Clevinger, who believes in argument, tries due process. An Action Board tries him; the charge is never made clear and the judges care mainly about whether he used a disrespectful word. They convict him. Weeks later his plane flies into a cloud over the Mediterranean and does not come out — no wreckage, no parachutes, a whole crew gone. Deaths are handled this way throughout: one sentence, no requiem, a trip-wire laid in the gap between two jokes.

Act three · The syndicate, in which everybody has a share

Mess officer Milo Minderbinder is the happiest man in the book. Using military aircraft as freighters, he turns the squadron's kitchen into a Mediterranean trading house: he buys eggs in Sicily, sells them to himself in Malta, buys them back at a higher price and sells them to the mess below cost — which ought to ruin him, except that the money somehow appears in the turning. Nobody can follow the arithmetic, and he has one line that shuts down every objection: everybody has a share in the syndicate. seed ③

He supplies several belligerents at once: a contract with the Americans to bomb the bridge at Orvieto, and a contract with the Germans to defend the same bridge with anti-aircraft fire, paid by both. His most spectacular deal is cornering an entire year's Egyptian cotton, which he then cannot move, which is why the squadron is invited to eat cotton with a coating of chocolate on it.

To find an outlet for the cotton he takes on new business: German money to bomb his own squadron. His planes arrive on schedule, drop their loads on schedule, kill and maim his own comrades. When the military investigates, his defense is simple: the contract was highly profitable, and everybody has a share in the syndicate — meaning the men who were bombed were paid for it. payoff ③ ↑The beauty of the move is that the egg accounts have already trained the reader: Milo's books never add up and his conclusions can never be refuted; the moment you stopped asking about the eggs, you conceded the bombing. The inquiry goes nowhere. He later becomes mayor of Palermo, vice-governor of Malta, and is worshipped as a rain god in parts of Africa — the man who runs a war as supply-chain management comes through it without a scratch.

Act four · The dead man in the tent and the doctor who wasn't on the plane

There is a dead man living in Yossarian's tent, named Mudd. He was put on a plane less than two hours after arriving and killed over Orvieto; because he never got around to reporting in, the paperwork has no record that he ever existed. So his belongings stay in the tent forever and nobody has the authority to remove them — a man who does not exist on file cannot be acknowledged to have died. seed ④

Doc Daneeka is afraid of aircraft down to the bone. To collect flight pay he has McWatt enter his name on the flight log each time without ever boarding. Then McWatt has an accident: he likes to buzz the beach, and one pass takes his propeller through a raft and cuts the young man standing on it in half; he pulls the plane up and flies it into a mountain.

Daneeka's name is on that manifest. So the doctor stands alive in the middle of his squadron while the official record has him dead. The pay stops, the tent is taken away, the men avoid him because talking to a dead man is unsettling; his wife in America receives the condolence notice and the pension, and also his own letters in his own hand, and believes the notice — she moves house. payoff ④ ↑Mudd laid the rule down first: in this system, whether you exist is decided by the files and not by your body. State the rule, then make a living man act it out, and the passage stops being a joke and becomes a verdict — Daneeka's only way to prove he is alive is to keep talking, and his talking is classified as the thing that makes people uncomfortable around the dead.

Act five · Rome: an old man's arithmetic, and a window

Leave is taken in Rome, where the squadron keeps an apartment full of whores, an old woman who manages them, and an old man who sits in a chair all day picking arguments. Nately, a nineteen-year-old from a rich family, falls in love with one of the women and intends to marry her and take her home. He quarrels with the old man about nations: America is the strongest country in history, Nately says, and will win. The old man answers without haste — strong is not the same as long-lived; Rome fell, Greece fell, Persia and Spain fell, and Italy, a country that gets beaten and surrenders, has outlasted every one of them. seed ⑤He has cheered the arrival of several sets of masters in his lifetime, he says, which is why he is still sitting in this chair. Nately is speechless with rage: he cannot refute the arithmetic, he only knows it is shameful.

Not long after, Nately flies his last mission: over La Spezia, Dobbs's plane hits his and both go down. Yossarian is the one who tells the woman. She decides that the man who killed Nately is Yossarian, and for the rest of the book she comes after him with a knife — at the airfield, in the mess, in the hospital, in the woods, she keeps turning up. The most tenacious avenger in the novel is pursuing a wholly innocent man for reasons that make no sense at all, and her grief is real.

Then come the blackest two pages. Aarfy, the navigator — the pipe-smoking, chronically lost, unfailingly amiable fat man — rapes a maid in the apartment and afterwards pushes her out of the window. Yossarian sees her on the pavement below. When the MPs come up the stairs he assumes Aarfy is finished; they come in, apologize to Aarfy, and arrest Yossarian on a clear charge: being in Rome without a pass.

Released, he returns to the apartment to find everyone gone: the girls driven off, the old man dead, the old woman alone in the wreckage. She asked the MPs what right they had, and they told her plainly:

"Catch-22 says they have a right to do anything we can't stop them from doing."— the old woman, quoting the MPs, late in the novel

Here Yossarian finally understands: Catch-22 is not a regulation, it is an incantation. It has no text, no source, no existence at all — but as long as everyone believes it exists it works better than any real law, because a thing that does not exist can be neither amended nor repealed.

That night he walks through Rome looking for the younger sister of Nately's girl. Every street he crosses has something happening on it: a man beating a dog, a man beating a man, soldiers cornering a woman, and no one intervening. The comedy stops dead in this chapter; there is not one joke in it. Heller saves up the whole book's laughter and cuts the power here — which is when you learn what the laughs of the previous four hundred pages were for: they were an analgesic, and the drug has been withdrawn.

Act six · Snowden's secret, and a rubber raft

After seventy missions Yossarian refuses to fly. He walks around the camp barefoot with his uniform on backwards and says openly that he is finished. Punishing him would tell the squadron that refusal is possible, so Colonel Cathcart and Lieutenant Colonel Korn offer him a deal — he goes home a hero: promotion to major, another medal, immediate transfer to the United States. All he has to do is tell everyone back home that he likes his two commanding officers and that eighty missions are no problem at all.

Yossarian accepts and walks out of the office feeling weightless — and Nately's girl, waiting outside the door, puts a knife into him.

He wakes up in the hospital and works out what the deal actually costs: his ticket home is paid for by the men who stay and keep flying, and paid in his own voice, the hero's mouth that will call it a good arrangement. What they are buying is not his silence. It is his testimony.

It is also in this hospital bed that the last card turns over. The name that has been dropping through the whole book is finally given whole: Snowden, a nineteen-year-old gunner, dying in a plane over Avignon. payoff ① ↑Over the intercom comes a call to help the bombardier; Yossarian crawls back and finds him lying there saying he is cold. There is a hole in his thigh, and Yossarian dresses it quickly and well and is almost proud of the job. Then he opens the flak suit — and everything inside slides out onto the floor. Only now does he see that the wound he has been working on so carefully did not matter.

He holds him and can only say, there, there. Snowden says he is cold once more, and then stops.

"Man was matter."— Snowden's secret, as Yossarian states it near the end

Drop a man out of a window and he falls; set fire to him and he burns; bury him and he rots like other garbage. The spirit gone, man is matter. Heller stacks four hundred pages of jokes in front of that sentence so that it needs no rhetoric when it lands. Look back at the education session and the Snowdens of yesteryear: then it sounded like a clever pun; now it is a man who has held a body asking everyone which column those names were entered in. Yossarian's refusal to wear clothes is explained here too — that was not madness, it was a uniform soaked through with blood.

Then the chaplain runs into the ward with news: Orr is alive. Orr rowed to Sweden. The small man who wrecked a plane every time, went into the sea every time, and was fished out every time, vanished after one last ditching and was never found — he sat in that little raft and rowed himself, stroke by stroke, to a neutral country. payoff ② ↑This is the lightest and loudest stroke in the book: like everyone in the squadron, the reader has spent four hundred pages treating Orr as comic relief, and all those crash landings and that endlessly dismantled stove were rehearsals. He never applied to be grounded because he never intended to use that door. The man everyone agreed was the craziest is the only one who solved the problem rationally.

Yossarian turns down the deal and refuses to fly. He will run: to Rome to find the little sister, and then somehow to Sweden. Major Danby calls this running away from his responsibilities. He disagrees.

"I'm not running away from my responsibilities. I'm running to them."— Yossarian to Major Danby, final chapter

The old man's arithmetic turns out to have been right: the hard thing shatters first and the soft thing survives. payoff ⑤ ↑The whole novel has mocked that cowardly calculus, and at the end it lets its hero act on it; the one thing that keeps it from being mere cowardice is that it now has a destination — not survival, but a particular person to go and find. The chaplain says he will stay and persevere. Yossarian goes out the door, the knife comes down again and misses him by inches, and he runs.

The causal chain of the book can be wound onto a single fuse: it was not the war that drove these men mad, it was one colonel who wanted his photograph in a magazine. Eighty missions, a flight surgeon declared dead by paperwork, a crew swallowed by a cloud, a naked man in a tree — all of it is the wake of that small vanity. The book is not about Germans; almost no German in it has a name. It is about how your own side's bureaucracy opens a second route to killing you alongside the enemy's, and opens it legally, with clauses to cite, and with a dividend for the dead.

Two slow-motion replays

One · Snowden, told seven times

The technical core of this scene is delay. Snowden's death happens before the main body of the novel, yet Heller cuts it into fragments and scatters them: first a name, then "that time over Avignon," then the insides, and finally the full ten minutes — each time a little more, always just as you are laughing hardest. The method imitates the shape of trauma itself: nobody remembers a terrible thing in sequence; one is tripped by it, over and over. So the book is not badly structured. Its chronology is the chronology inside Yossarian's head.

Two · The doctor's dialogue: three lines that shut a door

Daneeka's explanation of the rule repays taking apart. Heller gives the conclusion first, then the condition, and lets you discover that the condition has eaten the conclusion: a madman can be grounded (hope rises) — but he has to ask (hope survives) — and asking proves he isn't mad (the door shuts). The order cannot be rearranged; told in this order, the reader exhales with Yossarian on the second line and falls through the third.

The second layer is that the rhythm of the dialogue enacts something: cooperating makes it worse. Every time Yossarian presses, Daneeka states the rule more clearly, and every increase in clarity narrows his position — Clevinger's appeals convicting him is the same shape. Inside a closed system, cooperating with the inquiry means supplying the evidence.

The core device: how the book is built

The logical shape of the sentence

Catch-22 is not a regulation but a self-referential trap: the condition for the exemption (submitting the request) is exactly the evidence that cancels eligibility for it (it proves you are rational). It belongs to the family of "this sentence is false," but it is more practical than a paradox — a paradox leaves you nothing to say, this leaves you nowhere to go.

The second mutation matters more. Late in the novel the rule has stopped being about sanity at all, and the MPs use it as a different sentence: they have a right to do anything we can't stop them from doing. That is the book's high point as thought, because it exposes what the rule always contained: it was never a logic, it is power authorizing itself. Logic is the shell; whoever can enforce it decides. And its real strength is its nonexistence — no text, no legislator, nobody to appeal to.

Scrambled time: why this book cannot be told in order

The novel's forty-two chapters are each titled after a person or a place, like a deck that can be shuffled; events double back and consequences often arrive before their causes. By Heller's own account, and his scholars', he kept elaborate charts while writing, so the disorder is designed, not botched.

It buys two things. First, the reader inhabits the same kind of time as the characters: no progress, no advancing front, only that growing number of required missions. Second, it makes meaning arrive late. Honestly, this is also what makes the book tiresome: long stretches in the middle circle without moving, and plenty of readers put it down there. That is its device, not its virtue or its flaw — it is both at once.

What it makes you think about

Question one
Your superiors offer you a ticket home on condition that you go back and say everything here is fine. Do you sign?

Take the case for signing to its strongest. Yossarian has flown seventy missions, more than any contract asked of him; his friends are mostly dead; the war is nearly won. Refusing changes nothing — the count will not come down, the others will fly regardless, and he will simply put himself in a military prison. His testimony hardly matters either: what keeps men flying is orders and military law, not what he says in a newspaper. Refusing an evil you cannot affect, at your own entire expense, is that morality or fastidiousness? And nothing in the deal requires him to harm anyone; it requires him to keep quiet and say some pleasant things — which most of the world does daily, and for far less than getting home alive.

Now take refusing to its strongest. What the deal buys is not his silence but his standing as a witness: he is the only man who held Snowden and saw that uniform, and putting him on record saying eighty missions are fine converts the last person who knows into a publicist. The effect is not zero, either — it makes the next increase easier. When a victim says publicly that nothing is wrong, the institution gets the one thing it cannot manufacture: legitimacy. Every historical moment that has needed a representative figure to speak up was buying exactly that.

Today it has the shape of a non-disparagement clause in a severance agreement, a confidentiality payment, a request that you say something you don't believe in public for the good of the whole. What they have in common: you pay the entire cost, the institution takes the entire benefit, and refusing saves no one else. Yossarian's answer is to run, not to blow the whistle — the novel does not make him a hero, only keeps him from being a loudspeaker.

Question two
In a war you believe is just, does an individual have the right to quit?

Heller set the book in the Second World War deliberately, and it is the hardest discomfort in it: the fight against Nazism leaves almost no moral room for argument. So hear the case against Yossarian at full strength: he can run because others don't. His freedom is bought for him — if every bombardier priced his own share of the risk and walked, Europe is not liberated and the camps are not opened. This is the free-rider problem in its purest form: one man's exit harms nobody, therefore everyone has grounds to exit, and universal exit collapses everything. Further: any army must place "I'm frightened" outside the grounds for exemption, whatever the rule is called, or the army is what breaks first. And not one German in the book has a name, which is itself a partiality of framing: photograph only your own side's absurdity and the war appears to have no opponent.

Now Yossarian's side, also at full strength. He never opposed fighting the war; he opposed forty becoming forty-five, fifty becoming fifty-five and finally eighty, with no increase ever justified by military need — only by a colonel who wants a magazine cover. The institution tore up the contract first. His protest is not theoretical either: men who finish the count don't go home, dead men stay on the roster, a supplier bombs his own squadron and is not punished. When an organization turns its own rules into something it can revise at will, it forfeits the standing to invoke rules when sending people to die. Refusing a machine that has lost control and refusing the war's objective are two different acts.

Keep the publication date in mind: written by 1961, read in the sixties by the Vietnam generation as their own book, and Heller said more than once that his real subject was postwar America rather than 1944. So the dilemma's true shape is this: how a just war supplied the grammar for an unjust one. It turns up today in every sentence that begins "think of the bigger picture, your grievance is nothing" — the question is never whether the bigger picture matters, but who gets to put your share into it on your behalf.

Question three
"Everybody has a share" — does taking the dividend make you complicit?

Milo is the easiest character to dismiss as a joke and the hardest to refute. At full strength: he breaks no military law; he feeds the squadron better than it has ever eaten; every deal is voluntary, he cheats nobody and steals nothing, and he will show you the books (which nobody can follow); as for bombing his own squadron, that was a signed contract and the profit really was distributed by share, including to the men who were hit. His logic is frighteningly complete: if the proceeds belong to everyone, so does the act. That argument runs all day, every day: your pension bought an index fund, and the fund holds the company you object to; your phone sits at the end of a supply chain you would rather not examine. To demand that Milo stop is to demand that the market halt somewhere and consider morality — and on what grounds does it halt there?

The strongest counter is not that profit is wicked. It is that what makes Milo appalling is his consistency. A system that honors one principle and honors it all the way will arrive at the square marked "bombing your own men is also a piece of business"; the only way to avoid that square is to keep a line outside the principle that cannot be traded, and such a line is always logically arbitrary, always unjustifiable, always just someone saying: not this. Milo is never malicious, and that is the point: evil does not require malice, only a self-consistent set of rules and a group of people holding shares.

Question four
Who gets to certify that you are fine?

The sharpest thing about Catch-22 is not military, it is its general form: the procedure for requesting an exemption is used as the evidence for denying one. By its reasoning, only people who cannot state their case deserve to be excused; anyone who can explain the bind has thereby proved they can cope.

Take the institution's side at full strength — it is more reasonable than we like to admit: exemption cannot be left to self-report. If "I don't think I can do this" were sufficient to discharge an obligation, every place with obligations would leak like a sieve — and a good share of those obligations are what protect people. So there must be an outside judge of "cannot": a doctor, an assessment, a procedure. The only trouble is that any outside judge ends up defining "cannot" in whatever way is most convenient, and the most convenient definition is always that a person able to apply is a person still able to cope.

Now the individual's side at full strength. Yossarian's problem is not psychological, it is environmental — he is frightened because people really are shooting at him, and classifying that fear as an emotion amounts to calling the flak a hallucination. When a system diagnoses the correct response to danger as something to be overcome, it has already conceded that it cannot deal with the danger. The everyday reruns are dense: if you can write the email requesting leave, you can still work; if you can describe clearly how you were bullied, you are not broken enough; if you can produce evidence of discrimination, you have resources, so you are not the neediest case.

The novel offers no answer, but it does offer one very cold hint: Orr never applies. He does not argue with the rule, does not try to prove himself crazy enough, does not use the door marked "the insane may leave" — he spends four hundred pages quietly practising how to put an aircraft into the sea safely, and becomes the only man in the squadron to get home. That is not life advice. It is an observation: with a self-referential rule, you win by refusing its vocabulary.

The thing Heller kept writing

Line his books up and the same obsession keeps surfacing: a man caught inside an account of things he cannot refute, where what holds him has no malice, only procedure. In Catch-22 that thing is the army; in Something Happened (1974) it is the corporation — a middle manager slowly frightening himself to pieces while nothing happens, no flak anywhere, the shape of the fear identical; God Knows (1984) puts an aged King David in bed settling accounts with God.

In 1981 Heller was struck by Guillain-Barré syndrome and spent a period paralysed; he later wrote No Laughing Matter (1986) with a friend about it. It helps explain where the tone comes from: not sneering, but the humor of a man who knows the body can defect at any moment.

The other half of the obsession is language. The real weapon in his novels is never a gun, it is a sentence: a forged signature can jail a chaplain, "everybody has a share" can close an investigation into a bombing, a rule that does not exist can govern an entire command. In Heller, the final form of power is control over wording. And the individual's one remaining weapon is also language — Yossarian's whole dignity is his refusal, at the last moment, to say that eighty missions are no problem.

Criticism and controversy

The objections carry weight and there is no point covering for the book:

  • Women come in three kinds. Almost every woman here is a nurse, a whore or a victim, and almost none has an inner life. The person Nately genuinely loves, who drives much of the second half, has no name at all — the book calls her Nately's whore. The defense is that this is deliberate: we are inside the field of vision of a group of military men, and the objectification is the thing being exhibited. The criticism still stands: in these passages the novel's bantering tone and the gaze it means to indict are using the same voice.
  • Loose, repetitive, and hard going in the middle. The circling is by design, and the cost is real: long stretches of four hundred-odd pages go nowhere, and the supporting cast blurs. American reviewers split at the time, with the negative fire concentrated here; it did not win that year's National Book Award.
  • The trouble with calling it antiwar. Writing the Second World War as a bureaucratic farce has a price: the enemy in the book is your own colonel, and the death camps are outside the frame. Critics have argued that this drains the moral weight from that particular war and supplies ammunition for the lazier position that all wars are equally absurd. Heller repeatedly said his real subject was postwar America — both a defense and an admission of the mismatch.
  • The 1998 plagiarism flap. It was pointed out that the book resembles Louis Falstein's 1950 novel Face of a Hero in setting and several incidents, and the British press ran with it for a while. Heller said he had never read it; Falstein never made any accusation in his lifetime; most commentators concluded the resemblances stay at the surface of a shared kind of war.

Ten sentences to take away

① The enemy in this book is not the Germans. It is a colonel who wants his photograph in a magazine.

② The shape of Catch-22: the act of requesting the exemption is itself the evidence for refusing it.

③ Its greatest strength is that it does not exist — no text, no legislator, so it can neither be cited nor repealed.

④ The MPs' translation is its true self: they have a right to do anything we can't stop them from doing.

⑤ Milo is not wicked, only consistent. Evil needs no malice, just coherent rules and a group of shareholders.

⑥ Doc Daneeka is alive, the file says he is dead, so he is dead: here, existence is decided by paperwork.

⑦ The laughter is an analgesic. Somewhere on that night walk through Rome, the drug is withdrawn.

⑧ Snowden's secret is one line: the spirit gone, man is matter. Four hundred pages of jokes clear its way.

⑨ Orr wins by never arguing with the rule — he spends the book practising how to ditch a plane safely.

⑩ Yossarian does not become a hero at the end. He only refuses to be a loudspeaker, which some days is all there is.
