Novels, Deep-Read · Anthony Burgess · No.123

A Clockwork Orange

A Clockwork Orange · Anthony Burgess · 1962 · three parts, twenty-one chapters · first person

中文 →

A fifteen-year-old boy is strapped into a chair, his head held still, his eyelids pinned open with clips. Every so often a man in a white coat leans in and drips water into his eyes so they won't dry out.
On the screen: beatings, rapes, newsreel footage of the camps. His stomach is heaving. He wants to vomit, wants to close his eyes, and can't.
Then the soundtrack starts, and it's the composer he loves more than anything on earth.
He begins to scream that this is a sin — not what's on the screen, but what they're doing to the music.

This piece contains full spoilers — who the boy kills, how he is "cured," whom he meets once he's out, why he goes out of a window, and what happens in the final chapter that both the first American edition and Kubrick's film left out. Half the book's meaning sits in that chapter; there's no discussing the novel without it.

In One Line

A teenager who beats people up for fun is turned by the state, through a conditioning treatment, into someone who becomes violently ill at the very thought of violence — so he can no longer do evil, and can no longer choose not to. The novel asks which is more human: a good man who has had his choice taken away, or a bad man who chooses badly? And then, in its last chapter, it gives an answer plenty of readers refuse to accept: he simply grows up.

Where It Sits

Anthony Burgess (1917–1993) was a Mancunian raised Catholic who later lapsed, served in the army, taught in Malaya, wrote more than thirty novels and — seriously, all his life — composed music, symphonies included. A Clockwork Orange came out in Britain in 1962, a book of under two hundred pages he claimed to have written in three weeks. The same year he published a second dystopia, The Wanting Seed.

Its place in the dystopian line is unusual. Orwell and Huxley show a system pressing down on everyone; Burgess narrows the lens to one person, and picks the least sympathetic person imaginable — a rapist and a killer — to argue the case for free will. Kubrick's 1971 film made the title more famous than the book, and the film was based on the American edition, which was missing the last chapter.

The Story

Act One · A Night at the Milk Bar

England, a city, a not-very-distant future. At night the adults stay in with the television and the streets belong to the young. Alex is fifteen and leads a gang of four; the others are Pete, Georgie, and Dim, who is strong and slow. They sit in the Korova Milkbar drinking milk laced with drugs and decide what to do with the evening.

The novel's first sentence is a question: Seed ①"What's it going to be then, eh?" (the opening of Part One). Alex tells the whole story in Nadsat, a teenage slang built mostly on Russian roots: a friend is a droog (Russian drug), good is horrorshow (khorosho), to see is to viddy, to hit is to tolchock. For the first few pages you feel dropped into a foreign country; a dozen pages later you're talking it too. And every so often he turns to you directly: "O my brothers." From the first page you've been seated at his table.

The night that follows is close to an itemised list. In the street they stop an elderly man walking home from the public library with books under his arm, Seed ②tear the books apart, stamp on his false teeth and leave him bleeding. They rob a small shop and beat the owners. Near a disused power station they come upon a rival gang — led by Billyboy — about to assault a young girl, and the two gangs fight. Seed ③Dim swings his chain, Alex works his razor, and Billyboy's lot run. Nobody is rescuing anybody; fighting is simply the fun part.

Then they steal a car, drive out into the country and stop at a cottage with a sign on the gate reading HOME, claiming there's been an accident and they need the telephone. Inside are a writer and his young wife. The four burst in wearing masks, beat the writer down, and gang-rape his wife in front of him. On the table is a typescript; its title is A Clockwork Orange. Seed ④Alex reads a passage aloud — roughly, that forcing a living creature capable of growth and sweetness to obey laws fit only for a machine is an outrage — finds it ridiculous and pompous, and tears the pages all over the floor.

Home: Municipal Flatblock 18A, the lift broken again, the mural celebrating the dignity of labour covered in obscenities. His parents are asleep. Alex lies on his bed and puts on his music Seed ⑤— Mozart, Bach, and finally Beethoven, whom he calls "Ludwig van." As the music rises he sees razors and blood and hears screaming, and falls asleep in the pleasure of it. Burgess plants the book's sharpest idea right here: the boy's feeling for beauty is real, and it grows from the same root as his violence.

Act Two · The Leader's Chair, and an Old Woman With Cats

Next day he skips school. His "post-corrective adviser," P. R. Deltoid, turns up to warn him: one more incident and it won't be reform school. Alex shrugs him off, goes to the record shop to collect his copy of the Ninth Symphony, picks up two girls of about ten, takes them home, gets them drunk and rapes them while the Ninth plays. The novel passes over it in his usual breezy tone — and the breeziness is exactly what Burgess wants you to feel sick about.

That evening the gang turns on him. Georgie wants to move up to bigger jobs; Dim resents being hit by Alex over nothing the night before. By the canal Alex pulls his razor and draws blood from both of them, and is back in charge. To show he can listen, he agrees to Georgie's plan: rob an old woman who lives alone in a big house on the edge of town with a houseful of cats.

Alex climbs in alone, struggles with her among the cats, and knocks her to the floor. Sirens are already outside. As he runs out, Dim lashes him across the eyes with his chain and the three of them leave him there. The blow isn't an accident; it's the echo of the mutiny he has only just put down. The police beat him in the cells; Deltoid arrives and spits in his face. The old woman dies. At fifteen Alex is a murderer, sentenced to fourteen years.

Act Three · Number 6655321

Part Two opens with the same question — what's it going to be then, eh? — except that the one being asked no longer has a name. He is prisoner 6655321 in State Jail 84F.

Two years in, he has learned to behave. He assists the prison chaplain, plays the records at services, and reads the Bible — the wars, the floggings, the crucifixion — imagining himself as the Roman soldier with the whip. From the chaplain he hears of something new called Ludovico's Technique: two weeks of treatment and you walk out, never to offend again. The chaplain, who drinks, tells him what he really thinks: God wants goodness that has been chosen, and a man who cannot choose has stopped being a man.

What decides Alex's fate is a fight in his cell. The prison is overcrowded, another inmate is pushed in with them, they all set about him, and it is Alex who lands the blows that kill him. As it happens the Minister of the Interior is touring the prison — he wants cell space, because a different kind of prisoner will soon need it: political ones. He picks out the boy who has just killed again. Precisely because Alex is rotten all the way through, he makes the best possible demonstration.

The treatment is simple. Before each film he's given an injection; partway through, it takes hold and he goes cold and nauseated to the point of wanting to die. The films are all violence — beatings, rapes, and eventually Nazi newsreels. Dr. Brodsky explains that his body will learn to bind violence to this near-death sickness. Payoff ⑤ ↑Then one day the soundtrack to the Nazi footage is Beethoven, and Alex screams in the chair for them to turn it off — not because of the images, but because of the music. The boy who lay in the dark in Act One with Ludwig van playing and blood in his head now learns what it means that those two things grew together inside him: cut one out and the other comes with it. The doctor calls it regrettable — the line is hard to draw — and part of the punishment, presumably.

Two weeks later comes the demonstration. Officials and journalists fill the hall. An actor walks on stage and insults him, stamps on him, slaps him; Alex starts to raise a hand, the nausea floods back, and in the end he kneels and licks the man's shoe. Then a beautiful, nearly naked girl appears; he reaches for her by reflex, is driven back by the sickness, and stammers out a string of chivalrous compliments instead. Applause. The chaplain stands up to object: the boy is no longer a creature capable of moral choice; his goodness has been forced on him by fear. The official answer, more or less: we are not concerned with motive or higher ethics, only with cutting crime.

Alex asks a question from the stage, and it is the moment the book names itself: what about me — what am I in all this? Am I just to be like a clockwork orange?

Act Four · Everyone He Ever Hurt

Part Three: he goes home. His parents are awkward. His room has been let to a lodger called Joe, and his stereo and records were sold off by the police to compensate his victims. Joe treats him as the villain; his parents don't dare stand up for him. Alex goes back out into the street, alone.

He thinks about dying, and goes to the public library to look up painless ways to do it. Payoff ② ↑In the reading room an old man recognises him — the same man who came out of the library with his books in Act One and had them torn up and his teeth stamped on. He calls over a room full of old men, and they beat Alex, who can't so much as raise an arm to protect himself without retching. Those shredded books looked like nothing more than the first item on the night's list; only now do you see how carefully Burgess was keeping the account. He once chose the people least able to fight back; now he is the one least able to fight back.

Two policemen arrive to break it up. Payoff ③ ↑One is Dim. The other is Billyboy, the old enemy. Society's solution has been to recruit the toughest kids off the street into the police. The two boys who went at each other with chain and razor by the old power station now wear the same uniform, and the person they are going after together is the one who stood between them. The beauty of the move is that it needs no explanation: the state hasn't abolished violence, it has put violence on the payroll. They drive him out of town, beat him, and dump him by the road. It is pouring with rain.

He stumbles on until he sees a cottage with HOME on the gate. Payoff ④ ↑The writer, F. Alexander, takes him in — the gang wore masks that night, and he doesn't recognise him. His wife died not long after the attack. The typescript of A Clockwork Orange has since become a published book. Reading the papers, he realises this pitiful boy is a victim of the Ludovico Technique, and is thrilled: here is the perfect weapon against the government. The sentence Alex mocked and ripped up in Act One is now a word-for-word description of Alex himself — the living organism forced into the laws of a machine. And there is a second, crueller turn: the liberal writer who shelters him starts to treat him as a tool too. Alex and Alexander, two versions of the same name, are standing in the same room.

The writer's associates move Alex into a flat in town, promising to let him tell his story to the press. Meanwhile the writer grows suspicious: the way this boy talks is too much like someone he heard that night. Alex is locked in the flat; through the wall comes a symphony, louder and louder. The nausea rises like a tide and all he wants is for it to stop, for everything to stop. He throws himself from the window.

Act Five · Cured

He survives. He wakes in hospital in plaster from head to foot. The papers are in uproar: the government drove a boy to jump. While he was unconscious the doctors went back into his head and took the conditioning apart. The Minister comes to visit in person, photographers in tow, offering a good job and money, and mentions lightly that the writer has been put somewhere safe. A new stereo is wheeled into the room; it plays Beethoven's Ninth. Alex lies there listening, and sees the razors and the blood again. He was cured, all right — or so he tells us.

That's where the American edition ends, and where Kubrick's film ends. It closes on a cold irony: the evil has come back, and this time the state is on its side.

The British original has one more chapter. Payoff ① ↑Chapter twenty-one opens, once more, with "What's it going to be then, eh?" — the same question that begins each of the three parts begins this last chapter too. From page one it was just the gang's nightly catchphrase, a bit of street talk; Burgess makes it sound again at every turning point. When Alex is jailed it's put to a number; during the treatment nobody asks it at all; and in the final chapter, for the first time, it becomes a question Alex has to answer for himself.

Alex is eighteen. He has a new set of followers and still goes out at night, but he's getting bored. He has a job at the National Gramodisc Archives, and in his pocket he carries a picture of a baby clipped from a newspaper, without quite knowing why. One day he walks into a café and runs into Pete — the least conspicuous of the old four — respectably dressed, married, in a proper job, talking like a grown-up. On the way home Alex thinks: I want a son. He knows the son will probably do the same stupid things, like a wind-up toy marching straight ahead until it bangs into a wall, and nobody will be able to stop him; that's how the world goes round and round. He hasn't been cured. He's grown up.

Seen whole, the fuse of the book isn't the Ludovico Technique, or any single crime. The fuse is a fifteen-year-old handed over to two forces, neither of which intends to wait for him to grow up: on one side the appetite inside him that knows only pleasure, on the other a state that knows only statistics. The first made him a killer; the second ran an experiment on him; the writer tried to run a second one. Everything that follows — the torn books, the dismantled reflex, the fall from the window — comes from the same fact: nobody, Alex included, was willing to give him time. Chapter twenty-one simply lets time finish its stretch.

Three Slow-Motion Scenes

One · The Pinned Eyelids

What's terrible about the Ludovico sessions isn't what's on the screen — Alex did all of it himself in Part One, and narrated it with a grin. It's that the same narrating voice loses control for the first time. In Part One he was the director: he set the pace, and violence was a run of jokes in his mouth. In the screening room he's fixed to a chair; even his eyelids aren't his. Someone else cuts the footage; someone else picks the music. Burgess doesn't switch person or point of view. He just drains the swagger out of the voice, bit by bit: the sentences start repeating, then pleading, and real pain seeps into the slang. And the reader arrives at an awkward discovery: for dozens of pages you were laughing along with him, and now you're being tortured with him. The scene is about conditioning, but the reader is being conditioned too — into sympathy for someone you know doesn't deserve it.

Two · The Exhibit on Stage

The demonstration chapter turns on a beautifully judged mismatch of viewpoint. In the audience the officials, the doctors and the chaplain argue fiercely about what Alex is — a reformed citizen or an animal with its soul cut out — all in the third person, as if he weren't there. Meanwhile Alex is on stage, licking a stranger's shoe. The information arrives in a careful order: first the insults, so you see he can't hit back; then the naked girl, so you see that even desire has been taken from him, leaving only an empty patter of chivalry; and only then does he get to ask whether he's to be like a clockwork orange. Here the title steps out of a torn manuscript for the first time and lands on a living person. Burgess gives the right to name the book to the least qualified person in it. A murderer asks the question on behalf of everyone, and "but he's a bad man" is no longer a way out of it.

Three · Running Into Pete

Chapter twenty-one has almost no plot. No violence, no conspiracy, just a chance meeting. Its force is all in the drop. For twenty chapters a voice has been accelerating — beat, rob, caught, cured, beaten, fall — and now it abruptly slows into an eighteen-year-old in a café looking at an old friend and his wife and feeling slightly foolish. Burgess lets Alex's slang start to sound dated, even to him: he looks at his new followers as if at a bunch of silly kids. Choice doesn't return as a declaration. It returns as boredom — he loses interest in violence the way you lose interest in a piece of music you've heard too many times. Lots of readers find the chapter limp, even saccharine. But it's precisely because it refuses drama that it's the most thorough rebuttal of everything before it: everyone in the first twenty chapters wanted to fix Alex with a device, an operation, a campaign, and what actually worked was the least spectacular thing there is — time.

The Core Devices: Ludovico's Technique and Nadsat

The rule behind Ludovico's Technique is classical conditioning: pair a neutral stimulus (images of violence) again and again with one that already triggers a strong reaction (drug-induced nausea), until the first produces the reaction on its own. Pavlov's dogs drooled at the bell; Alex retches at violence. The logic has three links: (1) pairing; (2) a reflex forms; (3) the reflex replaces judgment. He doesn't believe hitting people is wrong; his body simply won't let him.

Which link holds? Aversion therapy was real. In the 1950s and '60s British and American doctors used emetic drugs alongside alcohol to treat alcoholism, and used the same method to try to "cure" gay men — now widely regarded as one of medicine's disgraces. The novel was written while such treatments were fashionable; its horror is not invented out of nothing. Even the detail that the music gets cut out along with the violence matches a real phenomenon: conditioned responses generalise to stimuli that are similar or that happen to be present.

Which link is there because the story needs it? Real aversion therapy had modest results, and conditioned responses fade — without continued reinforcement they weaken, and people learn ways around them. In the novel a two-week course produces a near-permanent reflex so precise that Alex can't even defend himself, and the doctors can later dismantle it cleanly in a matter of days. That's the needs of a fable, not a finding of psychology. Burgess wasn't writing a clinical report; he was building an extreme thought experiment: if there really were a way to make a person incapable of evil, would we want it? Make the technology perfect and the question comes out clean.

Nadsat is the second device, and it mirrors the first. Burgess said he built the slang on Russian roots partly because real street slang dates within a few years, while an invented one won't age, and partly because on a 1961 trip to Leningrad he saw the same kind of street youth there. The first edition had no glossary; he insisted readers should pick the words up from context. The result is that readers are trained by the language without noticing: by the end, horrorshow needs no translation. The novel condemns remaking a person through repetition and stimulus, and remakes its reader through repetition and stimulus. The difference is that you can shut the book whenever you like. Many critics have pointed this out, and it's the thing film could never reproduce: Kubrick could film the beauty of violence, but not the way a language slowly moves into your head.

One small detail is worth adding. The treatment is called "Ludovico," and Alex calls Beethoven "Ludwig van": the two names are forms of the same name in different languages. Burgess never points it out, but many critics doubt it's a coincidence — the thing that cures him and the thing he loves most are, by name, a pair.

What It Makes You Think About

One · Suppose a treatment could guarantee that a murderer never offends again, at the price of his ever choosing between good and evil himself. Do you sign the form?

The case for signing: think first about the writer's wife, the old woman with the cats, the two ten-year-old girls. What did Alex's "choice" bring them? Free will is a lovely phrase, but the bill is never paid by the person exercising it. Someone who has shown, over and over, that he will use his freedom to hurt people — why should society go on gambling on his right to choose? Besides, we shape people all the time: prison routines, school rewards and punishments, parental discipline — what are they, if not changing behaviour through consequences? Ludovico just does it more efficiently and more honestly. To the victims, a forced good man is ten thousand times better than a free bad one.

The case against: the question isn't whether Alex deserves it. It's that once you grant the state the power to rebuild people into machines that can't do evil, who defines "evil"? In the novel the Minister is clearing cells for political prisoners; today it's violence, tomorrow it's disobedience. More fundamentally, the treated Alex hasn't become good. He has become unable to do bad things — his desires are all still there, with a layer of nausea on top. He can't even defend himself. That kind of goodness belongs to no one. The victims don't get a remorseful offender, society doesn't get a citizen; all anyone gets is a safe instrument. Goodness is valuable precisely because it might not have happened.

Burgess is against signing. The chaplain's speech is close to his own view, and he said repeatedly in later essays that a man who chooses evil is more human than one who has good imposed on him. But he doesn't let that side win cheaply: he made Alex so vile precisely so that saying "don't sign" would cost you something. And the structure is not far from everyday life — electronic tags for people with records, chemical castration for sex offenders, algorithms that predict who will reoffend. Each time, we trade "he chooses for himself" for "the outcome is safe."

Two · He grew up, so he stopped. Does the life he took still count?

The case for "growing up is enough": chapter twenty-one rests on a plain fact. Most youthful violence fades with age; brains mature, people get bored, they want families. If that's true, maybe the best "treatment" for a fifteen-year-old is to avoid wrecking him and wait. Fourteen years inside, Ludovico, and political point-scoring only pile more violence onto him. Juvenile justice is separate from adult justice because society concedes that a fifteen-year-old is not yet the person he will finally be. Punish a child who would have come right on his own, and you are punishing someone he no longer is.

The case for "it still counts": but the old woman will never grow up. Nor will the writer's wife. What's most unsettling about chapter twenty-one is that while Alex is dreaming of a son, he barely thinks of them. "Growing up," in this chapter, is a natural process, like the seasons; it involves no remorse, no reparation, no facing the people he harmed. If maturity cancels crime automatically, justice becomes a waiting game: wait until the offender turns twenty and the slate is wiped. Why should a victim's family accept that the person who changed their lives just "went through a phase"?

Burgess clearly thought chapter twenty-one was the point of the whole book: without it the novel is a fable rather than a novel, because characters in novels change. But he also wrote it lightly — so lightly you can feel what's been left out. Closer to home: "everyone does stupid things when they're young," we say, and when someone's teenage posts resurface online, some say forgive and some say hold them to account. Where the line falls depends on whom the mistake hurt and how badly — and the novel deliberately chose the worst case there is.

Three · Your cause is right, but you need a victim as your banner. At that point, how different are you from the side you oppose?

The case for the writer: F. Alexander is the only person in the book who truly sees what Ludovico is. He knows that once the public accepts it, the next step is using it on dissenters; he knows the election is near and this is the only window to stop it. Weighed against the liberty of a whole country, what are one boy's feelings? And he himself is the greatest victim — his wife died of it — yet he takes this stranger in, bathes him and feeds him, which is already more restraint than most people could manage. Politics is never a choice between clean hands and dirty ones; it's a choice between a small sacrifice and a large disaster.

The case for "no different": but what he does is exactly what the Minister did — treat Alex as a sample that proves a thesis. The Minister wanted a successful exhibit; the writer wants a martyr hounded to death. Neither cares what Alex himself wants. And once the writer starts to suspect this boy is the one who came that night, his cause and his revenge become impossible to separate: the symphony turned up through the wall is just another Ludovico session. A man who opposes treating people as machines is starting the machine with his own hands.

Burgess takes neither side here. He lets both the government and its opponents place their bets on Alex, and lets both lose. The pattern turns up in every movement: a victim's story gets shared, retold, entered into evidence, and the person slowly vanishes behind the banner. Try asking yourself: the last time an issue moved you, do you remember the person, or the issue?

Through-Line: A Catholic's Lawsuit About Free Will

Burgess kept returning to one ancient argument, and often named it himself. Augustine held that humans are born with original sin and, without grace, will do evil; Pelagius held that human nature can turn to the good by its own will. His novels swing between those poles and refuse to close the case for either side. The Wanting Seed, published the same year, turns the argument into a pendulum of history: an overpopulated future England whose government cycles between a lenient phase that trusts human nature and a brutal phase that doesn't. A Clockwork Orange puts the same pendulum inside a single person. Alex in Part One is Augustine's evidence; the state in Part Two is a Pelagian engineer; and chapter twenty-one quietly casts a vote for Pelagius — people can come right on their own.

His other obsession was music. He thought of himself first as a composer, wrote symphonies, and late in life turned A Clockwork Orange into a stage play with music. That's why he made music the one pure thing in Alex — and then deliberately knotted it to violence: beauty doesn't guarantee goodness, a problem a composer set for himself. Later, Earthly Powers (1980) used the memoirs of an eighty-one-year-old gay writer to turn "does God permit evil so that we can choose?" into a vast novel, and 1985 (1978) — half commentary on Orwell, half Burgess's own dystopia — once again showed an individual swallowed by the system.

Controversies and Criticism

The violence is too seductive. The most common, and most serious, criticism is that the first-person voice, the playful slang and the chummy asides to the reader make rape and assault read like a party. Burgess himself later had some regrets, saying the book got him misunderstood and that he would rather not be remembered for it. Kubrick's film magnified the temptation: in the early 1970s British newspapers reported several crimes blamed on the film, and in 1973 Kubrick had Warner withdraw it from British distribution; it wasn't shown there again until after his death in 1999. Whether a work bears responsibility for its imitators is still unsettled.

Women are only bodies to be harmed. Almost every woman in the book is someone assaulted — the writer's wife, the two girls, the old woman with the cats — with no inner life and hardly a full sentence of her own. Some critics argue that this makes the novel's free-will debate a men-only affair: women never even get asked "what's it going to be then?" The film recast the two girls as apparently adult and near-willing; in the novel they are about ten, a difference that's often overlooked.

The chapter twenty-one fight. The American publisher dropped the final chapter in 1963; Burgess said he agreed because he needed the money. Kubrick said he only read that chapter when he had nearly finished the screenplay, and found it unconvincing and out of keeping with the rest. Many readers side with Kubrick: the twenty-chapter version is sharper, and the last chapter feels like a tepid moral patch. Others argue that without it the book collapses into a cynical joke about people being evil and unchangeable — the opposite of what Burgess meant. The complete text appeared in the US only in 1986, with an introduction by Burgess telling the whole story.

The scene drawn from Burgess's own life. Burgess said that in 1944 his first wife was attacked during the London blackout by several American deserters and later miscarried, and that the writer's-wife scene grew from this. The story comes mainly from his own telling, and biographers have treated its details with some caution. It is quoted constantly — sometimes as proof of the novel's "truth," sometimes to question an author turning a loved one's suffering into material. Both readings have their defenders.

Crossovers

  • Ethics · Aristotle on self-control versus virtue: the Nicomachean Ethics separates the self-controlled person (enkrateia), who wants to do wrong and holds back, from the virtuous person, who takes pleasure in doing right. Ludovico manufactures a forcibly self-controlled man: desire intact, nausea on top. Aristotle says we become just by doing just acts — but done by us, not drugged into us.
  • Psychology · Generalisation in the "Little Albert" study: in 1920 John Watson paired a white rat with a loud noise until an infant feared it, and soon rabbits and fur coats too. Alex's nausea at music is the same spreading. The difference: the novel's doctors foresee it and accept it.
  • Criminology · The age–crime curve: offending generally peaks in late adolescence and then falls. Terrie Moffitt distinguished a large "adolescence-limited" group from a small "life-course-persistent" one. Chapter twenty-one bets on the first; the weight of Alex's crimes looks more like the second.
  • Sociolinguistics · Halliday's "anti-language": in 1976 M. A. K. Halliday used "anti-language" for the argots of prisoners and gangs — same grammar, new vocabulary, building a small society set against the mainstream. Nadsat is exactly that, except a writer invented it rather than a group growing it.
