Novels, Deep-Read · Liu Cixin · No.37

Of Ants and Dinosaurs

Liu Cixin · novella 2004 / expanded novel 2010 · also published as The Cretaceous Past

中文 →

One afternoon in the late Cretaceous, a tyrannosaur opens its jaws as wide as they will go and then holds absolutely still.
A scrap of meat from three days ago is wedged between its teeth. It has gone bad, and the pain has kept the animal awake all night. There is nothing it can do: its fingers are as thick as half a tree trunk and will not fit inside its own mouth.
A column of ants climbs up over the lower lip and walks into a stinking room held up by two dozen white pillars, and begins carrying the meat out, piece by piece.
The beast does not close its mouth. The first civilization on Earth begins with that act of restraint.
Tens of thousands of years later, the same small hands will place bombs inside the skulls of that animal's descendants.

This page contains complete spoilers — why the ants decide to exterminate the dinosaurs, what happens during the one minute of Operation Cut the Brain, how the Cretaceous actually ends, and what two ants are saying three thousand years later. The premise of this site is that everything worth discussing lives past the ending. If you want to keep the suspense, read the book first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

In the late Cretaceous, dinosaurs and ants sign a contract that begins with a piece of rotting meat in a tooth — the dinosaurs supply the brains, the ants supply the only hands small enough to reach inside a machine or a body — and together the two species take the planet from stone tools to antimatter in fifty thousand years; then the ants, unable to tolerate what the dinosaurs are doing to the biosphere and to each other, use the access to dinosaur bodies they were granted for medicine to blow up five million dinosaur brains in a single minute. What they do not know is that the antimatter the dinosaurs have hidden hangs on a countdown that must be cancelled by hand every single day — and after that minute, nobody is left who can send the signal.

Coordinates

Liu Cixin (b. 1963), a power-plant engineer from Shanxi who became the author of the Three-Body trilogy. This book occupies an odd corner of his work: it is the one he wrote as a fable. It appeared first as a novella in 2004 and was expanded into a novel in 2010; in English it exists under two titles, Of Ants and Dinosaurs (Head of Zeus, 2020) and The Cretaceous Past (Subterranean Press, 2021, translated by Elizabeth Hanlon).

It runs in exactly the opposite direction from The Three-Body Problem. That book takes one specific human being and drops her into a cosmic scale. This one shrinks a cosmic argument down until it fits between two animals — one big enough to flatten a city with a foot, one small enough to crawl into the other's cerebral artery. English reviewers reach for Gulliver's Travels when describing it, and the comparison is exact: satire in the Swiftian line has always run on differences of scale.

Say the awkward thing up front: this is not Liu's best novel (the section on criticism gets specific). But it is the most naked specimen of the obsession he cannot leave alone — take a civilization apart, lay the pieces on the table, and show what actually holds it together and which screw it comes apart at.

The story

Act One · A piece of rotting meat, and a contract

The tyrannosaur in the opening is not cooperating with anyone. It is simply in too much pain to do anything else. And the ants climbing up its lip did not come to help; they came to eat. To them, that lump of carrion is a mountain, enough to feed the whole colony for a long time.

The turn comes when each side notices the same thing about the other: that thing is thinking. The ants realize that the mouth which could crush them into paste at any moment is being held open deliberately — the animal is enduring this. The dinosaur realizes that the black specks are not milling about at random; they are dividing labor, queuing, finishing a job.

And so the first interspecies agreement on Earth: the ants clean teeth, and are paid in food. The agreement keeps expanding after that — from dentistry to medicine, from medicine to surgery, and finally into the hands of an entire civilization.

It can expand because the two species are short in precisely complementary ways, almost comically so. The dinosaurs have brains capable of conceiving anything and hands incapable of making anything smaller than a fingerseed ①; an individual ant's brain barely deserves the word, but a colony can carry out operations of arbitrary precision, down to tying off a blood vessel. One side has ideas and no hands. The other has hands and no ideas.

Buried in that contract is a clause nobody thinks twice about. For the ants to practise medicine, the dinosaurs have to let them inside their bodies — mouths, wounds, organs, and eventually the skull.seed ② At the time it is a medical procedure. Over tens of thousands of years it becomes the most ordinary thing in the world, so ordinary that no dinosaur alive would think to review it.

Act Two · Fifty thousand years, from stone tools to antimatter

What follows is the fastest-moving stretch of the book: stone, bronze, iron, steam, electricity, atoms, information — and the figure the novel gives is fifty thousand years. Humans needed many times that. The reason this civilization sprints is the division of labor itself: the moment an idea exists, a tireless workforce with micron precision is standing by to build it.

The split hardens into institutions. The ants take over all of medicine — there are no dinosaur doctors in dinosaur hospitals, only colonies; surgery means tens of thousands of ants filing into a patient. The ants take over all precision manufacturing — circuits, wiring looms, the insides of instruments, every space a dinosaur finger cannot enter. The dinosaurs keep the other half: posing the problems, drawing the plans, building the large things, deciding where the world goes next.

Neither can survive without the other, and neither admires the other — in a way that is structural rather than personal. To a dinosaur, an ant is a useful tool, a consumable that walks to work on its own. To an ant, a dinosaur is a creature that burns enormous resources in exchange for feelings: dinosaurs have art, pride, love, and politics violent enough to start wars over a sentence, and to a species with no individuals, no private desires, a federation that functions like a single organism, all of that is simply waste.

And here is the most consequential line of the whole design: the ants can turn any drawing into an object, but they cannot produce a single new drawing.seed ③ Collective intelligence is superb at execution and optimization, and incapable of invention. Over fifty thousand years the ants physically build everything on the planet without originating any of it — and they are not troubled by this. A species without vanity does not experience that as a problem.

Act Three · Two empires, and an underclass that starts to be afraid

The dinosaur world splits. Two superpowers face each other across an ocean: the Gondwana Empire and the Laurasia Republic — named for the real Cretaceous supercontinents, which is Liu doing something quietly clever: the names alone tell you the stage for this standoff is the entire planet.

They fight for a long time, until the atomic age arrives. After that there are two hundred years without war, not because anyone grew wiser but because both sides have stockpiled enough thermonuclear weapons to turn the Earth into a sterile furnace. The balance of terror holds, and so does the peace. It reads like a photocopy of the Cold War, which is plainly the intention.

Something else is happening at the same time. Dinosaur numbers are exploding, and dinosaur appetite faster still. Forests go down stand by stand, seas turn, the planet wears thin. The dinosaurs cannot see this; the ants can — because the ants live in the soil, flat against the ground, and when an ecosystem starts failing it is always the smallest creatures who count the bodies first.

So the Ant Federation plays the one card it holds: a strike. They are the hands of this world, and when the hands stop, dinosaur hospitals, factories, power stations and missiles all stop with them. Their demand is not better pay. It is that the dinosaurs dismantle their nuclear weapons and stop stripping the biosphere — in today's language, a general strike by labor over the environment.

The dinosaurs answer with force. Killing ants requires no technology at all: a foot, a fire, a canister of poison. With ant cities erased in swathes, the Federation reaches a cold conclusion: at this table we have no leverage. We only have access. If they cannot be argued with and cannot be fought, one option remains — make the species cease to exist.

Act Four · Cutting the wire, cutting the brain

The ants' weapon is the thunder grain: a bomb the size of a grain of sand, which only an ant can install and only an ant can deliver. The plan has two halves, executed simultaneously.

Operation Cut the Wire — the novel's figures are a hundred million ants placing two hundred million thunder grains on the wiring inside dinosaur machinery. The insides of those machines were always maintained by ants anyway; all they have to do is leave one extra thing behind during routine work.

Operation Cut the Brain — twenty million ants enter the skulls of five million elite dinosaurs and fix thunder grains to the main cerebral vessels. The five million are not chosen at random: heads of state, scientists, engineers, the holders of critical expertise. The ants are not aiming at the dinosaur population. They are aiming at the dinosaur mind.

Stop here and look at how hard this lands.payoff ② ↑ Operation Cut the Brain is only possible because of the medical agreement from Act One. The ants' right to go inside a dinosaur body was granted by the dinosaurs themselves, renewed for tens of thousands of years, until it stopped being a permission and became a convenience, a benefit, a default nobody audits. That scrap of meat in a tooth created a civilization and a corridor into the other side's skull at the same instant. Liu does not invent a clever espionage mechanism; he simply runs the tender opening image backwards. The best seeded detail is never the hidden one — it is the one left in plain view the whole time, which you kept reading as good news.

One more thing happens while the thunder grains are going in. The ants' chief scientist, Qiaoye, plants listening devices alongside them, and so the ants learn the deepest secret of both empires: each holds a fragment of antimatter captured from interstellar space — Gondwana's is called Poseidon, Laurasia's Bright Moon — far beyond any nuclear weapon, and never disclosed.

What matters more is how they are stored. Both fragments sit in containers held in a permanent negative countdown: a clock always running down, which resets only on receiving a daily cancellation signal from a control station at home.seed ④ The designers' intent is transparent — decapitate us or paralyse us, and our doomsday weapon fires itself, so you die too. It converts mutual destruction from a promise into a machine.

The ants understand the mechanism. And they proceed anyway. This is the most fatal judgement in the book and also the most believable one, because it is exactly where the appeal of decapitation lies: it looks clean. One minute, five million heads, nobody else touched, a species lifted out of the driver's seat. Whatever is wired under that seat is a problem for afterwards.

Act Five · Nobody left to send the signal

The minute goes according to plan. Five million brains stop at the same instant; two hundred million thunder grains sever the nervous system of every machine in the dinosaur world. Cities go dark, communications drop, and a civilization that was discussing interstellar flight becomes, in sixty seconds, a heap of motionless steel and a great many bewildered giants.

And then somebody remembers the countdown.payoff ④ ↑ The control stations that transmit the daily cancellation, and the specialists who know how to operate them, were on the target list — the ants struck precisely at "the brain of the civilization," and sending that signal was one of the brain's daily chores. The small thing that had to be done once a day is now a thing nobody can do. Poseidon and Bright Moon go on counting quietly down toward zero, exactly as designed.

The war ends at that moment, faster than it began, because it has become pointless: nothing on Earth survives those two containers reaching zero. What follows is the most moving and most ironic stretch of the novel — two species that were exterminating each other an hour ago now throwing everything they have into saving each other.

And here those hands from Act One become the hinge a second time.payoff ① ↑ Repairing a control station and getting that signal out means somebody has to reach into the circuitry and splice it — and the dinosaurs cannot. They outsourced that capacity tens of thousands of years ago and never grew it back. The only hands that can save the dinosaurs are the hands that have just slaughtered them. "Fingers too thick," which was almost a joke in Act One, turns out to be the method of execution for an entire species: they are not killed by the blast. They are trapped by a thing they never learned to do.

They fail. Matter meets antimatter, and two annihilations set the sky on fire. The Cretaceous ends — not with an asteroid, but with a contract, a strike, a one-minute decapitation and a button nobody was left to press. The dinosaurs are gone, and a winter three thousand years long comes down.

The ending is a quiet conversation: three thousand years later, two ants talk about the empire that used to exist.payoff ③ ↑ The ants survived as a species and retained nothing as a civilization — they inherited a planet and could not inherit the half of the mind they never had; hands that can build anything, in a world with no new drawings, are just feet. Only now does that line from Act Two show its full weight: the ants did not lose the war. They won it, and then discovered they had no use for what they had won. At the end they guess that some new thinking creature will appear on this planet one day, and that it will have to be smaller than a dinosaur and larger than an ant. The reader knows who that is.

Put the five acts together and the fuse in this book is not the nuclear arsenal and not the antimatter. It is the meat wedged in that tooth. It produced three things at once: a civilization in which neither party could survive alone, a corridor into the other party's body, and two species each crippled in one half. Everything afterward is those three things coming due — the division of labor produces abundance, abundance produces waste, waste produces confrontation, and in confrontation each side reaches for its own half: the dinosaurs flatten ant cities with force, the ants take the corridor into the skull. They were not killed by each other. They were killed by the contract that made them a civilization.

Three slow-motion scenes

1 · The open mouth: a difference of scale written as trust

The craft in the opening is entirely in whose eyes the camera sits behind. Liu puts it at ant height: the teeth are pillars, the tongue is a range of hills, the rotting meat is a mountain. Then the camera pulls back and we see that all of this is inside a mouth, and the mouth belongs to an animal that could close it at any moment.

The scale change is not a trick; it manufactures a very particular tension. The reader knows how exposed the ants are, the ants know it too, and the giant is using endurance to say: I am not closing. An abstraction — trust — gets written as a muscle held motionless. That is worth more than any sentence about friendship forming, and it is what gives the massacre tens of thousands of years later somewhere to land: what gets betrayed at the end is not a treaty, it is that muscle.

2 · The minute of Operation Cut the Brain: a massacre performed in the posture of intimacy

This passage is built the way the Guzheng Operation is built in The Three-Body Problem: freezing engineering prose, enormous numbers, not one drop of blood. A hundred million ants, two hundred million thunder grains, twenty million entering skulls, five million elites — the figures carry the emotion here, because their precision tells you this was planned for a long time and planned like a construction project.

It is more disturbing than the Guzheng Operation, though, and the reason is posture. The Judgment Day was cut open from outside by a weapon. The dinosaurs are blown open from inside by invited guests, and the invitation was medical. The instrument of the killing is not a new tool; it is the old procedure that had always been used to express good will. That is the most wounding category of betrayal: it does not need to pick the lock, because you have been holding the door open yourself.

3 · The countdown nobody can stop: let reconciliation happen, then let it fail

Most apocalypse stories either run out of time before reconciliation or make reconciliation work. Liu takes a third road: the reconciliation happens — early enough, sincere, both sides at full effort — and it makes no difference whatsoever. The two species that were exterminating each other turn around and repair a transmitter together, ants in the wiring, dinosaurs on the scaffolding, nobody bringing up yesterday.

The passage works because it moves the question off "who was the villain." By this point there is no villain left, only a machine already running. The whole cruelty of the negative countdown is that it converts destruction from something a person has to decide into something a person has to prevent, daily. And the people who did the preventing were just eliminated by the ones they were preventing it for. The tragedy is not that someone did evil; it is that nobody is left in time to do the small thing that was always done.

The central device: an outsourced civilization

This book has no free-standing law like the dark forest. It has one device, pushed cleanly: split intelligence into two halves — the conceiving half and the doing half — hand each to a different species, and watch what grows.

What holds up. The foundation is sturdier than it looks. Collective intelligence in ants is a real phenomenon: with no central command, following simple local rules, colonies accomplish genuinely complex engineering — nest construction, foraging-route optimization. "Many simple units producing complex behavior" is not a literary invention. Dinosaur forelimbs really were unsuited to fine manipulation. Sturdier still is the layer above: a system that assigns design to one party and manufacturing to another is enormously efficient and automatically generates a hostage relationship — you do not need science fiction for that, only a look at any transnational supply chain.

What is there because the story needs it. Label it honestly: this is a premise, not biology. Real colonies do not perform surgery, read, write, negotiate or install listening devices. Equating collective intelligence with an industrial workforce you can subcontract is Liu's invention — the one invention the book requires. And two civilizations arising in the Cretaceous was never meant to satisfy paleontology; this is a stage, not a hypothesis.

The negative countdown deserves its own note. Hanging a doomsday weapon on a clock that must be cancelled every day is counterintuitive engineering: the first principle of real nuclear command systems is preventing accidental launch, not guaranteeing it. But the idea is not pure invention either. During the Cold War the Soviet Union built a semi-automatic retaliation system known as Perimeter — "Dead Hand" in the Western press — whose logic was exactly this: make retaliation independent of whether the leadership is still alive. Liu pushes that logic to its limit and then asks a real question: when you remove the human veto in order to make deterrence credible, have you made the world safer, or handed its switch to a clock nobody is watching?

What it makes you think about

Question 1
Splitting "thinking" from "making" and subcontracting one of them: accelerator, or noose?

Take the case for it to its strongest. Division of labor is the source of every prosperity humans have ever had, without exception. This civilization covers stone tools to antimatter in fifty thousand years precisely because each capacity is pushed to its extreme: no dinosaur wastes an hour practising fine motor skills, no ant wastes a cycle on meaning. Any system that demands everyone be competent at everything ends up slow and mediocre. The screen you are reading this on was designed on one continent and built on another. Rejecting the split means rejecting modern life.

Now take the case against it further than its supporters usually do. The price of the split is not lost efficiency; it is atrophy, and atrophy is not reversible. The dinosaurs are not "temporarily out of practice" — after fifty thousand years they have no concept of how the fine work is done. The ants are not "temporarily not inventing" — the species no longer has the organ. On the day you need to save yourself, you discover the missing half cannot be improvised. What you outsource is capacity; what comes back is dependence.

Bring it home. The loudest development of the past few years is humans outsourcing the "making" at scale — code, copy, images, accounts. One concrete question first: if ten years from now your ideas are as good as ever but you can no longer build any of them, are you stronger or weaker? And the mirror image is just as sharp: if you get better and better at completing other people's drawings while producing fewer of your own, which one are you in this system — the dinosaur or the ant? The book gives no answer. It gives an ending: the two halves die together.

Question 2
When the other side really is destroying the shared home, and really cannot be reasoned with — is decapitation the only rational move?

The ants' case is close to airtight. They do not start with knives. They warn, they negotiate, and then they mount the most powerful non-violent action available in that world: a global general strike. The reply is ant cities erased. Every peaceful instrument has been tried and has failed, and the failure proved something further: within this structure of power they will never obtain leverage that changes the outcome. Meanwhile the biosphere is visibly going down and the nuclear standoff could slip into a real exchange any day. Trading five million lives for an entire planet is an arithmetic every utilitarian calculus answers the same way. Every revolutionary and every resister in history has walked this same line of reasoning.

The case against sits somewhere else. Decapitation always rests on one buried assumption: that the decapitated system will stop quietly. Complex systems never do; they go on running in ways you never read about. And the ants knew about the negative countdown — they simply judged it a problem for later. That is not ignorance; it is the error every actor makes: thinking in fine detail about the part you control, and thinking fast about the part you don't. More fundamentally, the ants indict the dinosaurs for letting one species decide for the whole planet, and then decide for the whole planet.

The structure is everywhere at ordinary scale: replace the entire management team to save the company, overthrow a broken institution to save a country, delete the legacy code nobody understands to save the system. The question is never "should we act." It is "have you seriously worked out what the part you are cutting out is quietly doing for you."

Question 3
Hanging destruction on a clock that runs by itself: the safest design, or the most dangerous?

The case for it is hard to dismiss: deterrence only works while it is credible. If your adversary believes one successful first strike removes your ability to answer, the strike becomes worth attempting and deterrence evaporates — which is exactly where the temptation to decapitate comes from. Handing retaliation to a machine that does not depend on the leadership being alive announces in advance that killing us buys you nothing, so nobody tries. A great deal of why the Cold War stayed cold runs on this logic. There is even a mercy in it: make the machine irrevocable and the humans never have to fire.

The case against is equally hard, and this novel is its demonstration: such designs change the default for destruction from "does not happen" to "happens." From then on, what keeps the world in existence is not anyone's restraint but a small action correctly performed every single day. Human organizations always, eventually, miss the small action — war, coup, system failure, epidemic, one bad shift handover. You have protected the credibility of your deterrent by betting the world on never making a mistake, indefinitely.

This is the one that transfers most directly into ordinary life, because "inaction triggers it" mechanisms are all around you: auto-renewals, opt-out defaults, clauses that execute on expiry, automated processes nobody reviews, the script you forgot about that still runs monthly. Worth asking occasionally: how much of my life is only not happening because I remember to stop it every day?

Question 4
Is a civilization that only realizes, and never originates, a civilization at all?

The ants' claim is stronger than it first sounds. Every physical object of Cretaceous civilization — every circuit, machine and city — was made by ant hands; no dinosaur ever touched one. If making is authorship, this civilization was theirs from beginning to end. And the ants are not vain, do not tear themselves apart internally, do not go to war over pride; they are efficient, restrained and ecologically responsible. Measure "civilized" by almost any index and the ants do not come out behind. Why should inventing be the criterion that counts?

The other side holds too, and the ending stands on its side. A system that cannot pose a new question is a tool rather than a subject, however exquisite it is. After three thousand years of winter, the ants have inherited a whole planet and continued nothing — not because the technique was lost, but because in a world with no new drawings a perfect pair of hands has nothing to do. The proof is that closing guess: they hope some creature between a dinosaur and an ant in size will come along. Hoping someone else will do the thinking is itself the admission.

The question is unusually sharp right now, because authorship is being redistributed: for the first time, the party with the idea and the party that builds it need not be the same kind of thing at all. When you hand over the making, you may believe you are handing over labor when you are handing over the credit; when you only do the making, you may believe you are keeping the skill when you are keeping the seat. Liu's answer lives in the structure rather than in dialogue: he makes the two halves inseparable and then kills them together. He does not think there is a winner here. He thinks the answer to this problem is that the split was the mistake.

The motif Liu Cixin keeps writing

Lay his books side by side and the same obsession keeps surfacing: at a large enough scale, does the morality we are used to still hold? The Three-Body Problem asks whether an enemy four hundred and fifty years away justifies surrendering freedom today. This book rotates the scale: not vastness in time but disparity in body. When two parties do not even feel pain at the same order of magnitude, can they understand each other at all?

What deserves more attention is the line of reasoning this book shares with that one. Before Ye Wenjie presses the reply key, she reads a book about how pesticides kill their way up the food chain, and her conclusion is: this species is poisoning the entire biosphere and is incapable of correcting itself, so an outside force has to intervene. The Ant Federation reasons in exactly the same words, except that this time the outside force is themselves. And both books deliver the same verdict: the intervener believes they are saving the world and has only moved the destruction forward. Liu is plainly both sympathetic to this reasoning and deeply suspicious of it — again and again he sends his clearest-eyed character up that path and then makes the world pay for the clarity.

The other thread is inheritance. In Supernova Era, every adult on Earth has a year to live and the entire technological civilization must be handed to children under thirteen — the problem is not that children are stupid, it is whether a civilization can be handed to people who do not understand it. This book is the extreme version of that problem: the heirs not only fail to understand it, they have just killed the teachers. What he writes about is never "who destroyed the world"; it is whose hands the machine is in, and whether they know what they are holding.

Criticism, honestly

No boosterism: within his body of work this book sits in the lower half, and the criticism is fair.

  • There are no characters. Even the named ones are little more than talking viewpoints, which is close to consensus among English-language reviewers. The Three-Body Problem at least gives you Ye Wenjie and Da Shi. Here you finish the book remembering "the ants" and "the dinosaurs" and not one individual of either. A fable is allowed to skip characters, but the price is that nothing in the book makes you fear for a specific fate.
  • The argument is stated too plainly. The environmental warning and the disarmament case are largely spoken outright, at a didactic density closer to books for young readers — several English reviews classify it as light and well suited to a younger audience, and that judgement is accurate rather than unkind. Liu at his best hides the idea inside a device and lets you collide with it; here he often just tells you.
  • The first half reads like a chronicle. "Fifty thousand years from stone tools to antimatter" is a long stretch of era-advancing summary with low narrative density — more development brief than novel. The real fiction starts at the strike, the decapitation and the countdown, which is to say the best of the book is its last third, and getting there takes some patience.
  • Casual biology. Ant cognition and manipulation are inflated enormously, and two Cretaceous civilizations have nothing to do with paleontology. The author never intended otherwise — but if you came for hard SF, know in advance that this one is not hard.
  • Contested political readings. In the English-speaking world it is popular to read the book as an allegory of contemporary geopolitics; the South China Morning Post review is headlined as a satire about the United States and China. To be clear: that is a reviewer's reading, and the novel names no real country — the two empires are named after ancient supercontinents. Stating the verifiable facts here, and passing no judgement.
  • Which text is "the book." The 2004 novella and the expanded 2010 novel are not the same length, and English has two differently titled editions, so readers discussing this book are not necessarily discussing the same text.

Ten lines to take away

1. The first civilization on Earth began with a giant refusing to close its mouth — the first brick is restraint, not cleverness.

2. Dinosaurs had minds and no hands, ants had hands and no minds; together, stone to antimatter in fifty thousand years; apart, neither can live.

3. The best seeded detail is not the hidden one but the one left in plain sight that you kept reading as good news — the ants' access to dinosaur bodies was granted by the dinosaurs.

4. What makes Operation Cut the Brain terrible is not the body count but that the instrument was not a new weapon: it was the old procedure that had always meant good will.

5. The negative countdown changes destruction's default from "does not happen" to "happens": the world now exists only because someone remembers to stop it daily.

6. Decapitation always assumes the decapitated system will stop quietly. It will not.

7. The ants did not lose that war. They won it, and then found they had no use for what they had won.

8. What you outsource is capacity; what comes back is dependence — the dinosaurs were not killed by the blast but trapped by a thing they never learned to do.

9. The most moving passage is two species that had just tried to exterminate each other repairing a transmitter together, too late. Reconciliation happens, and does not help.

10. Three thousand years later two ants guess that the next thinking creature will be smaller than a dinosaur and larger than an ant. The book is a letter to that creature, signed: the Cretaceous.
