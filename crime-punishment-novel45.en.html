Deep Read · Fyodor Dostoevsky · No.45

Crime and Punishment

Six parts and an epilogue · Fyodor Dostoevsky · serialized 1866 in The Russian Messenger

中文 →

Petersburg in July, hot as a kitchen with the windows shut, the canal giving off a smell like chalk and rot. A student who has dropped out of law school comes down from his attic, trying not to wake the landlady.
He has counted the steps: from his door to that door, seven hundred and thirty. Today he carries no axe. He has come to look — how dark the stairwell is, whether the old woman puts the chain on before opening, whether his hands will shake in front of her door.
He calls this walk a rehearsal.
Two days later he takes the same seven hundred and thirty steps with an axe hanging in a loop he has sewn into the lining of his coat.

This page spoils the novel completely — who he kills, how the second victim walks in, the fact that the police never hold a shred of physical evidence, what finally makes him talk, whether he ever repents (he does not), and the illness, the dream and the riverbank in the epilogue. You know the murderer by page seventy; the suspense was never who, it was how long he can hold. Without the ending there is no way to discuss what the book is actually asking. If you'd rather find it yourself, read the novel first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

A half-starved, half-mad ex-student who believes he may be entitled to be an exception kills a moneylender with an axe, then kills her harmless sister, who walks in at the wrong moment; for five hundred pages nobody can produce evidence against him, and what finally walks him into the police station is his own body, a girl selling herself to feed her family, and a magistrate who investigates the man instead of the scene; he confesses, serves eight years, and to the last pages still does not think the killing was wrong — only that he wasn't the sort of man who could carry it.

Coordinates

Fyodor Dostoevsky (1821–1881). In 1849 he was sentenced to death for belonging to the Petrashevsky Circle, a group of young men who read banned books and argued about serfdom; he stood blindfolded on the scaffold until the reprieve arrived at the last moment, and then served four years of katorga — penal labour — at Omsk, where the one book he was allowed to keep was a New Testament. This is not background. Every character he wrote afterwards was written by a man who had already been executed once and then sent back.

Crime and Punishment ran through 1866 in The Russian Messenger. In the same year a brutal publishing contract forced him to deliver a second novel; he hired a stenographer, Anna Snitkina, dictated The Gambler in twenty-six days, and married her the following year. The book was written between epileptic seizures and visits from creditors.

Its place in literary history is easy to state: this is the founding text of the psychological novel, and it is also a detective story written backwards. The murderer acts in Part One; the reader knows who and how from the start; so the entire tension moves elsewhere — not who did it, but what it takes to live inside what you did. Every novel since that puts us behind the criminal's eyes, and every interrogation written as psychological combat, is walking a road this book cut.

The story

Act One · A room like a coffin, and a letter

Rodion Romanovich Raskolnikov, twenty-three, has left law school because he cannot pay for it. His attic is too low to stand up straight in; the book keeps calling it a cupboard, a coffin. He owes rent, avoids his landlady, barely eats, barely speaks to anyone. He is not the kind of poor man who has gone numb — he is far too clever, and it has been a long time since anyone contradicted anything inside his head.

He goes to pawn his father's silver watch. The moneylender, Alyona Ivanovna, charges savagely, lives off everyone in the district who has run out of options, and bullies the meek half-sister who shares her flat, Lizaveta — a large, soft woman who agrees with everybody. He memorises each step at that door: the chain, the lock, the light, the stairs.

On his way home he ducks into a tavern and meets Marmeladov, a dismissed clerk five days into a drinking bout, who dissects himself in public for an audience. He has lost his post and drunk the last of the household; his wife Katerina Ivanovna is consumptive, keeping three small children and the last shreds of her respectability alive on nothing; and his daughter by his first marriage, Sonya, has taken out a "yellow ticket" — the certificate issued to registered prostitutes in imperial Russia, which struck your name off the roll of respectable persons — so that the family can eat. The first evening she came home with money, she put it on the table and lay down with her head covered for the rest of the day. Then Marmeladov asks the question the whole novel turns on: what becomes of a person when there is nowhere left in the world for them to go?

At home a letter from his mother is waiting. His sister Dunya is to marry Luzhin, a middle-aged lawyer — respectable, going places, and, crucially, willing to give her brother a leg up. Raskolnikov reads the marriage instantly: his sister is selling herself, and the price is his career. Sonya has a yellow ticket, Dunya will have a marriage certificate; to him they are two editions of the same transaction. The idea he has been turning over for half a year stops being an idea and becomes a plan: the money that could save them is locked in the box of an old woman who has ruined people all her life.

He remembers a conversation he overheard in a tavern not long before. A student was telling an officer: the old woman is vicious and stupid, the money will rot in her hands; kill her, spend it on a hundred young people who are going under, and it isn't a crime, it's arithmetic — one life against a hundred, the ledger is clear. The officer asked: so will you kill her? The student laughed and said of course not, I was only talking. seed ①

Then comes the dream. He is seven, walking past a tavern with his father. A drunk peasant named Mikolka insists that his skinny, spent old mare pull a cart full of people. She can't. He whips her, then beats her with the shaft, then takes up an iron bar and kills her in front of a laughing crowd. The child tears free of his father's hand, throws his arms around the bloody muzzle and kisses it. seed ② He wakes soaked in sweat: am I really going to pick up an axe?

That evening in the Haymarket he happens to hear someone tell Lizaveta something: tomorrow at seven she will be out. He climbs back to his attic feeling that the choice has been taken from him — even the hour has been set for him.

Act Two · Seven hundred and thirty steps, and someone who was not in the plan

The preparation is frighteningly precise. He sews a loop into the lining of his coat so the axe hangs flat under his arm. He makes a dummy pledge: a smooth block of wood wrapped with a strip of iron, tied crosswise, tied hard — so that she will have to bend over it and pick at the knot, and the longer the better. seed ③

The first step goes wrong: he means to take the axe from the landlady's kitchen and the servant is in the kitchen. He stands on the stairs almost persuaded that heaven has forbidden it. Downstairs the porter's lodge is empty and an axe is lying under the firewood. Afterwards he will call this fate helping him — the last time he ever feels the world is on his side.

Seven hundred and thirty steps. The old woman questions him through the door for a long time before opening it. He hands over the dummy pledge; she turns to the window to pick at the knot. He draws the axe and brings it down with the blunt side — he had thought even this through: the blade is too quick, the blood would come back at him. She makes no sound. He takes the keys from her neck, opens the box, grabs a purse and a few trinkets.

And then the door opens. Lizaveta has come home. She sees her sister on the floor, sees the axe, and does not scream or run; she only lifts one hand in front of her face, the way a child does when it is about to be hit, and backs away. He kills her with the blade. payoff ① ↑ The tavern arithmetic is void in that instant: there was never a term in his equation for Lizaveta — she lends nothing, harms nobody, and is the most abused person in the building. She is the one who is in every grand calculation and in nobody's sums, and she came through the door a few minutes early. Dostoevsky does not let him fail; he lets him succeed: the plan works perfectly, at the cost of one extra body, and that body cancels the reason for the whole thing.

Then the tightest ten minutes in the book. Someone is pulling the bell outside — a client come to redeem a pledge, then a neighbour. The door is on its inside hook; they see that something is wrong and run down for the porter; he slips out in the gap, ducks into an empty flat on the floor below where painters are working, and walks out once the men have gone upstairs. The axe goes back under the firewood.

His first act of "disposal" is to shove everything he took under a stone in a courtyard and press it down. He never counts it. He never spends a kopeck of it. payoff ③ ↑ That dummy pledge, engineered so exactly to make her bend her head, and this stone make a matched pair: the murder was committed by an actuary, the disposal by a sleepwalker. The motive he had advertised — money for people who need it — has already given itself away here.

Then a police summons arrives. He goes in a state of terror and finds that it is about an IOU for unpaid rent. As he is exhaling, the officers at the next desk start discussing the murders at the pawnbroker's: two women, one axe. He faints on the floor.

Act Three · A patient, and one act of charity

He is ill for days, nursed by his one friend Razumikhin — the only person in the novel who is simply decent, strong and inexhaustible. As soon as he can walk he wanders out, runs into Zametov, a police clerk, in a cheap restaurant, and starts discussing the murders with him in a joking voice: and what if it was me? He even offers advice on where a murderer ought to hide the goods. This is the first time he walks toward the fire on purpose — not to confess, but because he cannot hold it in. The loneliness of being the only person who knows what he did has begun to kill him.

Then he walks into a street accident: Marmeladov, drunk, has been run down by a carriage. He helps carry the man home and watches the family bury him in a room full of screaming; Katerina coughs blood while arranging the funeral, and Sonya, still in the clothes she works in, kneels by her father. On the way out he leaves every rouble he has — some twenty of them, just sent by his mother, while he himself is going hungry — on the windowsill. seed ④

Going down the stairs he feels suddenly, absurdly alive. A man who has just murdered two people, having done one foolish and useless kindness, feels for a moment that he is still among the living. Then his mother and sister arrive in Petersburg. He sees them, says a few words, and faints again.

Act Four · An interrogation that never visits the crime scene

Enter Porfiry Petrovich, the examining magistrate: plump, smiling, endlessly circling. The first meeting comes about because Razumikhin brings his friend to ask about a pawned watch — the most natural pretext in the world. Porfiry immediately turns the conversation to a magazine article published six months earlier. You wrote a piece called "On Crime", didn't you?

So Raskolnikov has to lay out his theory in front of everyone. People fall into two natural classes. The ordinary exist to reproduce, obey and keep the world running. The extraordinary — the ones who have actually moved history — have the right, in their own conscience, to step over obstacles, including over blood, if their new idea requires it. If Newton's law could only be published at the cost of ten lives, he would have had a duty to spend them; Napoleon walked up over a few hundred thousand corpses and got a statue. What Raskolnikov wants is not forgiveness. It is a licence.

Porfiry, still smiling, asks two questions. The first: before a man acts, how does he know which class he belongs to — what if he has miscalculated? (Ordinary people never imagine themselves extraordinary, comes the answer; that is the filter.) The second is buried in small talk: on your last visit to the pawnbroker, were there painters at work in the empty flat? The painters were only there on the day of the murders. He swallows the answer just in time. Porfiry produces no evidence in this meeting because he has none. All he wants is for the man to know that he is being looked at.

A few days later a stranger, a tradesman, walks up to him in the street and says one word: murderer. (He is the witness who was hiding in that empty flat and saw him ring the bell, and has not dared be certain.) In the same weeks Svidrigailov arrives — Dunya's former employer, who made advances to her, whose wife Marfa Petrovna died in circumstances people gossip about, and about whom the countryside says worse things still. His wife left Dunya three thousand roubles; he has followed her to Petersburg with money of his own, to try once more. There is something in him that lowers the temperature of a room: he never defends himself; there is simply nothing he cannot do.

The second interview happens at the bureau. Porfiry keeps him waiting a long time in the anteroom first — that is step one of the interrogation — then circles, then suddenly says it: psychology is a stick with two ends, it proves nothing on its own; but on the night of the murders you went back to the flat, rang the bell and felt the wall for blood. Why? He drives him to the point where only confession or collapse is left, and says there is a surprise waiting behind the door. The door opens — and in bursts Nikolai, the house painter, whom everyone calls Mikolka, who falls on his knees and shouts that he did it.

The confession is false. Nikolai belongs to a schismatic sect (a branch of the Old Believers, some of whom hold that suffering itself washes guilt away); he picked up a jewel case dropped in the stairwell, panicked, and has decided to take the whole crime and the suffering with it. Worth a pause: the peasant who beats the mare to death in the dream is called Mikolka, and the painter who steps forward to be punished for a murder he did not commit is also called Mikolka. One is the man swinging the bar, the other is the man begging for the sentence. The novel never comments — and both of them are halves of Raskolnikov.

Porfiry has to let him go. In that gap he goes to Sonya, sits in her crooked, ill-shaped room and asks her the cruellest questions he can find: what has God done for you? What happens to the children the day Katerina dies? Then he makes her read him the raising of Lazarus from the Gospel of John. A murderer and a prostitute, bent over one guttering candle, reading a book about a dead man walking out of a tomb — it is one of the most famous scenes in nineteenth-century fiction. On his way out he tells her: tomorrow I will tell you who killed Lizaveta.

He does not notice the empty room next door. seed ⑤

Act Five · Two kinds of lie, and a confession

First Luzhin. Dunya has broken the engagement and he wants revenge: at Marmeladov's memorial dinner he makes a show of giving Sonya ten roubles, slips a hundred-rouble note into her pocket while nobody is looking, and then announces that the money is missing. The girl is about to be dragged off as a thief when his own lodger Lebezyatnikov — a self-declared progressive — stands up and says he watched Luzhin put the note there. The frame collapses on the spot. Luzhin is the control group: he and Raskolnikov run the same arithmetic — a person may be used as a means — and the only difference is that he does not dare use an axe, only a banknote.

That night, in Sonya's room, Raskolnikov says it: I killed her. He says he did it for the money, and hears himself deny it; for his mother and sister, and denies that; to be a Napoleon. What is left at the bottom is the true version: he needed to know whether he was a trembling louse or a man with the right — and the only way to find out was an experiment, and the experiment had to be run on a living person. He also says the line that could serve as the book's subtitle: I did not kill the old woman, I killed myself.

Sonya's first reaction is not fear but a cry of grief: what have you done to yourself. Then she holds him and says they will go and serve the sentence together, and tells him what to do —

Go now, this minute, stand at the crossroads, bow down, kiss the earth you have defiled, then bow to the whole world and say aloud: I have killed.— Sonya, Part Five (paraphrase of the Russian, not a verbatim quotation)

She takes the cypress-wood cross from her own neck and gives it to him, to put on the day he goes.

When the confession is over, a man steps out of the room next door: Svidrigailov. payoff ⑤ ↑ To get near Dunya he had rented the room adjoining Sonya's, and heard every word through the wall. The stroke pays off twice over. The only person in the novel who holds anything over Raskolnikov turns out to be the one man with no interest in morality — he will not go to the police, he only wants to trade the secret for Dunya. And that scene of absolute intimacy had a third party in it all along. Even confession is not private.

Meanwhile Katerina breaks. Turned out of her lodgings, she dresses the three children up and drives them into the street to sing and dance for coins, shouting at passers-by about her family and the academy for young ladies she attended; she collapses coughing blood and dies in Sonya's room, refusing a priest — she has been squeezed dry, and God has no further claim to make on her. The funeral is paid for by Svidrigailov; the orphans are placed by Svidrigailov; the money that lets Sonya follow a convict to Siberia is Svidrigailov's too. The most charitable person in the book is the worst man in it — Dostoevsky deliberately refuses to let good people and good deeds line up.

Act Six · A bullet, and a man on his knees

Porfiry's last visit is voluntary. He sits down, apologises, and says calmly: you are the murderer. He admits he has nothing usable — Nikolai's false confession is still sitting on the case — but says he will get it, and urges the man to give himself up. The legal argument first: a confession will cut the sentence, and cut it a great deal. Then the heavier one: what you need now is air, and suffering; take the consequence and there is still something to be done with you, refuse it and all you have left is running. On his way out he adds one piece of appallingly considerate advice: if you should decide on the other solution, leave a note saying which stone the things are under.

Svidrigailov's last day runs alongside this. He lures Dunya into his locked room and uses her brother's life as leverage. She takes out Marfa Petrovna's old revolver and fires: the first shot grazes his scalp, the second misfires. He walks toward her and asks whether she could ever love him. Never, she says. He hands her the key and lets her go. It is the one time in his life he does not take what he wants — and it is at that moment that he runs out of reasons to be alive.

That night he gives money away in every direction: a sum to Sonya, a sum for the orphans, a sum for the sixteen-year-old girl he is engaged to. Then he takes a room in a damp hotel and dreams of rats, of rising water, of a five-year-old girl whose face turns into a prostitute's. At dawn he walks to a fire-watch tower, tells the fireman on duty that he is off to America, and shoots himself.

The same morning, Raskolnikov says goodbye to his mother. She asks nothing — she has read "On Crime" in the magazine and half-guessed something she does not dare finish thinking. He kneels for her blessing, puts on the cypress cross, and walks toward the police station.

Crossing the Haymarket he goes down on his knees, presses his forehead to the filthiest ground in the city, and kisses it. payoff ② ↑ People laugh; one says he is drunk, another that he is a pilgrim setting off for Jerusalem. He gets up, and the sentence — I killed — does not come out of him. At seven he threw himself on a beaten mare and kissed her face; then he became the man with the iron bar; five hundred pages later he kneels and kisses the earth. One gesture runs the length of the novel: from pitying a creature, to destroying one, to needing to make peace with something that cannot speak before he can face people at all.

Inside the station he is received by the lieutenant nicknamed Gunpowder, who chats away warmly and mentions a piece of news: Mr Svidrigailov shot himself yesterday morning. Raskolnikov listens, turns and walks out — he is going to walk away. In the yard he sees Sonya standing there, white-faced, watching him. He turns round, climbs the stairs again and says, in a flat voice: it was I who killed the old woman and her sister Lizaveta with an axe.

Epilogue · Siberia: he still does not think he was wrong

The sentence is eight years of second-class penal labour — lighter than anyone expected. The court establishes a pile of facts that contradict his theory point by point: he confessed; he never touched the stolen money; he had kept a consumptive fellow student alive out of his own pocket and looked after the man's old father after he died; he had once carried two children out of a burning house. payoff ④ ↑ The roubles on the windowsill were the same kind of act. What ends up shortening his sentence is precisely the "useless softness" his theory would have cleared away — the world that judges him does not run his equation; it weighs the part of him he despised.

His mother does not live to hear the verdict; she dies of an illness nobody dares tell her the truth about. Dunya marries Razumikhin. Sonya follows him to Siberia, takes in sewing, writes letters home for the convicts, and within months everyone in the town knows her.

The hardest thing to sit with is what happens in prison: he does not repent. He audits the ledger over and over and concludes that the ledger was right and he was the error — a real strong man would not have run a fever and fainted; he is not Napoleon, only a louse with aesthetic pretensions. He even despises his own confession as weakness rather than conscience. The other convicts hate him for reasons he cannot follow, call him a godless man, and one day nearly set on him. All of them love Sonya; when she appears, hats come off.

The turn comes in the second year, in Lent, when he falls ill in the prison hospital and dreams of a plague out of Asia: the infection is a microscopic creature with a will of its own, and everyone it enters becomes unshakeably certain that he alone possesses the truth. No one can be persuaded, no one can compromise; people kill each other in gangs, armies turn on themselves halfway to the front, fields go unsown, towns burn. At the end a handful of people are left who were meant to begin a new humanity, and nobody has ever seen them. It is the first time his theory appears in front of him in epidemic form — not one man claiming the exception, but everyone claiming it at once.

One morning after his recovery he is sitting on the riverbank looking across at the steppe, where time seems to have stopped somewhere back in the age of Abraham. Sonya comes and sits beside him, and he suddenly falls at her knees and weeps. The book is careful here: two pale, wasted, sick faces already lit by the dawn of a new life. The New Testament under his pillow — the one they read Lazarus from — he has still not opened. Later, he thinks. Seven years left. Only seven years.

Take the six acts together and the fuse of this novel is not poverty. Poverty is the fuel and his mother's letter is the match, but the fuse is the article he himself wrote and published six months earlier — once a man has set down "I may be an exception" as a respectable proposition, the proposition starts demanding evidence. Everything after that is the blast wave: the axe, Lizaveta, the loot under the stone, the taunting of a police clerk in a restaurant, the two conversations with Porfiry, the knees on the Haymarket cobbles. He did not kill for money. He killed to get a data point for a sentence.

Three slow-motion replays

1 · Inside the door: locking the reader into the murderer's body

Someone is yanking the bell outside. He is standing inside with the axe still in his hand and two bodies at his feet. The bell rings once and stops; rings again; then knocking, swearing, an eye at the crack. He stands still, hearing his own heartbeat, hearing the hook shake in its socket.

The technique here is compressing all information down to sound: we are never told what he thinks, only what he hears. The reader's ear is strapped to his, and something morally awkward happens — you want him to get away. That is not a slip, it is the design: put the reader physically on the murderer's side first, then spend five hundred pages asking why they went there so easily. And notice the contrast in tempo. The killings themselves are fast and dry — a few lines, one movement, no sound — while these ten minutes are stretched almost to real time. The act is instantaneous; the consequences are slow. The architecture of the whole novel is demonstrated in a single scene.

2 · The tavern monologue: a drunk dissecting himself for you

Marmeladov's speech is the passage readers skim, and it is the best demonstration of what Dostoevsky can do that nobody else could. He is not an object of the author's judgement; he is his own prosecutor — a father who pushed his daughter onto the street and then asks her for drinking money, and who describes this more savagely than any bystander would dare. This is what Mikhail Bakhtin called polyphony: characters are not loudspeakers for the author's opinion, each holds a complete voice capable of defending itself. The reader is stripped of the right to feel contempt — you cannot think of a single reproach he has not already made. And the passage is structural: it puts up two versions of self-sacrifice (Sonya, then Dunya in the letter) before the hero supplies his version, which is the sacrifice of somebody else.

3 · The knees on the Haymarket: one gesture instead of a chapter of argument

Sonya tells him to kneel at the crossroads and kiss the earth. He does it — and then the sentence does not come out, and the crowd laughs at the drunk.

This is the most finely judged moment in the book. Had he knelt, cried out, and had the crowd fallen silent, the novel would be ruined — it would become a tract. By putting the most sacred gesture in the filthiest market and giving the onlookers the most vulgar interpretation, Dostoevsky protects its weight: it is not a ceremony performed for others, it is his first partial peace with himself. It is also the peak of the book's rule that the body moves before the idea does. This is a man who lives entirely inside his own reasoning, and the novel keeps letting his body vote first: fevers, faints, loss of control, the inability to stand up in a police station. Dostoevsky does not believe anyone is saved by thinking their way through. He believes people move first and understand afterwards what they have done.

Taking the idea seriously: does the theory hold?

The novel's central device is an essay growing inside it — Raskolnikov's "On Crime". It deserves to be taken apart as though it were a real proposition.

Step one is a description of fact, and it holds. History is full of people who broke the law first and wrote it afterwards: revolutionaries, founders, reformers who smashed an order. A good deal of what is now in constitutions was once a capital crime. Calling this step a fallacy would be dishonest.

Step two is the substitution, and the whole problem starts here. Sliding from "they stepped over, and posterity ratified it" to "therefore a class of people has the right to step over" skips a decisive difference: ratification is retrospective and granted by others; permission is prospective and issued by yourself. Napoleon's statue was given to him by history, not handed to him before he acted. What Raskolnikov wants is a pass issued in advance — which converts "the winners are not judged" into "I expect to win, so I am not judged now."

Step three asks who issues the licence. His screening test is psychological: the genuinely extraordinary do not doubt themselves. The comedy of this is that it rewards exactly the people with too much confidence — under that rule, the only candidates who pass are megalomaniacs and tyrants, because anyone capable of agonising over a killing is automatically filed as ordinary. Porfiry's "and what if you have miscalculated?" is not a debating point; it is the one fatal interface in the system.

Step four is dismantled by the novel itself: the experiment is invalid. The sample is contaminated — he did not act coolly, he acted feverish, starving, minutes after reading his mother's letter. There is an uncontrolled variable: Lizaveta. And most damning, he never uses the result: the money stays under the stone and not one kopeck reaches the people he claimed to be saving. A man genuinely testing whether he can step over does not flinch from counting the loot. He was testing something else, and he got his answer: he cannot.

The honest boundary: the book does not prove the theory false, it shows what happens when one specific man tries to execute it. His collapse refutes "he is one of those people"; it cannot refute "such people exist". Plenty of twentieth-century figures ran this logic at scale and slept soundly for fifty years. What the novel offers is not a disproof but a warning: the danger of the theory is not that it is wrong, but that it recruits first among precisely the people who should never hold it.

There is a second device worth naming: the inverted detective story. The reader knows everything from the start and the police never obtain physical evidence — Nikolai's false confession even derails the case. So the pressure of "solving" it moves entirely into the psychological register, and Porfiry does only one thing: make the suspect convict himself in his own mind. That structure is not a trick, it is the theme: a book about conscience can only be built this way.

What it makes you think about

Question 1
What is wrong with "one life for a hundred" — and what if nothing is?

Take this side at its strongest first. The student's ledger is not absurd: the old woman is elderly and ill, lives by squeezing people who have nowhere else to go, beats her sister, and the money will rot in a monastery's fund for lamp oil after her death; meanwhile the same city contains hundreds of young people going under for want of a few dozen roubles. If the point of morality is to reduce suffering, the trade shows a profit in pure numbers. This is not sophistry, it is standard utilitarian reasoning, and it is what public-health rationing, collateral-damage estimates in war and crash-priority rules for autonomous vehicles all run on. Refusing the arithmetic also has a price: insisting that no one may be used as a means often means watching more people be harmed.

The novel's rebuttal is not a sermon; it is a character. Lizaveta walks in. Every calculation with human lives as terms has the same structural flaw: you write the equation, but you do not choose who shows up. There is no real-world option marked "only one person dies" — only the option you believed was that. And one layer deeper: the arithmetic requires converting people into comparable quantities, and once that conversion is permitted once, there is no principle stopping the next one — and you will not be the person deciding who goes into the denominator next time.

Here is the distinction that usually gets skipped: "what does this calculation conclude?" and "who is entitled to make it?" are two different questions. Nearly all argument gets stuck on the first, while the second decides the outcome. You can accept that ICU beds must be allocated by some calculation and still insist that the sum be done by a public procedure, auditable and reversible. In Raskolnikov's version, the same man writes the equation, executes it and judges it. That concentration, not the arithmetic itself, is where the answer goes wrong.

Question 2
He confesses but does not repent. Which of the two did we actually want?

This is the most uncomfortable and most honest thing in the epilogue: a man can turn himself in, serve the full sentence, accept every consequence — and never for one minute agree that he was wrong.

The case for not requiring repentance, at its strongest: the law can only demand conduct. You harmed someone, you carry the consequence, the account is settled; demanding inward surrender as well adds a thought-audit on top of the punishment. Besides, remorse cannot be verified, and forced contrition only rewards the best performers — every society that has practised public self-criticism knows this in its bones. Further: a person who obeys in conduct while dissenting in private is the baseline setting of a free society. We do not actually want everyone to agree in their heart with every law.

The other side is just as strong: a sentence served without repentance is only a payment. Eight years becomes a price, and the crime becomes something purchasable — Lizaveta's life converted into an interval of time. There is also a safety problem: a man who thinks he merely miscalculated will calculate better next time. Sonya's route — kiss the ground, then say it aloud in front of everyone — insists on the body and on publicity precisely because what she wants is not a price but a person rejoining the human race.

In ordinary life the question is: do you accept an apology that says "I'm sorry, but I don't think I did anything wrong"? Would you rather work with someone graceless who paid the damages and changed their behaviour, or someone who wept convincingly and did it again? Dostoevsky's answer is deliberately unsatisfying — his hero never says the word, only weeps once and thinks: later. He believes change happens; he does not believe a verdict can force it out of anyone.

Question 3
Is Sonya's kind of redemption an answer, or one more sacrifice?

At its strongest: in the novel's contest of ideas, the thing that actually punctures the theory is not Porfiry's logic but Sonya's existence. She is the counter-example to the arithmetic — by any "who is more useful" standard she belongs in the column to be cleared away: uneducated, propertyless, a registered prostitute. And she keeps a family alive, holds a murderer's secret that he cannot look at himself, and follows him to Siberia. What she demonstrates is not the cheap proposition that love redeems, but a harder one: worth does not come from a ranking of capabilities. People are rarely argued out of anything; they are quite often pulled out of the water by one specific person.

The other side must also be put fully: the entire cost of this solution is carried by an eighteen-year-old girl. Her sanctity is built on her having been destroyed first — the yellow ticket comes before the candle and the Gospel. Writing the suffering woman as the hero's channel of atonement is a long and questionable tradition, and this is one of its most influential instances. She is given almost no desires, plans or future of her own; the novel never once seriously asks what Sonya wants, and her ending is one form of giving herself away exchanged for another.

In life the shape of this is very plain: every family and every team has the person who always absorbs it. Everyone benefits, everyone is grateful, everyone says it would fall apart without her — and it is exactly that gratitude that ensures nobody ever asks what kind of life she would have chosen. Calling someone a saint is the most respectable way of excusing her from being an ordinary person with claims of her own.

Question 4
Does suffering have value in itself — or is that an alibi for whoever inflicts it?

Porfiry tells him to go and suffer; Nikolai takes another man's crime in order to suffer; the epilogue sets the resurrection in a labour camp. The book does argue that bearing the cost is what turns a person back into an agent. There is hard support for this: an apology that costs nothing is empty, and it is only after carrying the consequence that something stops being "a thing that happened to him" and becomes "a thing he did". The whole practice of restorative justice is built on that intuition.

The counter-case must be put at full strength, because it is among the most serious criticisms of Dostoevsky: suffering by itself produces nothing. What produces meaning is a person, and only a few of them, in unusually lucky conditions. The direct consequence of sanctifying suffering is that it supplies a ready-made defence for whoever is causing it — this is good for you. That logic has been used to underwrite despotism, poverty, corporal punishment and every version of "hardship builds character", with the bill paid by people who never had a choice. Note also: Dostoevsky came back alive from a scaffold and a penal colony, so he has standing to say it. Almost everyone who quotes him does not.

Bring it home: when you tell yourself that a bad stretch must mean something, that is usually courage — a way of standing up in something that hasn't ended yet. When you say the same sentence to somebody else, be very careful — it may be comfort, or it may be the thing that lets you feel at peace about not overturning whatever is hurting them.

The obsession Dostoevsky kept rewriting

Line his novels up and the same fixation appears: can an idea kill someone? Not metaphorically — he genuinely believed that a thought reasoned all the way through grows limbs and goes looking for a body to carry it out.

The man in Notes from Underground spends his life objecting to one thing: that two times two is four. He would rather have toothache, rather ruin himself, than concede that human behaviour can be computed — that is the overture to Crime and Punishment, except that he only dares rebel on paper. In Demons, a circle of young men execute a theory of liberation all the way to murdering one of their own. The Brothers Karamazov completes the chain: Ivan works out in his study that if there is no God everything is permitted, and the man who acts on it is Smerdyakov, the servant who listened to him talk. The one who formulates the idea and the one who executes it are not the same person — that is what Dostoevsky was really afraid of; Raskolnikov is unusual only in playing both roles himself, which is why he collapses alone.

The second thread is his diagnosis of too much consciousness. The sickest people he writes are the cleverest, and nobody is ever rescued by a better argument — only by a specific, unreasonable human being: Sonya, Prince Myshkin in The Idiot, Alyosha in The Brothers Karamazov. He spent a career writing one sentence: a person can think everything through and then be completely unable to live.

Criticism, honestly

Without flattery, the book has some solid weaknesses, and the objections are fair:

  • The conversion in the epilogue is bolted on. Five hundred pages establish the man's pride as airtight; then in a few final pages an illness, a dream and a bout of weeping deliver a new life — and the text says outright that he still has not opened the Gospel. Nabokov, in his lectures on Russian literature, rated Dostoevsky very low and was especially scornful of the murderer-and-harlot-over-the-eternal-book manner, which he considered cheap sentiment. The defence: six hundred pages had already written the man's body — the fevers, the faints, the nightmares, the knees on the cobbles — as a verdict delivered ahead of his ideas, so the epilogue is not a turn but an admission. Which concedes that the change was announced late rather than dramatised.
  • Too many coincidences. He happens to overhear the "one life for a hundred" conversation; happens to hear that Lizaveta will be out; Svidrigailov happens to have rented the room next to Sonya's and happens to be there on the night of the confession. The defence — this is hysterical realism, not probabilistic realism — is real, but as a technical matter it remains a flaw.
  • It runs close to melodrama. People shriek, kneel, faint and cough blood in the street; the planted banknote at the funeral dinner, the mad performance on the pavement, both carry the excess of nineteenth-century serial fiction. Readers who prize restraint will wince.
  • The women. Sonya is the most famous instance of the holy-prostitute type; Dunya is close to flawless; both function mainly as coordinates in the hero's moral situation and are given few purposes of their own. The usual defence points to Katerina Ivanovna — proud, cruel, boasting about her finishing school as she dies — who genuinely is one of the most complicated figures in the book.
  • Its polemical streak. The novel was written in the middle of the Russian radical ferment of the 1860s and hits back at the fashionable "rational egoism" of the day; Lebezyatnikov, the progressive, is close to caricature. Critics reasonably call this a straw man: the actual opponents were not that stupid.
  • The author's own positions. The later Dostoevsky was an open Slavophile, monarchist and Orthodox partisan, and his Writer's Diary contains antisemitic passages — a verifiable fact. The novel itself carries a trace: at Svidrigailov's suicide the fireman on duty is written as a caricatured Jew with an accent. Stated as fact, without further judgement here.
  • Translation matters more than usual. English versions run from Constance Garnett's smooth Victorian prose to recent translations that stay closer to the Russian and keep its awkwardness and repetition; the hero's incoherent monologues come out considerably more or less unhinged depending on which you read. A large part of what readers call "Dostoevsky's voice" is a translator's voice.

Ten lines to take away

1 · This is not a story about a poor student killing for money. It is about a man killing to obtain evidence for a sentence he had written about himself.

2 · The decisive moment is not the axe coming down, it is Lizaveta opening the door — every equation with lives as terms contains a person who is not in your sums.

3 · The claim that the money was for saving people confessed itself the moment he shoved the loot under a stone and never dared count it.

4 · Porfiry never has physical evidence. He does one thing only: make the suspect convict himself internally. That is where structure and theme become the same object.

5 · The theory does not fall because exceptional people don't exist; it falls because the licence is self-issued. Retrospective ratification and prior permission are a whole civilisation apart.

6 · At seven he kisses a beaten mare; later he becomes the man with the iron bar; at the end he kneels and kisses the earth. One gesture carries the entire novel.

7 · The most charitable character is Svidrigailov, the worst man in the book. Dostoevsky deliberately refuses to make good people and good deeds coincide.

8 · What shortens his sentence is exactly the "useless softness" his theory would have eliminated. The world judging him does not run his equation.

9 · Confession is not repentance. He serves eight years and concedes only that he wasn't strong enough — never that it was wrong. That is the most honest and least comfortable thing here.

10 · The dream of the plague is the theory in its final form: not one man claiming the exception, but everyone certain at once that only they hold the truth — and then killing each other over it. It reads more like the news now than when it was written.

Deep Read · The story is all here; the questions are yours — for the weight of the sentences themselves, go to the novel.
