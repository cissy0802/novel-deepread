Novels, Deep-Read · Nikolai Gogol · No.125

Dead Souls

Nikolai Gogol · 1842 · Part One in eleven chapters · fragments of Part Two

中文 →

A light bachelor's carriage, neither new nor old, rattles into the provincial town of N and pulls up at the inn.
Two peasants in the doorway of the tavern opposite pay no attention to the passenger. They are looking at the wheel. If it went to Moscow, says one, would it get there? It would, says the other. And to Kazan? No, not to Kazan.
The gentleman inside is not handsome and not ugly, not too fat and not too thin. You could not call him old, and you could not call him young.
His arrival causes no stir in the town at all. That is his greatest talent.

This piece contains full spoilers: what the gentleman is buying, how a drunken shout at a ball gives him away, how the whole town ends up wondering whether he is Napoleon, and where the troika on the last page of Part One is heading. The book's meaning rests on the late answer to "who is this man?", so there is no discussing it without going all the way.

In One Line

A courteous, presentable, entirely unobjectionable middle-aged official goes from estate to estate in the Russian countryside offering to buy the landowners' serfs who have already died but are still alive on the government's register. One by one he closes his deals, each landowner more absurd than the last, and what the book finally exposes is not one swindler but a whole country of living dead souls.

Where It Sits

Nikolai Gogol (1809–1852) was born into a family of minor gentry near Poltava in Ukraine and wrote in Russian. He made his name with Evenings on a Farm Near Dikanka, a collection of Ukrainian village tales thick with devils, then wrote the comedy The Government Inspector (1836) and the Petersburg stories "The Nose" and "The Overcoat." Dead Souls is his only novel. Part One appeared in 1842; by Gogol's own account, the idea came from Pushkin.

He gave this prose book an odd label: poema, a long poem. The plan was three parts, often compared to Dante's Inferno, Purgatory and Paradise. Only the Inferno was ever finished. He worked on Part Two for a decade, and in February 1852, in the grip of a religious crisis and a self-imposed fast, he put the manuscript in the stove. He died about ten days later. What survives of Part Two is a handful of draft chapters assembled afterwards.

The Story

Act One · Into Town: A Man Who Is Just Right

The gentleman is Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov, a collegiate councillor, sixth rank in the civil service table. He travels with two servants: Selifan, the coachman, who likes a drink and lectures his horses on the road; and Petrushka, the valet, who loves to read without caring what, enjoying only the way letters keep turning into words. Foreshadowing ①The peasants' idle remark about the wheel is dropped on the first page like two passers-by a painter dabs into the corner of a canvas. Nobody is going to care how far that wheel can go.

Chichikov spends over a week in town doing exactly one thing: paying calls. The governor, the prosecutor, the police chief, the postmaster, nobody is missed. He tells the governor that the roads of his province are like velvet; the governor, at the time, is embroidering on tulle. Whatever the topic, he goes precisely as deep as is pleasant: on horses he knows horses, on taxes he knows taxes. He never talks about himself, and when he does, it is only to call himself an insignificant worm who has suffered much in the world.

By the end of the week the town has reached a verdict: a thoroughly decent, thoroughly delightful man. Even the landowner Sobakevich, who speaks well of nobody, calls him pleasant. At the officials' dinners Chichikov meets several landowners from the surrounding district and accepts their invitations. That was the whole point of coming.

Act Two · Manilov and Korobochka: One Too Sweet, One Too Scared

First comes Manilov. His garden holds a little summerhouse with a green dome and sky-blue columns, inscribed "Temple of Solitary Meditation." At first glance he seems amiable; at second glance, still amiable; at third glance you wonder what on earth he is actually like, and then you can't think of anything at all. On his desk lies a book with a bookmark at page fourteen, where it has been for two years. On the windowsill, the ash from his pipe has been knocked out into neat little rows of mounds. Eight years married, he and his wife still pop pieces of apple into each other's mouths. He dreams of building a stone bridge across the pond with little shops on it. All his plans live permanently in the mood of "wouldn't it be nice if."

After dinner, behind the closed door of the study, Chichikov lowers his voice and states his business: he would like to buy some dead peasants. Manilov's pipe falls to the floor and his mouth stays open. He searches his guest's eyes for signs of madness and finds them clear and calm. Not literally dead, Chichikov explains, but those still alive in the eyes of the law: souls who have died since the last census and are still on the books.

Here we have to stop and explain the rules. In serf-owning Russia, a landowner's male serfs were counted as "souls." Every so often the state held a census, called a revision, and entered each estate's male serfs on a list. Between revisions the list did not change, and the landowner paid a poll tax on every name on it, including the ones already in the ground. To a landowner, a dead serf was a liability he was still paying for; to Chichikov, he was an identity card nobody had cancelled. Manilov wants to know only whether this is legal and whether it might harm Russia's future. Reassured, he is moved to tears: he will give them away, and pay the transfer duty himself. As the carriage pulls away, he is already imagining the two of them living side by side, talking philosophy by the river, until the Tsar hears of their friendship and makes them both generals.

On the way to the next estate the rain comes down. Selifan, drunk, drives into a muddy field and tips the carriage over, and Chichikov lands in the mud. In the dark they stumble on a house belonging to a widow named Korobochka, a surname that means "little box." Her clock, before it strikes, hisses like a nest of snakes.

When they talk business, Korobochka is not in the least shocked by the word "dead." She is afraid of only one thing: Foreshadowing ②selling too cheap. Eighteen of her men have died, but she has never sold a dead man before and has no idea of the going rate. Perhaps dead peasants might come in handy about the house? Perhaps another merchant will come along next week and offer more? She keeps trying to sell him hemp, or honey, or lard, anything but the dead. Chichikov sweats with rage, privately calls her a blockhead, and finally lies that he buys for the government and will be back for her honey. Only then does she let the eighteen go for fifteen paper rubles.

Act Three · Nozdryov, Sobakevich, Plyushkin

At a roadside tavern Chichikov runs into Nozdryov. Gogol calls him a "historical man": wherever he goes, some history happens, whether he is carried out of a meeting or beaten up by his friends. One of his side-whiskers is thinner than the other, because in his last fight someone pulled out a fistful. He lies for no reason; he simply cannot stop. He drags Chichikov home to show him his dogs, his horses and the boundary of his land: everything on that side is mine, and everything on this side is mine too.

Chichikov makes a mistake. Foreshadowing ③He mentions the dead souls. Nozdryov is delighted, refuses to sell and insists on trading: the souls for his stallion, his barrel organ, a dog. Then he wants to stake them on a game of checkers, cheats by nudging the pieces with his cuff, and when he is caught he orders his servants to beat his guest. Just as the fists go up, a district police captain walks in: Nozdryov, a few days earlier, got drunk and thrashed a neighbouring landowner with birch rods, and is wanted in court. Chichikov slips out in the confusion and spends the whole ride cursing himself: how could he have told this to a man who cannot hold his tongue?

On that flustered road his carriage tangles with a handsome coach drawn by six horses, and the two coachmen trade insults. Foreshadowing ④In the coach sit an old lady and a blonde girl of about sixteen, her face as clear and luminous as a freshly laid egg held up to the light. Chichikov stares for a long time. Only after the coach has gone does his mind swing back onto its usual track (now, if this girl had a dowry of two hundred thousand…). But the stare was real.

Next comes Sobakevich. He looks like a medium-sized bear, and his first name is Mikhail, which is what Russians traditionally call bears. Everything in his house is solid, heavy and like its owner: the walnut bureau on four legs is a perfect bear; the walls hold portraits of Greek independence heroes, every one with astonishingly thick thighs. He announces that every official in town is a crook, except the prosecutor, who is decent, "though, to tell the truth, a pig."

He is the only one in the book who understands at once and names a price before he is asked. You want dead ones? A hundred rubles apiece. But they're dead, Chichikov protests. Foreshadowing ⑤So Sobakevich starts praising his dead: Mikheyev the carriage-maker, whose carriages never broke; Stepan Probka the carpenter, strong enough for the Guards; Milushkin the bricklayer, who could build a stove in any house; Maxim Telyatnikov the cobbler, who could make a boot with one jab of the awl. He talks as if death were a minor blemish that didn't affect the merchandise. At this point it reads as a comic sales pitch. They settle at two and a half rubles a head, and he slips in a woman's name, spelling "Elizaveta Vorobey" so it looks like a man's.

Sobakevich mentions a neighbour named Plyushkin, who owns some eight hundred souls that are dying like flies. Chichikov goes straight there. A peasant he asks for directions has never heard the name, only a nickname: "the patched one."

Plyushkin's estate is a study in rot. The roofs leak, half the windows are boarded up, and the garden has grown into a forest with a beauty found nowhere else in the book. In the yard stands a figure in a robe that could belong to a man or a woman, a bunch of keys at the waist; Chichikov takes it for the housekeeper and addresses it as "mother." It is the master. Indoors lie a lemon shrivelled to the size of a hazelnut, a clock whose pendulum has stopped, and a yellowed toothpick that may have been used before the French entered Moscow. Every day he roams the village picking up whatever he finds: an old sole, a shard of tile.

He was not always like this. He was once a shrewd, hard-working householder with a wife, a son and two daughters, and neighbours came to him for advice. His wife died and he grew suspicious; a daughter eloped with a cavalry officer and he cursed her; his son joined the army, lost at cards and wrote for money, and got not a kopeck. The tighter he gripped, the more it rotted: grain turned to earth in the barns, cloth turned to dust, and he was still out collecting broken tiles. A man who saved everything ended up with nothing. He has a hundred and twenty dead souls and seventy-odd runaways, which he sells kopeck by haggled kopeck. He offers his guest an Easter cake his daughter brought years ago, now hard as stone, and a liqueur from which he first fishes out the bugs.

Act Four · The Millionaire and the Rumour

Back in town, Chichikov has collected nearly four hundred souls. On paper he will "resettle" them in the southern province of Kherson, then being opened up with land almost free for the taking, and then mortgage these paper peasants to the Board of Guardians, a state institution that lent money against serfs at two hundred rubles a soul. Buy at a few rubles, borrow at two hundred: four hundred souls would be enough to make him a real landowner.

That night at the inn he spreads out the lists to copy them, and partway through he stops. Payoff ⑤ ↑The names start coming alive. Probka the carpenter must have fallen from a church cupola he was working on. Maxim the cobbler probably drank himself to death. Abakum Fyrov, one of Plyushkin's runaways, may be hauling barges on the Volga right now, singing with the other haulers. The narrator's voice blends with Chichikov's and turns tender and sad. When Sobakevich boasted of his dead, it was a sales pitch; when Chichikov sits alone with the list, it becomes a requiem. The same gesture, listing a dead man's trade, is business the first time and elegy the second, and only the second time do you see that these "souls" were people.

The next day the deeds are registered at the civil court, stamps fly, and a celebratory dinner is held on the spot. Word goes round: Chichikov has bought hundreds of serfs; he is a millionaire. The ladies begin to talk about him; the officials worry about whether so many peasants being moved into the steppe might riot on the way.

The governor gives a ball. As soon as Chichikov enters, the ladies surround him like a bright garland. He is basking in it when he sees, beside the governor's wife, Payoff ④ ↑the blonde girl from the collision on the road. She is the governor's daughter, just home from boarding school. Chichikov goes to pieces. He spends the whole evening hovering beside her, talking about things she neither understands nor cares about, and leaves the room full of ladies standing. The ladies remember. The collision had read like a travel episode, a flicker of humanity in a con man; here it becomes a fuse. The one time in his life he lets feeling override calculation is the time he loses the goodwill of every woman in town, and everything he had in that town was built on goodwill.

At that moment Nozdryov bursts in. Payoff ③ ↑Half drunk, he yells across the room: isn't that the Kherson landowner? The one who buys dead souls? He says it in front of the governor, and everyone hears. The curse Chichikov muttered on the road now comes due; Gogol had planted the explosive in the least reliable hands available. A man who never tells the truth tells the only true thing in the book, and because he never tells the truth, people half-believe him, which is exactly what makes the rumour grow so wild.

The next night a battered carriage shaped like a fat watermelon rolls into town carrying Korobochka. Payoff ② ↑Back home she grew more and more uneasy. Fifteen rubles: had she been cheated? The first thing she does in town is ask around: what's the going price for dead souls these days? Her fear of "selling too cheap" in Act Two looked like a bit of rustic comedy about a stingy old woman. Here it becomes the second leg that carries the scheme into every ear in town. A carefully built con is taken apart by a madman's mouth and a widow's abacus, one after the other.

The news reaches two ladies, whom Gogol calls "the simply pleasant lady" and "the lady pleasant in all respects." In a drawing room they knit the threads together: the dead souls are a cover; what he really wants is to carry off the governor's daughter! Within half an hour the town has split into two parties. The women believe in the abduction; the men cling to the dead souls.

The men's theories grow. He forges banknotes; he is a secret inspector sent from Petersburg, which terrifies officials whose hands are all dirty. They gather at the police chief's house to confer, and the postmaster slaps his knee and launches into "The Tale of Captain Kopeikin."

Kopeikin lost an arm and a leg in the 1812 war against Napoleon. Back home with no way to live, he hobbles to Petersburg to petition for a pension. He waits and waits in a great official's anteroom, is told each time to come back in a few days, and watches his money vanish in taverns. When he finally makes a scene, he is escorted out of the capital. A few months later a band of robbers appears in the forests of Ryazan, led by none other than Kopeikin. Chichikov, the postmaster announces, is Kopeikin! Someone points out that Chichikov has both arms and both legs. The postmaster smacks his forehead and calls himself a calf. Someone else suggests he might be Napoleon, escaped from St Helena.

The prosecutor is so shaken by all this that he goes home, thinks and thinks, slumps in his chair and dies. Only then do people realise, with regret, that the deceased had a soul after all; he had simply been too modest ever to show it. The book's sharpest "soul" belongs to a living man: only at the moment of death is he proved to have been alive.

Chichikov, meanwhile, has been shut in his room with a swollen cheek and knows nothing. When the swelling goes down and he calls on the governor, the porter says he has orders not to admit him. Officials cross the street to avoid him. In the end it is Nozdryov who drops by and cheerfully tells him every rumour in town, then offers to help him abduct the governor's daughter for a loan of three thousand rubles.

Act Five · Who Is He?

Chichikov decides to leave at once. But Selifan reports that the horses need shoeing and Payoff ① ↑a wheel needs a new iron rim; the smiths, seeing his hurry, dawdle and triple the price. When he finally sets off, he meets a funeral procession coming the other way: the late prosecutor. He draws the curtain and peeks out from behind it.

Only now, as the carriage clears the town gate, does Gogol tell you who this man is. Chichikov was born into impoverished gentry. When his sickly father took him to school in town, he left him one piece of advice: please your superiors, make no friends, and above all save, kopeck by kopeck, because money is the one thing that never lets you down. The boy remembered. At school he hid the treats classmates gave him and later sold them back. He was the best-behaved pupil in the school, because the teacher loved obedience above all. When that teacher later fell into poverty and the boys took up a collection, Chichikov contributed a coin or two.

He entered government service as the lowest clerk. He courted his superior's daughter, called the old man "papa," and once the promotion came, never set foot in the house again. He sat on a commission to build a government building that never rose above its foundations in years, while every commissioner built himself a fine house; when that came out, he lost his post. He moved to the customs service, became famous as a ferocious incorruptible, and then went into business with the smugglers, driving flocks of "Spanish sheep" across the border with double fleeces and Brabant lace hidden in the inner layer. He made a fortune, was denounced by his partner after a drunken quarrel, and lost everything. Every time he started again from zero, a little cleaner, a little more patient, a little more respectable.

After his last fall he worked as an agent arranging the mortgage of an estate where half the serfs had died in an epidemic. He worried that the Board would refuse the loan. The clerk shrugged: dead or not, they're on the census list, aren't they? One offhand remark lit up the rest of his life.

Here the narrator turns to the reader. You probably don't like this hero. True, he is not a virtuous man; but virtuous men have been written to death, that poor horse has been ridden long enough, and it is time to harness a scoundrel. He is merely an "acquirer." Then comes a ruder question: which of you, alone at night, dares to look inward and ask whether there isn't a little Chichikov in you too?

Out on the open road, Chichikov tells Selifan to drive faster. What Russian doesn't love a fast ride? Mileposts, woods and villages fly backward. And the narrator's voice suddenly lifts from Chichikov's carriage to the whole country: Russia, aren't you too a troika that no one can overtake? Other nations stand aside to let you pass. Part One ends on a question: "Rus, where are you flying? Give an answer. She gives no answer" (Part One, end of chapter eleven). On the first page two peasants asked whether a wheel would make it to Kazan; on the last, the narrator asks where the whole carriage is going. From idle talk about a wheel to the fate of a nation, and neither question is answered.

If you had to gather the whole chain of cause and effect into one fuse, it is not the father's advice, and not any of the landowners. It is the census list: a state that sees its people only on paper, and only looks every few years, so that the dead can go on living on paper, paying tax, being sold and mortgaged. Chichikov invents nothing. He simply reads the rules more carefully than anyone else. Five landowners, a ball, a prosecutor's death: all of it is the aftershock of that list. Where people exist only on paper, the dead become a business, and the living start to die along with them.

Three Slow-Motion Shots

One · "Mother"

Plyushkin's entrance is the most carefully delayed in the book. First a nickname ("the patched one"), then a village whose roof beams stick out like ribs, then a garden gone wild and strangely beautiful, and only then the figure in the yard that could be man or woman. The point of view stays with Chichikov; he gets it wrong, and so do you. When you learn that this bundle of old clothes is the master of the estate, the mistake itself becomes the verdict: a man who has hoarded until you can't tell his sex has become one more object in the pile. Then, for the first time, Gogol gives a landowner a past. The other four have only a present. Plyushkin alone has a "before," which is why he alone is a tragedy rather than a cartoon. The laughter is taken back and replaced by a small chill.

Two · The List at Night

At the start of chapter seven Chichikov copies out his lists, and the scene breaks every rule of his character: for six chapters he has seen nothing but prices. Yet as the names go down one by one, he starts thinking about them. Gogol takes a real risk here: he lets the narrator's voice quietly take over Chichikov's head, so that you can't tell whose pity this is. Even the coldest swindler, if he looks at a name long enough, will see a person stand up behind it. Then he glances at his watch, sees it is noon, and hurries off to the court. The elegy lasts a single page. That release and snap-back cuts deeper than any moral comment could.

Three · The Troika

Part One ends by lifting its voice to the highest pitch at the very moment a swindler is running away. For ten chapters everything has been close-up: pipe ash, a toothpick, a side of mutton, shoes at a ball. On the last page the camera pulls back and Russia becomes a carriage at full gallop. The beauty lies in the mismatch: the man riding in this emblem of the nation is the con man who has just been found out. Gogol never says so. He simply seats the hymn and the satire in the same carriage and ends on a question nobody answers.

The Core Device: How a Business Deal Becomes a "Poem"

The scheme itself would actually work. The census list, the poll tax and the Board of Guardians lending against serfs were all real nineteenth-century Russian institutions. The only thing the novel asks you to accept is that one man could buy nearly four hundred dead peasants without being thrown out at the first door, and that step depends not on the law but on the people: Manilov doesn't understand and is too polite to ask, Korobochka cares only about price, Sobakevich understands instantly and only wants more, Plyushkin just wants to save on tax. The real condition for the con is that everyone is clear-sighted only about their own small patch of interest.

The scheme is also a passport. It gives Chichikov a reason to knock on door after door, so the novel becomes beads on a string, one estate, one portrait. This is the picaresque's old trick: send a rogue down the road and let him reflect whoever he passes. But Gogol changes one crucial thing. His rogue is not a pauper fleeing society but a man trying to get into it, to become a landowner. What he reflects is not the margins but the centre.

The third device is the narrator, who stops whenever he likes to wander off. A simile can sprout its own plot: the officials' black tailcoats at the governor's party are compared to flies swarming over a sugar loaf on a summer day, and then the flies get half a page of their own. He breaks into lyricism, or harangues the reader. These digressions are what let Gogol call the book a poem: the plot is a caricature, the digressions are a lament, and you never know whether the next sentence will make you laugh or sigh. Nabokov later made the point that in Gogol the characters and images that pop up in passing are more alive than the main line.

One link needs an honest label. The trilogy plan, first the descent, then Chichikov's spiritual resurrection in the later parts, is a literary hope, not something the novel achieved. All the force of Part One comes from death, and it leaves no solid foothold for resurrection. That the book ended up as only its first part may not be just because its author fell ill.

What It Makes You Think About

One · The rules allowed it, the dead feel nothing, the landowners even saved on tax. Whom exactly did Chichikov harm?

The case that he did nothing wrong: everyone in the deal comes out ahead. The landowners shed a taxable liability, the court collects its fees, Chichikov gets his start-up capital. The dead cannot be hurt, and not a single living person is sold, beaten or moved. All he did was understand a loophole in the system and follow procedure to the end. What's absurd is a system that taxes the dead, not the man who reads it correctly.

The case that he is guilty: but the two hundred rubles the Board lends are real money, secured against something that doesn't exist, and the hole eventually lands on the state and on future borrowers. More to the point, the whole deal rests on treating people purely as numbers: one soul, two hundred rubles. The worst thing about a system that sees people as paper is not the system itself but the class of people it breeds who live off that paper and are each quite sure they've done nothing wrong.

Where does Gogol stand? He lets Chichikov escape Part One almost unscathed and has the narrator call him "not a thorough villain, merely an acquirer." His anger isn't aimed at one man but at the atmosphere that makes such men seem normal. Bring it home: have you ever done something inside a system that was fully compliant and still felt slightly wrong, padding a report, filling every box on a metric-driven review? In that moment, how far were you from Chichikov? Today we call the same move arbitrage, tax optimisation, or gaming the system.

Two · Does a writer who can only diagnose the disease owe us a cure?

The case that he does: Gogol believed it himself. After Part One he came to feel that depicting only ugliness was not enough, even sinful: to display a nation's sickness to the world without telling it how to get well, how is that different from mocking a sick man? He wanted to write positive Russians and to resurrect Chichikov. In 1847 he published Selected Passages from Correspondence with Friends, preaching obedience and piety. Criticism that offers nothing constructive easily turns into a pose.

The case that he doesn't: Vissarion Belinsky answered with his "Letter to Gogol," accusing him of betraying his own work. And the facts bore Belinsky out: the decade Gogol spent writing prescriptions was the most tormented and least successful of his life. The model landowner and virtuous merchant of Part Two are flat as posters; he couldn't bear them himself, and burned them. Perhaps a writer's real gift is seeing the disease, and prescribing is another trade; insist on doing both and you ruin both. Honestly writing "this is what we are like" is already an action.

Gogol chose the first path with his life, and his work testified for the second. Bring it home: you point out a problem at work and are told "don't raise it until you have a solution." Sometimes that is responsibility. Sometimes it is a way of shutting people up.

Three · Thrift, self-discipline, perseverance, a self-made rise: are these still virtues when Chichikov has them?

The case that they are: look at his character sheet. He doesn't drink or gamble; his clothes are always clean; he has fallen three times and started from zero each time without complaint; he is polite to his superiors and not rude to his inferiors. Manilov is daydreams, Nozdryov is waste, Plyushkin is rot; the landowners are all consuming what their ancestors left them, and only Chichikov is building something. If Russia needed a man with a modern spirit, he is the only candidate in the book. His problem isn't his character but a society that left him only crooked roads.

The case that they aren't: but every one of those virtues serves a single end, money. His discipline keeps his calculations error-free, his courtesy lowers people's guard, his patience waits for the next loophole. He watches his old teacher sink into poverty unmoved and drops his superior's daughter the moment she has served her purpose. A virtue blind to the harm it does is no longer a virtue, only efficiency.

Gogol's answer is hidden in that challenge to the reader in chapter eleven: he won't let you look down on Chichikov from above; he wants you to look inside. Bring it home: today's language of discipline, hustle and relentless self-improvement is also a whole vocabulary of virtue. It persuades us to get up early, work out, review our performance, and rarely asks what all of it is for.

The Obsession: Dead Things Move, Living Things Don't

Gogol kept writing one inversion: lifeless things come alive and living people turn into objects. In "The Nose" a major's nose walks out on him and rides around Petersburg in a civil servant's uniform. In "The Overcoat" a copying clerk's entire life is filled by a new coat; when it is stolen, he dies, and returns as a ghost to strip coats off other people. Dead Souls pushes the inversion as far as it will go: the dead live on the register, are bought, sold and mortgaged, while the living on their estates turn one by one into furniture.

Another obsession is how rumour manufactures a person. In The Government Inspector a penniless junior clerk is mistaken by a guilty little town for an inspector travelling incognito. Chapters nine and ten of Dead Souls echo it: the same guilty conscience this time frightens a real swindler into being Napoleon. Gogol's provincial towns are always afraid of "someone from above," and the more afraid they are, the more easily an empty shell fills the space.

The third obsession lived in Gogol himself: the writer of the sharpest satire in Russian was also a man who longed for redemption, grew ever more devout, and finally wore himself out with asceticism. Part One of Dead Souls is the one moment that split was held in perfect balance: laughter and tears on the same page, neither yet devouring the other.

Criticism and Controversy

Is it really an indictment of serfdom? Belinsky and his followers read it as an exposé of serfdom and bureaucratic Russia, and that reading shaped literary education for more than a century. Yet Gogol himself never advocated abolishing serfdom, and in Selected Passages he was still advising landowners on how to guide their peasants. Nabokov's short 1944 book Nikolai Gogol attacked the "realist" reading head-on, arguing that Gogol's world grew out of his own head. And the book really does contain no explicit plea on the serfs' behalf: the serfs appear almost entirely as names, which can be read as the force of the indictment or as a blind spot.

The women. Most of the women are targets of satire: Korobochka is stinginess and stupidity, the two "pleasant ladies" are a rumour machine, and the governor's daughter has little character beyond being blonde and young. In a "poem" that claims to depict all of Russia, the gap is conspicuous.

The failure and burning of Part Two. The surviving chapters are widely judged stiff: positive characters like propaganda posters, preaching in place of observation. Accounts of why he burned it differ. Some stress his religious crisis and the influence of a priest close to him; others think he simply admitted at last that it wasn't good. These are conjectures. The burning and the death that followed are the only certainties.

Censorship and the title. In 1842 the censors objected to the title, since in Orthodox teaching the soul is immortal, and the first edition's cover read The Adventures of Chichikov, or Dead Souls. "The Tale of Captain Kopeikin" also had to be revised; Gogol toned down its references to the Petersburg authorities before it was passed.

Whose writer is he? Born in Ukraine, writing in Russian, his early work steeped in Ukrainian folk culture, he was enshrined as one of the founders of Russian literature. Since the war between Russia and Ukraine began, whether he is a "Russian" or a "Ukrainian" writer has become an openly contested question, and Ukrainian sources more often use the Ukrainian form of his name, Mykola Hohol. We record the fact and offer no verdict.

Crossovers

  • Political anthropology · James C. Scott's "legibility": in Seeing Like a State (1998), Scott argues that the modern state can see only what it can record (registers, cadastral maps, standardised surnames) and governs accordingly, while a large part of reality always falls outside the paper. The census list is exactly that kind of simplification, and Chichikov lives in the gap between the paper and the people. The difference: Scott worries about how simplification destroys local life, whereas in Gogol the one exploiting the gap is the man most fluent in the state's language.
  • Social psychology · the basic law of rumour: in The Psychology of Rumor (1947), Gordon Allport and Leo Postman proposed that a rumour's strength is roughly its importance times its ambiguity. The town of N maxes out both: officials with guilty consciences find any "inspection" desperately important, and "dead souls" is ambiguous enough that nobody can explain it. So one man becomes a bandit, a forger and Napoleon.
  • Finance · collateral that exists only in the records: before the 2008 crisis, subprime mortgages were bundled, rated and pledged again, with each party along the chain reading documents rather than asking whether the borrower could pay. The Board lending against a census list has the same shape: value living only in the record. The difference: Chichikov knows his collateral is empty, while many in 2008 genuinely believed the paper.
  • Buddhism · the hungry ghost: among the Buddhist realms of rebirth, the hungry ghost (preta) is often pictured with a swollen belly and a needle-thin throat, unable to swallow the food before it, a fate tied to avarice and clinging. Plyushkin is almost an earthly version: grain rots to dirt in his barns while he picks up shards on the road. The likeness is the shape of hoarding without being able to use; the difference is that the ghost's hunger is the fruit of a past life, while Plyushkin's happens on the estate he lives on.

Ten Lines to Take Away

① Not handsome, not ugly, not fat, not thin: a man who is just right is perfectly suited to doing harm in a place that is just right.

② In a state that only reads registers, the dead are more useful than the living: they don't talk, and they can be mortgaged.

③ Manilov's bookmark has sat at page fourteen for two years, and so has his kindness, forever stuck at "wouldn't it be nice."

④ Korobochka isn't afraid of "dead," only of "cheap," and that fear rides into town and takes the scheme half apart.

⑤ The one true thing Nozdryov ever said was the one thing nobody could fully believe.

⑥ Listing a dead man's trade: for Sobakevich, a sales pitch; for Chichikov, a eulogy.

⑦ Plyushkin saved everything and his barns hold only dirt; he is the only landowner with a past, which is why he is a tragedy.

⑧ Only when the prosecutor died did anyone discover he had a soul.

⑨ Chichikov let his heart move once in his life, and it cost him the whole town.

⑩ The first page asks if a wheel will reach Kazan; the last asks where Russia is going. Neither question gets an answer.
