Novels, Deep-Read · Giovanni Boccaccio · No.48

The Decameron

Il Decameron · Italy · c. 1349–1353 · Giovanni Boccaccio

中文 →

On a Tuesday morning in 1348, seven young women are kneeling in a church in Florence. They know one another. The eldest is twenty-eight, the youngest eighteen. Mass is over and nobody gets up to go home—home to what? Their fathers, husbands and brothers are mostly buried, and buried the way cargo is stowed in a ship's hold, a layer of bodies and a layer of earth.
The bells have stopped. There are too many dead to ring for, and the living cannot stand the sound. Things lie in the street and people walk around them.
The eldest of the seven begins to speak, and what she says is not consolation. She says: let us go. We all have houses in the country, clean air, spring water, wine, everything we need. Rather than sit here counting whose turn it is tomorrow, let us go and live like people for a while.
The other six agree. The only problem is that they have no men with them—and at that moment three young men walk into the church.

This page contains full spoilers—how the ten of them write themselves a constitution, what Dioneo eventually does with the exemption he asks for on the first night, how a man who spent his life doing evil is buried as a saint, what Ghismonda says to her father's face, the verdict Boccaccio himself passes on Griselda's twelve years of "testing," and where the ten go after the hundredth story. If you want to keep the suspense, read the book first; this page will wait.

In one sentence

In the worst summer of the Black Death, ten young Florentines withdraw to a country villa and set themselves rules: each day one of them is crowned ruler, the ruler sets the day's theme, each of the ten tells one story—ten days, a hundred stories. This is not a book that passes the time with anecdotes. It is a group of people standing in the wreckage of a civilisation who reassemble the question of how to live out of a homemade timetable and a hundred superbly told stories—while outside, the dying city goes on dying.

Coordinates

Giovanni Boccaccio (1313–1375) was the illegitimate son of a Florentine merchant, raised among the banks and trading houses of Naples, meant for business, and lost to books instead. He and Petrarch were lifelong friends, and between them they are the other half of Italian literature—the two who matter most after Dante.

The Decameron was written around 1349–1353 in Tuscan volgare, the spoken Italian of the day rather than Latin. Its slot in literary history is unambiguous: it is the first great European narrative written entirely in prose, entirely about secular life, and written to be a pleasure to read. Before it, long serious narrative was either verse or hagiography. Chaucer, Shakespeare, Lessing and Keats all took material straight out of these hundred stories.

One more thing: it was written with Dante as its opponent. The Commedia has a hundred cantos and runs from hell to heaven; this book has a hundred stories and runs from a plague city to a garden, never once leaving the human world.

The story

Act one · A title, a plague, and an idea born in a church

Start with the cover. The book's full title is The Decameron, or Prince Galehaut (Prencipe Galeotto). Galehaut is the go-between who carries messages between Lancelot and the queen. In Inferno V, Dante has Francesca explain how she and her husband's brother crossed the line: they were reading Lancelot together and could not go on past the kiss—the book, she says, and the man who wrote it, were Galehaut (Galeotto fu 'l libro e chi lo scrisse). Meaning: the book was the pander. The book was an accomplice. Boccaccio took the charge Dante laid against a book and printed it, squarely and without apology, as his own subtitle.seed ①

The preface is addressed to women in love, shut up indoors with nothing to do: a man who is unhappy can hunt, trade, ride out; a woman cannot. Then comes the famous introduction. In 1348 the Black Death reaches Florence, and he writes it up like a man keeping accounts: first a swelling in the groin or the armpit, some the size of an apple, some of an egg, then dark blotches over the body, and once the blotches came you had about three days. He records the three strategies of the living—shutting yourself away on a light diet, drinking all night in the taverns instead, or simply leaving; the bells that stopped, the corpse-bearers tipping bodies into trenches the way cargo is stowed in a hold, a layer and a layer; families abandoning one another, and worst of all, parents abandoning their children. One small image cuts deepest: a pauper dies in the street, his rags are thrown out after him, two pigs come and root and tear at them, and after a few minutes both convulse and drop dead. Nobody in 1348 knew how the disease travelled, and he did not either, so he does not explain—he simply sets down a piece of evidence the eye can see, and walks away.

Then the church: Santa Maria Novella.seed ② Seven young women meet there; Boccaccio notes that the names are invented, to protect real people. The one who takes charge is Pampinea. Her argument for leaving is not stirring in the least: we owe nobody our lives here; what could be done has been done and what could be buried has been buried; staying saves no one else and does not save us either—a person has the right to preserve their own life, and it is no sin.seed ③ They are not martyrs and make no pretence of being. The only difficulty is that seven young women travelling alone is a problem for both their safety and their reputation—and at that moment three young men walk in: Panfilo, Filostrato and Dioneo.

They leave at first light. Here is the fact on which every pleasant thing that follows rests: they take cooks, maids and a steward; somebody else makes their beds, cooks their food and cools their wine in the spring—and the book never spends one word being uneasy about it. On arrival, Pampinea's first act is not celebration but legislation: a company that wants to stay happy needs a head, or it falls apart. So each day one of them is elected king or queen and crowned with laurel for that day alone; the day's schedule and the day's theme are theirs to set. In the heat they tell stories in the shade, one each, then sing and dance. No stories on Friday, kept for the Passion, and none on Saturday, when the women wash their hair—so ten days of stories take a fortnight to get through.

At the end of the first day Dioneo asks for something. He was born to make people laugh, he says; he came here for that and not to keep rules, and he would like a standing exemption—never to be bound by the day's theme, and always to tell the last story of the day.seed ④ They laugh and grant it, taking it for a boisterous young man wangling himself a convenience. The machine is now built: a monarch replaced daily, a theme replaced daily, a fixed quota of one story per person, and a single exception written into the constitution.

Act two · The first three days: a scoundrel becomes a saint, and three identical rings

Day one has no theme. Panfilo speaks first and tells Ser Ciappelletto.seed ⑤ This notary from Prato has spent his life forging documents, perjuring himself, egging men on to lawsuits and murder, cheating at dice, and entering churches only to blaspheme—and not for profit, but as a matter of taste. Sent to Burgundy to collect debts, he lodges with two Italian moneylenders and falls fatally ill. His hosts are trapped: no priest will hear a confession like his; without confession he cannot be buried in consecrated ground and must be thrown out like a dog; and the town already hates Italian usurers, so the scandal will bring a mob down on them. The brothers discuss it through the wall, and the dying man hears every word. Send for the priest, he says. I will handle it.

The friar who comes is famous for his holiness. Ciappelletto tells him he is still a virgin; that he fasts three days a week without touching water; that he has never dared speak an idle word in church. Then they arrive at his gravest sin, and he begins to weep: once, as a small boy, he cursed his mother. He weeps so hard he cannot go on; the friar assures him it is nothing; he insists it is unforgivable. He goes on to "admit" that he once spat in the house of God, that after a fast he once drank water with too much relish, and that he once scolded a servant for not sweeping properly. Not one word is true, and every word is more like a confession than the truth would have been—everything he owns up to is a sin only a saint would count. He dies that night, is buried in the church, has his clothing torn into relics, and is thereafter venerated as San Ciappelletto, with miracles credited to him.

The second story is the follow-up blow. Abraham, a Jew, is being urged by a friend to convert, and insists on first going to Rome to see how the papal court actually lives. He spends months there and comes back reporting lechery, avarice and the sale of church offices from top to bottom—and then asks to be baptised, on this reasoning: these men are working extremely hard to demolish their own religion, and it keeps growing anyway, so something must be holding it up. The third is the three rings. Saladin, short of money, tries to trap the rich Jew Melchizedek by asking which of the three faiths—Jewish, Muslim, Christian—is the true one. The old man answers with a story: a ring that marks the heir passes down to a father with three sons, all equally loved, so he has two perfect copies made and gives one to each on his deathbed; afterwards nobody can tell which is the original, and the matter has never been settled.

Day one has now set three pillars in place: language can manufacture facts; an institution is not the same thing as the faith it claims; and the truth may not be verifiable at all. The next nine days move among those three. Day two is about people who come through misfortune to unexpected good fortune: Andreuccio, a horse-dealer from Perugia, arrives in Naples with five hundred gold florins, is fleeced by a Sicilian con-woman who passes herself off as his half-sister, falls through a plank into a cesspit at midnight, is press-ganged by grave-robbers into opening the tomb of an archbishop buried that day, hides the ruby ring in his mouth and swears he cannot find it, and is sealed in the tomb; when a second gang prises the slab up he seizes the leg reaching in, they flee convinced the dead have risen, and he goes home with the ring. In one night he is fleeced, drowned in filth and shut in a grave, and he comes home richer than he left. The book's view of Fortune is at its plainest here: she does not reward the good, she rewards whoever has not yet been frightened to death.

Act three · Days four to six: the half of love that kills, and a sentence that saves a life

Day four opens with the author breaking in to answer the people already attacking him for not doing serious work. He tells half a story—the only one not counted among the hundred. A man raises his son alone on a mountain; at eighteen the boy has never seen a woman. They go down into the city, he sees a group of girls and asks what they are. His father, not wanting him stirred, says: those are goslings, they are no good. The boy says: Father, buy me a gosling.

The theme is love that ends badly, and it produces the most solemn story in the book: Ghismonda. Prince Tancredi of Salerno loves his only daughter too much to marry her off; widowed and living at home, she chooses for herself—Guiscardo, her father's valet, of the lowest birth and the best man at court. Tancredi dozes off in her chamber, hidden by the bed curtains, and wakes to see everything. He has Guiscardo seized and confronts her. Ghismonda does not weep and does not beg. She finishes what she has to say to his face: you are made of flesh, and the daughter you fathered is made of flesh too; you are old and have forgotten what the young are, and you cannot judge my years by the rules of yours. As for rank—nobility lies in conduct, not blood: name one man of yours whose character matches his. She asks only one thing: kill him if you must, but show me no mercy, because I do not intend to live. Tancredi has Guiscardo killed, cuts out his heart, and sends it to her in a golden cup. She takes the cup and says, quite calmly, that such a heart deserves a coffin of gold; then she pours in the poison she has already distilled, drinks it, and lies down holding the cup. Her father arrives too late. He buries them together, which is the only thing left for him to do.

The same day gives us Lisabetta and the pot of basil. Three merchant brothers in Messina discover their sister's affair with Lorenzo, a young man in their business, say nothing, lure him out of the city and kill and bury him. He comes to her in a dream and tells her where. She digs him up, cannot carry the whole body, and cuts off the head to take home, buries it in a large earthenware pot, plants basil on top, and waters it daily with her tears. The basil grows thick and fragrant. Her brothers, suspicious of a woman who sits weeping over a pot, take it away while she is out, empty it, find the head, and flee that night. She has lost her pot, and she cries until she dies.

Day five reverses it—love that ends well after suffering—and produces the most beautiful and most appalling story in the book: Nastagio degli Onesti. A rich young man of Ravenna, rejected and mocked by the woman he wants, moves out to the pine woods. On a Friday afternoon in May he sees a naked woman fleeing through the trees pursued by two mastiffs, with a knight riding behind her, sword drawn. He moves to help; the knight stops him: don't, we have both been dead a long time. In life the knight loved this woman, was refused with contempt, and killed himself in despair; she died too, unrepentant. Their sentence is to repeat it here every Friday: he overtakes her, cuts open her back, tears out the heart that would never soften and throws it to the dogs—then she gets up where she fell and runs, and it starts again. Nastagio watches, and an idea occurs to him. The following Friday he lays on an open-air banquet at exactly that spot and invites the woman he loves and all her family. Halfway through the meal she runs screaming into the clearing, the dogs behind her, the knight above her with his sword, and the whole table watches her opened and gutted and fed to the hounds. Nastagio calmly explains what they are seeing. That same evening the young lady sends word that she will marry him. Boccaccio adds a line: from then on, the women of Ravenna were a good deal more accommodating to their suitors.

Day six turns to the quick answer—how a single sentence gets you out of trouble—and its last story catches the swindler from day one: Friar Cipolla.payoff ⑤ ↑ Cipolla comes every year to Certaldo (Boccaccio's own home town) to collect alms, and this time announces he will display a feather from the wing of the Angel Gabriel. Two local jokers, while his servant is chasing a kitchen maid, swap the feather for a handful of charcoal. In front of the whole town he opens the casket, sees the coals, pauses for half a second, and begins: he was sent east in search of relics, through a string of invented countries, and brought back a finger of the Holy Ghost, a phial of the sweat St Michael shed fighting the devil... and then—I see my error, I have brought the wrong casket; these are the coals over which St Lawrence was roasted. He marks crosses on their shirts with the charcoal and promises a year's protection. The money comes in, the town thanks him, and not one person doubts. The notary who talked his way into sainthood and the friar who sells charcoal as a relic are performing the same act; Boccaccio never joins them up for you, he simply puts one at the opening of day one and the other at the close of day six—the two most conspicuous positions in the book.

Act four · Days seven to nine: the least respectable and most lifelike stretch

Dioneo is king on day seven, and his theme is the tricks wives play on their husbands. The best known is Peronella and the barrel. Her husband comes home early; she pushes her lover into a big storage jar in the yard and opens the door on the offensive, berating the man for not earning enough. He announces happily that he has just sold the jar for five silver pieces. She sneers: bad luck, I sold it for seven, and the buyer is inside it right now inspecting the thing. The lover climbs out complaining that it is filthy and must be scraped clean. So the husband climbs into the jar and scrapes, his wife leans over the rim pointing out the spots he has missed, the lover finishes his own business outside, pays his seven pieces, and carries the jar away.

Days eight and nine widen it to everyone cheating everyone. The regular victim is Calandrino, worked over by his two "friends" Bruno and Buffalmacco—all three real Florentine painters. They convince him the river holds a stone that makes its finder invisible; he gathers a riverbed's worth and walks home while the two pretend they cannot see him and pelt him with rocks the whole way, and he takes it without a sound, because a word would break the spell. But the thing worth remembering from these days is not a joke. It is Rinieri the scholar and Elena the widow. To please her lover, the widow leaves a scholar who is courting her standing in a snow-filled courtyard all night, nearly crippled by cold, and the town laughs about it. He survives, and spends a year planning. He tells her he can work a spell to win her lover back, on condition that she stand naked and alone at noon in July on the top of a ruined tower to perform it. She does. He takes away the ladder and leaves. She is burned all day in the sun until her skin splits and the horseflies find her, and she very nearly dies. Boccaccio gives this far more detail than any prank before it. The book never passes judgement, and here its silence gets very heavy.

Act five · Day ten: generosity, and the man who takes it apart

Panfilo is king on the last day, and the theme is those who have acted with generosity in love or in anything else: a man gives up the woman he has courted half his life to his rival; another beggars himself to keep a promise. After ten days, this small society awards itself a set of commendations. Then comes the final story—and by the exception written into the constitution, the final story is always Dioneo's.payoff ④ ↑

He tells Griselda. Gualtieri, Marquis of Saluzzo, pressed to marry, chooses the daughter of the poorest peasant in the district, and on the wedding day asks her publicly: whatever I do, can you promise never to resent it, never to refuse, never to show displeasure? She promises. Then the "testing" begins. She bears a daughter; he has the baby taken away and lets her understand it is to be killed; she says nothing. Years later she bears a son; the same again, and she asks only that the child be buried properly so the animals cannot get at him. Years after that he announces he is repudiating her for a noble bride and makes her hand back her dowry in public—she says she brought nothing but her body, so he may have that back, and asks only for a shift to cover herself, since she bore his children in it. She walks home to her father's house barefoot in a shift. Before long he sends for her again: come and sweep and make the beds and wait on the new lady, you know the house. She does the rough work, arranges the seating for the bride, and smiles and asks her husband not to torment this one as he tormented the last, since the girl is very young and might not bear it. Only then does Gualtieri announce that the twelve-year-old "bride" is their daughter, that the boy is their son, and that both have been fostered in Bologna all along. The whole city celebrates.

Dioneo does not, like the others, add a word of praise and sit down. He delivers a verdict on the spot: what Gualtieri did was not generosity but matta bestialità—mad brutality; and he adds, drily, that such a husband deserved a wife who, once thrown out in her shift, had gone and found another man and got herself a decent dress out of it. That is what the privilege was finally used for. On the first night he asked to be exempt from the theme and to speak last, and they took him for a boy angling for a favour; ten days later he used it to turn the culminating position of a day devoted to praising generosity into a public demolition of the day's premise. The one person in the book who refuses from beginning to end to keep the rules is the one person who says the whole of what he thinks.

The next morning Panfilo announces the return, with exactly the coolness that got them out: the rules existed to keep things decent; stay too long and the talk will start; a thing should be left while it is still at its best.payoff ③ ↑ They left the city because they had worked out that staying was pointless, and they go back because they have worked out that staying longer will cost them their reputations. In a full fortnight nobody proposes going back to nurse or help anyone. So they return to Santa Maria Novella, the church where they met two weeks earlier,payoff ② ↑ the seven women take leave of the three men, and each goes home—home into a city that is still dying. Boccaccio gives it a single sentence and not one word of feeling. That closed circle is the coldest stroke in the book: the stories are finished, the plague is not, nothing has been changed—except the ten people who did the telling.

The book is still not over. An Author's Conclusion answers the abuse he expects, and the defence is startlingly modern: stories are neither good nor bad in themselves, like fire or wine, it depends who uses them; a corrupt mind will find filth in any sentence; these were told in a garden to idle young people, not preached from a pulpit—and besides, every story has a heading telling you what is in it, so skip the ones you would rather not read. And then: after all, the book told you at the very top what it was called.payoff ① ↑ Dante used "Galehaut" to condemn a book for corrupting its readers; Boccaccio printed the word on his own title page. Yes, this book is a go-between. I never claimed otherwise.

Take the hundred stories together and the causal chain has a single fuse: Pampinea's sentence in the church—a person has the right to preserve their own life. The plague did not produce this book; the plague is only the setting. What produced it is a group of people in a moral vacuum writing themselves a new law, and then discovering that once you have legislated you have to find something to do. First came "we are allowed to leave"; then the villa, the rotating crown, the daily theme, the hundred stories—and everything splendid and everything questionable about this book.

Three slow-motion replays

1 · Two pigs: how to make a thing clear without explaining it

The plague introduction reads like a damage report—numbers, symptoms, institutional collapse, all of it. But the part nobody forgets for six centuries is the two pigs, and the technique is leaving the causation for the reader to join up. He had no way of explaining it, so he does not; he sets down a single piece of visible evidence and walks off. The placement is the sharper move: it sits in the middle of a long abstract passage like a lens suddenly pulling focus. The standard failure in writing about catastrophe is that every image is the same size; his solution is to give the whole plague exactly one close-up, and to put no human being in it. Too many dead stops being an image at all; two pigs dying next to a heap of rags is an image.

2 · A golden cup: putting the fiercest emotion into the most controlled gesture

Ghismonda's story runs at the highest emotional pressure in the book, and Boccaccio barely writes her emotions at all. He writes gestures and objects: a golden cup, a heart, a measure of poison, a bed. The order is exact. First she is given a full paragraph of reasoning, as cool as a submission in court—put the weeping first and you have melodrama; put the argument first and the death that follows carries weight. Then the cup arrives, and her first act is not to scream but to appraise it: such a heart deserves a coffin of gold. She accepts her father's humiliation as a gift and compliments the wrapping. After that: pour, drink, lie down. Three verbs and not a syllable of lyricism. Emotional intensity does not come from stacking adjectives; it comes from making a character do something horribly precise. Tancredi meant a heart to destroy her; she takes it and makes it a grave-good, turning his humiliation into her own funeral arrangements. The only person who loses control in that scene is the father who won.

3 · The banquet in the pine wood: the book's finest device, at its most embarrassing job

The first time we see the ghostly hunt, the reader is exactly where the hero is: shocked, frightened, wanting to intervene. The knight's explanation converts it into a sentence with a stated crime. Then comes the brilliant turn: the hero is not frightened off—he sees, on the spot, that this can be used as a prop. What follows is staged with the precision of a rehearsal: that clearing, that Friday, her whole family on the guest list, the horror timed to arrive between courses. He has scheduled an infernal torture as the entertainment after dinner, inside his own courtship. In craft terms this is the second performance: play a scene twice, once to teach the audience the rule and once to let the rule land on somebody, and the effect doubles. The trouble is what it is in service of. In this story the perfection of the technique and the ugliness of its purpose rise together. Botticelli later painted the four scenes as a set of panels, given as a wedding present—even the provenance is telling you that people took this for a happy story.

The core device: why the frame story only comes of age here

The Thousand and One Nights had already shown what a frame story can do when the stakes are inside the plot: stop telling and die. The Decameron does something entirely different—nobody is holding a sword over these ten people—and gets something else in return. There are four working parts:

  • The frame is a constitution, not an excuse. A monarch for a day, a theme set by that monarch, a quota of one story each, Fridays and Saturdays off, plus one permanent exemption. This is not "a pretext to hold a hundred stories"; it is a group of people hand-building a miniature polity in the middle of a collapse. They fled a city where the law had stopped working, and the first thing they did on arrival was legislate.
  • The themes create comparison, and comparison is meaning. A hundred stories in no order is a joke book. Line up ten variations on wives deceiving husbands, then ten on love that ends in death, and the reader starts sorting automatically: which deception is charming and which is vile, which devotion is heroic and which is stupid. The themes are what raise this from a collection to an investigation.
  • The ten narrators are ten palates, not ten names. Filostrato reaches for the bitter, Dioneo for the bawdy, Pampinea for rules. Every story is followed by a paragraph of audience reaction: who laughed, who blushed, who argued. It is a very early European exercise in the deliberate gap between narrator and author.
  • The exemption clause is the conscience of the machine. Dioneo's privilege looks like a gag and is in fact the back door Boccaccio kept for himself: because of it, the book always contains one voice not bound by the day's correct answer. A system that leaves no seat for dissent will tell a hundred stories that are all the same story.

The numbers are not accidental either. A hundred cantos from hell to heaven; a hundred stories across ten days told by ten people. Dante measures human behaviour against eternity; Boccaccio puts the same behaviour back into the Tuesday afternoon on which it happened—the same adultery that earns eternal punishment in Inferno V is, here, a comedy played out around the rim of a barrel.

What it forces you to think about

Question one
The city is dying and you take your servants up the hill to tell a hundred jokes. Is that self-preservation or desertion?

Take the prosecution first, and take it further than any prosecutor would. These ten have villas, stores and servants; their "preserving themselves" is an option you have to be able to afford. The people without country houses stay in Florence to cook for them, carry corpses, and die. Pampinea says what could be done has been done—done by whom, and what? The book does not say. They leave no money behind and bring nothing back. For a full fortnight, across a hundred stories, almost nobody catches the plague; it is written magnificently on page one and then expelled from the book entirely. That is not forgetting, it is eviction—they never once stop to remember a single one of the dead. You could argue that the whole pleasure of this book rests on screening that out, and that the cleaner the screen, the better it reads.

Now the defence, at full strength. When a disaster is large enough that no individual act makes any difference, is staying a moral position or a pose? The people who stayed in Florence did not save anyone; they simply died in greater numbers. And what these ten do is genuinely rare: they become neither of the two types the introduction describes—not the ones locked in trembling, not the ones drinking themselves to death—they rebuild an order. Fixed hours, rotating authority, a settled turn to speak, a piece of work that must be finished daily: these apparently trivial rules are the smallest working unit of a civilisation. What actually collapses in people under extremity is not the body but the belief that tomorrow will resemble today, and a timetable is the only vehicle that belief has. As for the screening out, note one fact: the single most important literary witness to the Florentine plague of 1348 is the first chapter of this book. It was not written by a hero who stayed. It was written by the man who left.

The dilemma recurs every week now. When catastrophe, war or mass redundancy fills the feed and somebody posts holiday photographs, the replies arrive: how can you, at a time like this. And demanding that everyone suspend their life simultaneously has never saved anybody either. The hard question is not whether you are allowed to be happy. It is whether your happiness requires somebody else to pay for it, and whether you know who is holding the bill. These ten had a bill, and it was settled by the people who cooled their wine and made their beds and never got a name.

Question two
Nastagio buys a marriage with a horror show. On what grounds is that filed under comedy?

Argue the comedy side properly, and more forcefully than a medieval reader would have needed to. In that value system the story is not intimidation, it is instruction: the knight's damnation carries a stated charge—his refusal drove a man to his death, and she never repented of it. That is a world which holds coldness to be an act with lethal consequences rather than a matter of temperament. What the young lady sees is her own road with the far end filled in; her rapid change of heart can be read as the moment she realises she is not merely turning down a nuisance but taking part in something whose outcome is not hers to control. And in fairness: if a work may contain a vision that turns a sinner from sin, why may it not contain one that turns a woman toward a suitor? Inside that cosmos it is not a threat. It is the truth, shown once.

But the case against is harder. This showing of the truth was designed, scheduled and executed by a man. The location, the day, the guest list, the timing between courses—every element serves one purpose: to obtain the decision he wants from a woman in a state of extreme fear. Her consent is given on the evening she watched a woman publicly disembowelled. And Boccaccio's own closing line gives the game away: from then on, the women of Ravenna were more accommodating to their suitors. That sentence names the story's real audience. It was never addressed to Nastagio; it is addressed to women who have refused somebody. Its teaching is that your refusal has a price, and that the price can be demonstrated on another woman's body.

The device has not dated at all. Every sentence of the form "I'm not threatening you, I'm just showing you what happens" is a modern staging of that banquet. Forwarding somebody's ruin to a person who is still hesitating; ending an argument with "look what happened to the ones who wouldn't listen"; every public punishment carried out in the name of education. The book's rare honesty is that it does not pretend she was persuaded—it says plainly that she was frightened, and then files the outcome under happy endings. The question is not whether Boccaccio thought this was a comedy. It is how easily all of us stop asking what a method is, once the method works.

Question three
Griselda endures twelve years. Is that the summit of virtue, or a bad story that has been told for six hundred years?

Put the case for greatness at its strongest—stronger than any of its historical defenders managed. Petrarch, having read the Decameron, translated this one story into Latin, and said explicitly that what he wanted readers to learn was not a wife's obedience to a husband but the absolute steadiness a person may keep toward fortune, and toward God. On that reading Gualtieri is not a husband at all; he is fortune itself—arbitrary, unreasoning, beyond appeal, exactly like everything that actually happens to people. And Griselda's silence is not weakness: she never once pleads, never once performs her suffering to buy pity, never once uses the children as leverage. She refuses to enter his game. Look at how the power is distributed as the years pass: he holds everything, and yet he must keep raising the stakes to prove he can move her; after twelve years the one who is exhausted is him, and she has not given an inch. There is a kind of self-possession that cannot be humiliated, because humiliation requires that you mind. The person actually on trial in this story is the husband who cannot stop testing.

Now the other side, all the way down. To call "she did not break" a victory is to issue the abuser a licence: as long as they can take it, nothing I do is excessive. Worse is what the story has actually been used for over six centuries—copied into wedding sermons, printed in books given to brides, held up as the pattern of a wife. As allegory it can point at God; as a manual it points at one word, endure. And the accounting will not survive inspection: who repays the twelve years in which her children were taken? The story's answer is that they were not really dead, so it does not count—but she did not know they were alive, and she really did lose those years. A story that writes off everything short of irreversible damage is teaching people how to forgive a rehearsed cruelty.

What is superb here is that the book knows. It does not serve this story up as its conclusion; it hands it to the one man who asked for an exemption on the first night. Dioneo finishes and immediately takes it apart in front of everyone: this was not generosity, this was mad brutality. So the question left to the reader is not whether Griselda was right. It is whether a story's power and a story's teaching can be collected separately—whether we can grant that this is written so well it cannot be argued with, and still insist that anyone who lives by it will ruin their life. Every relationship around you that goes by the name of "a test," "working through it," or "he only wants what's best for me" is running on this story's grammar.

Motifs: what Boccaccio kept coming back to

Three things preoccupied him for a lifetime, and the Decameron gives each of them its days: Fortune, Nature (usually appearing as desire), and ingegno—wit, ingenuity, brains. Day two is Fortune turning her hand over; days three, six, seven and eight are wit stealing things out of Fortune's gaps; days four and five are Nature driving people toward death or toward life. The worldview is plain and hard: Fortune does not reason, Nature cannot be suppressed, and the only tool in your hands is your head. The father who called the girls goslings is all three compressed—he tried to defeat Nature with education and lost outright.

The second line is his preoccupation with women's experience, and the contradiction running through it. Early on he wrote the Elegy of Lady Fiammetta, a first-person monologue by an abandoned woman; late in life he wrote De mulieribus claris in Latin, the first collection of biographies in the West devoted entirely to women; the Decameron is dedicated to women readers and seven of its ten narrators are women. And the same man wrote the Corbaccio, a full-throated misogynist invective. No reading has ever really reconciled the two faces. The honest formulation is that his interest in women was real and his period's contempt for them was real, and both were in him.

The third line is his lifelong wrestling with Dante. He wrote a life of Dante, and in 1373 was commissioned by the city of Florence to lecture publicly on the Commedia—the first time anyone taught a work in the vernacular as a classic. And the hundred stories he wrote himself are precisely an operation of taking the people Dante consigned to hell and setting them back down in the human world to be looked at again. A man can be a master's most devoted promoter and write a book that runs in the opposite direction.

Criticism and controversy

  • The hundred are wildly uneven. Ghismonda, Cipolla and Griselda are permanent classics, but days eight and nine contain a stretch of stock swindler routines running on one cuckold joke, and many readers slow badly in the middle. That is the built-in cost of a collection, and it does not need defending.
  • Alibech (day three, story ten) should be described plainly. An adult hermit uses religion to induce a naive young girl into sex, and the whole thing is written as a dirty joke. A number of nineteenth-century English translations dealt with it by leaving the Italian untranslated or cutting heavily—which is itself an admission that later translators knew there was a problem. "Different times" explains why it was written. It does not explain why it is funny.
  • The tension between the misogyny and the "written for women." The book is dedicated to women readers, gives seven of ten narrators to women, and produces a figure as formidable as Ghismonda—while a great many of its stories treat women as things to be tricked, and day nine includes one whose conclusion is how to manage a wife. Add the Corbaccio, and the popular line that Boccaccio was a friend to women needs heavy qualification.
  • The class position of the ten. The entire idyll rests on servants' labour, and the book has no awareness of it whatsoever. Any reading of the Decameron as a manifesto of human liberation has to start there: the people being liberated own villas. It also spends an entire day praising generosity and self-command, and ends on a story in praise of endurance—it is as fascinated by order as it is by transgression.
  • Prohibition and official bowdlerisation. The book was placed on the Index in 1559; expurgated versions were later produced under Tuscan official sponsorship, and the most famous method was to leave the plot alone and change the identities—the lecherous friar becomes a layman, the erring nun becomes a noblewoman. The story is word for word the same; only the guilt no longer lands on the Church. The method is the best acknowledgement the satire ever received.
  • Editions and translations. Modern English readers have several complete versions, and early English translations cut heavily; texts differ in base manuscript, register and nerve about the bawdy material. Before arguing about this book, it is worth saying which one you read.

Ten sentences to take away

① This is not a joke book with a plague for a backdrop: a group flees a city where the law has stopped working, and the first thing they do on arrival is legislate—telling stories is the second.

② The fuse is Pampinea's cool sentence: a person has the right to preserve their own life. Everything splendid and everything questionable in the book grows out of it.

③ The strongest stroke in the plague chapter is not a hundred thousand dead but two pigs dropping beside a heap of rags—no explanation offered, only evidence set down.

④ Story one is a notary who talks himself into sainthood; the last story of day six is a friar selling charcoal as a relic. This book knows exactly what language can manufacture.

⑤ Ghismonda takes the cup holding her lover's heart and remarks that such a heart deserves a coffin of gold: the fiercest feeling has to be spoken through the most precise gesture.

⑥ Nastagio's is the most perfect piece of machinery in the book, its purpose is to frighten a woman into marriage, and it is filed under the day for love that ends happily.

⑦ The system of daily themes turns a collection into an investigation: line up ten adulteries and the reader begins, unprompted, to sort the charming from the disgusting.

⑧ The exemption Dioneo wangled on night one is spent, ten days later, stamping "mad brutality" on the culminating story—a system must keep a seat for the person who disagrees.

⑨ They left because staying was pointless and returned because staying longer would cost them their reputations; in a fortnight nobody proposed going back to help anyone.

⑩ Dante used "Galehaut" to condemn a book for corrupting its readers; Boccaccio made it his subtitle. Yes, this book is a go-between. It never said otherwise.
