Novels, Deep-Read · Fernando Pessoa · No.111

The Book of Disquiet

Livro do Desassossego · Fernando Pessoa (as Bernardo Soares) · written 1913–1934 · first published 1982

中文 →

Near closing time, the light leaves the rooftops across the street first.
He sets the dip pen down beside the ledger, two lines short of the bottom of the page. In the back room the boss is turning papers; in the front, someone is reaching for a hat.
Outside is the Rua dos Douradores — the Street of the Gilders, with no gold anywhere on it.
He doesn't move. He isn't waiting for the end of the workday. He's waiting for the dark, when the only life he considers real can finally begin.

Full spoilers ahead. The "ending" of this book is that nothing happens. The man never leaves the office, never loves an actual person, never finishes the book — and the man who wrote him died leaving it as a trunk of loose pages, assembled by other hands nearly half a century later. Then there is the last card: who this "he" really is. There is nothing to think about here without all of that.

In one line

An assistant bookkeeper at a fabric warehouse in Lisbon spends some twenty years of evenings writing hundreds of diary-like fragments — weather, street scenes, his boss, boredom, daydreams, a principled refusal to act. He calls it a "factless autobiography." The whole book is about a man who deliberately lets nothing happen on the outside so that everything can happen on the inside.

Where it sits

Fernando Pessoa (1888–1935) was born in Lisbon, spent his childhood in Durban, South Africa, came back at seventeen and hardly left again. He made his living translating and drafting foreign-trade letters for Lisbon firms, drifting through a long series of offices. The only book of Portuguese verse he published in his lifetime was Message (Mensagem, 1934). His most famous invention is the heteronym: not a pen name but a whole fictional poet, with a biography, a temperament and a style of his own — and the heteronyms reviewed and argued with one another.

The Book of Disquiet is the prose book he worked on, on and off, for two decades and never finished. Only a dozen or so pieces appeared while he was alive. The rest lay in the trunk he left behind — more than twenty-five thousand sheets of manuscript, typescript, and scraps written on envelopes and company letterhead. The first full edition came out in Lisbon in 1982. Its place in literary history is odd: a book shelved as one of the great modernist "novels" of the twentieth century that has no plot and no order its author ever fixed. This piece follows the general shape of Richard Zenith's widely read edition; editions differ enormously in what they include and in what order, which matters a great deal, as we'll see.

The story

Act One · The man in the little restaurant

In most editions the first voice you hear isn't the protagonist's but Pessoa's own. He writes a short preface: he used to eat at a small Lisbon restaurant on a mezzanine above a shop, and there he began to notice a man who always sat in the same place — about thirty, thin, unremarkable, his face carrying a kind of controlled, hard-to-name weariness. Nods turned into conversation; the man worked at a warehouse nearby and wrote in his spare time. The preface makes the arrangement plain: what follows is this man's writing, passed to the reader through the "I."Setup ①

His name is Bernardo Soares. After the preface, the book is handed to him and never handed back.

Soares is assistant bookkeeper at a wholesale fabric warehouse on the Rua dos Douradores. Above him is the head bookkeeper, Moreira; above Moreira, the boss, Senhor Vasques. The work is always the same: copy goods in, goods out, debts and bills of exchange, line by line into the big ledger; go home to a rented room on the same street. No family near, no friends to visit, no lover, no ambition, and no plans to acquire any.

At first it reads as a grievance. The street is his cage, the ledger his chain, and Vasques — solid, practical, occasionally kind, permanently interested in accounts receivable — is the warden.Setup ② He writes about colleagues gossiping and counting pennies, about the afternoon office like a pot left too long on the stove. But he is clear from the start: he isn't going to leave, and has never seriously meant to. He despises this life and clings to it, because it offers something no other life will — it asks him to be no one in particular.

Act Two · The window · Dreaming as a trade

The real story begins after work.

He goes back to his room and stands at the window. Lisbon changes faces several times a day for him: clean morning light; evening light like a glaze slowly wearing off; rain darkening the roofs one after another and running down the tram tracks; a windy night when the whole city feels like a ship that never weighs anchor. He writes the sky again and again, the mist on the Tagus, the walk of a woman passing below, a boy whistling — not to record them, but to turn them into his own sensations, and then to feel the sensation of feeling them.

This is the vocation he has given himself: dreamer. Not sleep but waking, cultivated daydream. In his dreams he is a king, a man who is loved, a voyager; he goes to China, to some eastern port he'll never see; then he wakes, still on the Rua dos Douradores, and doesn't feel cheated. One of his most quoted lines: "To travel, it's enough to exist" (from a fragment in the Book). Real travel, he argues, only trades the perfect landscape of the dream for tickets, luggage, strange beds and disappointment.

He also says exactly what these pages are: a factless autobiography, a history without a life — he describes his writing this way more than once, in roughly those words.Setup ③ There will be no facts — no promotion, no marriage, no moving house, no journey, no quarrel — only shifts of feeling, the day's weather, and his analysis of both. He even gives his condition a texture: a tedium without cause or consequence, a fatigue with existence itself, flatter than sadness and longer-lasting than despair.

Nothing "happens" in this act, yet it is the spine of the book. Here the reader starts to feel a strange tension: the more beautifully he writes, the more clearly this man has nothing; the more he insists he wants nothing, the more you suspect something in him hurts.

Act Three · A few very small deaths

Now and then the outside world gets in — never much, always like a needle.

The office boy is leaving to go back to his village. An ordinary departure; Soares has hardly exchanged a word with the kid. Yet he finds himself oddly bereft, as if a piece of him had been carried out the door — the gist of what he writes is that whenever someone leaves, a part of us dies with them. Another time he goes to the barber's and asks after the assistant who used to shave him, and is told the man died not long ago. Soares knew nothing about him. He thinks about him all day.

The sharpest needle is a photograph. The firm has a group picture taken of the whole office staff. The prints get passed around. He finds himself in it: a flat, featureless face, standing there looking less like a man with an inner life than any of the colleagues he quietly looks down on. He has been building a palace on paper, page after page, and the camera saw a clerk standing by the wall.

It is after these needles that his account of the street and the boss begins to shift. He writes about Vasques: how he sometimes catches himself inexplicably fixed on the man; how his bulk, his hands made for counting money, his occasional offhand courtesy, are simply the way the world is. Then the line: "Boss Vasques is Life" (from a fragment in the Book).Payoff ② ↑ He even admits that if he ever did leave, he would miss the office, miss Moreira, miss Vasques.

Look back at Act One and the warden reads as a satirical walk-on. But a book with no plot can only move by changing the meaning of something it keeps returning to: the same Vasques goes from jailer to Life — not because the boss changed, but because Soares finally concedes that the mediocrity he sneered at is the only thing he has ever actually possessed. It's the nearest thing the book has to a turn.

Act Four · The aesthetics of abdication

Soares does not walk out into the world. He does the opposite: he turns not walking out into a theory.

He calls it an aesthetics of abdication. Every action soils the dream, so don't act. Loving a real person means enduring her body, her moods, her absences, so love only the imagined. Every opinion makes you a member of some party, so hold none. Better a spectator who sees everything than an actor who sees only his own small part. He treats this as a kind of nobility — not of blood but of sensibility.

The theory keeps leaking. The plainest leak comes when he reads the seventeenth-century Jesuit preacher António Vieira, a passage on King Solomon building a palace, and the rhythm of the sentences makes him weep. A man who claims indifference to politics, to humanity, to every ism, is knocked flat by three-hundred-year-old Portuguese prose. In that same fragment he writes the book's most famous sentence: "My homeland is the Portuguese language" (from the Vieira fragment).

He says he believes in nothing, and on a single page gives away the one thing he does believe in: language. A man who has abdicated everything gets angry about spelling reform and cries at the rise and fall of a sentence. The book's truest emotion isn't in the dreams he describes but in the words he describes them with.

Later, the fragments shorten and the tedium deepens: more fatigue, more sleepless nights, a state in which even dreaming feels like too much work. There is no climax and no ending. At some fragment, the book simply stops.

Act Five · A trunk

Now it's the turn of the man outside the book.

In January 1935, in a long letter to the critic Adolfo Casais Monteiro, Pessoa laid out his heteronyms systematically for the first time. Of Soares he said this was a "semi-heteronym": not a personality different from his own but a mutilation of it — "me minus reasoning and affectivity" (letter to Casais Monteiro, 13 January 1935). Soares, he added, always turned up when he was tired or drowsy, when his powers of reasoning and inhibition were somewhat suspended.

So the thin, restrained, weary stranger the "I" noticed in the little restaurant was Pessoa himself in a certain state.Payoff ① ↑ On the surface, the preface was an editor formally introducing a manuscript someone had entrusted to him. Underneath, it was a man seeing himself from across the room. He invited the weakest, most private part of himself to sit at another table, then nodded to it and made polite conversation, as one does with strangers. This isn't a critic's overreach; it's a card the author laid on the table himself, in that letter.

On 29 November of that year Pessoa was taken to hospital; he died the next day, aged forty-seven. In his trunk, some pages of the Book were marked "L. do D.," some weren't. Some dated from the early 1910s and were assigned to an earlier fictional author, Vicente Guedes — symbolist pieces full of palaces, lakes and an "Our Lady of Silence." Only the pages from 1929 onward belong to the Rua dos Douradores and Vasques. As early as 1914, Pessoa had complained in a letter that his writing produced nothing but "fragments, fragments, fragments" (letter to Armando Côrtes-Rodrigues, November 1914).

So the book's final shape was decided by editors after his death. The 1982 first edition is one arrangement; Zenith's is another, threaded by theme and mood; Jerónimo Pizarro's later edition tries to follow the order of composition. The same book, in different readers' hands, begins differently, ends differently, and doesn't always contain the same pieces. Soares said he was writing an autobiography without facts — and sequence is a fact too, so this book doesn't even have that one.Payoff ③ ↑ What first read as an elegant bit of self-mockery turned, on the day the trunk was opened, into a precise set of instructions.

The fuse

The fuse here isn't an event. It's a bargain: a man trades a job that asks him to be no one for an undisturbed inner life — and then discovers that the price of the bargain is exactly what makes him "factless": no one sees him, no one waits for him, nothing proves he lived. Everything else — the skies from the window, the abdication from action, the flat face in the photograph, the tears over a page of prose — is aftershock. And outside the book the bargain was bigger still: Pessoa split himself into several writers and got a literary continent for it, at the cost of being nearly transparent in his own lifetime, like the man in the corner of that little restaurant.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion one · A group photograph

The power here comes from a sudden reversal of point of view. For the whole book, Soares is the one looking: at the sky, the street, colleagues, the boss, his own sensations. He is the only camera. Then one day an actual camera is pointed at him.

He writes it with restraint. The print reaches him; he looks at the others first, then finds himself, and then comes a very specific humiliation — not ugliness but ordinariness, the sense that nothing shows. A man proud of his sensibility is sentenced by a machine: your inner life has left no mark on your face. Pessoa doesn't let him rage or console himself; he lets him acknowledge it, and then the next fragment is about something else. That holding-back is the book's basic rhythm: a needle goes in, he doesn't cry out, the next page is about rain as usual. The reader does the hurting.

Slow motion two · A page of Vieira

This is almost the only time in the book he loses control. Ordinarily he feels first and then steps back to analyze the feeling, like a man touching his own wound in gloves. Reading Vieira, the gloves come off: he weeps first and only afterward asks why.

The technical point worth noticing is that the object of feeling here is neither a person nor a landscape but syntax — how a sentence rises, turns and lands. By letting a man who claims coldness toward everything be moved in the least likely place, Pessoa defines him at a stroke: he isn't without passion; he has put all of it into the one thing nobody else can see or take from him. And "My homeland is the Portuguese language," coming from someone who has just declared he holds no positions, lands with startling weight.

Slow motion three · The table across the room

The third scene sends you back to the first page — ideally after you've read that 1935 letter.

In the preface Pessoa describes noticing the man, his looks, his restraint, how they went from nods to talk. It moves very slowly and very politely, with exactly the distance of two strangers. Once you know Soares is "me minus reasoning and affectivity," the passage becomes something else: a man practicing looking at himself with a stranger's eyes, in no hurry to claim what he sees. That distance is Pessoa's whole method — every heteronym starts with pushing a part of himself to the table opposite so it can speak. Why does it work? Because it turns a psychological fact (we can often only face our most helpless side by pretending not to recognize it) into narrative structure.

The device, taken seriously: one man becomes several, a book has no shape

The book runs on two devices, one belonging to the author and one to the book itself.

The first is the heteronym. By Pessoa's own account in the 1935 letter, he invented imaginary companions from childhood; in adulthood the impulse grew into a literary system. The three full heteronyms: Alberto Caeiro, a barely schooled country poet of nature who insists on seeing things as they are without thinking about what they mean, and whom the others treat as their "master"; Ricardo Reis, a physician who emigrated to Brazil and writes cool, measured, Stoic odes after Horace; and Álvaro de Campos, a naval engineer trained in Glasgow who writes sprawling, Whitman-like poems that grow more desolate over time. Each has a birth date, a horoscope, a physical description; they review one another. Pessoa also wrote poems under his own name — the "orthonym."

Soares sits at the innermost ring of that map: so close to Pessoa that he only counts as a semi-heteronym. What's the rule? A full heteronym differs from the author in style and conviction; a semi-heteronym is what's left of the author after certain functions are switched off. Caeiro is who Pessoa wanted to be and couldn't; Soares is who Pessoa didn't want to be and often was.

What holds up about this device is that it isn't a trick; it's a form of honesty. All of us speak differently and take different positions in different relationships; Pessoa simply refused to pretend these voices would eventually be unified into one "real me." Where it holds up less well — where literature needs more than the facts support — is that much of the heteronyms' biography was filled in after the fact, and the 1935 letter is itself a carefully staged self-mythology. Pessoa claimed Caeiro arrived on a single day in March 1914, when he wrote more than thirty poems at a stretch; scholars going through the manuscripts found those poems were revised many times. The heteronyms are a real psychological structure and a performance, and the two can't be pulled apart.

The second device is the fragment. In an ordinary novel, cause and effect come from "the next page." This book has no next page: each piece stands alone and can sit beside any other. Its coherence comes from three things — one voice, one street, one tedium. That makes it like a diary and not a diary: diaries have dates and this mostly doesn't; diaries record events and this deliberately has none. The result is that you read it the way Soares lives: open to whatever day it is, each day much like the last, with slightly different light. The form doesn't depict that life. The form is that life.

What it makes you think about

One · Is a job that asks you to be no one a refuge or a retreat?

Soares could have found a more respectable position. He didn't. He wanted undemanding, identical days so that all his energy could go to the few hours after work. Suppose you're at that fork today: a job with a clear ladder that will eat your whole attention, or a dull, stable one where everything after six o'clock belongs to you. Which do you take?

The strongest case for "refuge": attention is finite, and nobody can give all of themselves to a career and to an inner life at once. Pessoa lived exactly this way — translating business correspondence in offices, and the trunk was the return. Kafka worked at an insurance institute; Wallace Stevens worked at an insurance company. This isn't a loser's excuse; it's a cold-eyed allocation of resources. The person who doesn't care about rank may be the only one who keeps their time.

The strongest case for "retreat": a refuge turns into a cage, and you won't notice. Soares starts out calling the street his choice; later he's writing about the photograph, the office boy who left, the barber's assistant who died. He isn't protecting an inner life; he's losing the world by inches. Someone who never risks anything ends up with an inner life made mostly of weather. Pessoa had the trunk. Most people who choose the refuge end up with neither a trunk nor a world.

Where does the author stand? Hard to say. "Boss Vasques is Life" can be read as reconciliation or as surrender. Bring it home: when you say "once I have time I'll do the thing I really want," is your job holding your time for you — or holding your excuse?

Two · When the world is on fire, is it wrong to write only about the sky?

The late Soares fragments date from 1929 to 1934. In those years Portugal moved from military dictatorship to Salazar's Estado Novo, and authoritarian regimes were rising across Europe. The book barely mentions any of it. It is one man, one street, one tedium.

The strongest case that it's wrong: the aesthetics of abdication isn't neutral. When an educated, articulate person announces that no position interests him, he has in effect handed the world to those most interested. Soares dresses indifference up as nobility, and that costume is contagious — it makes not caring feel refined. What that era needed was not more people who could write beautifully about rain.

The strongest case that it isn't: the first thing authoritarianism wants is the inner life — it demands that everyone take a side, declare, participate. A person who insists on writing only what he truly feels is guarding exactly the ground such regimes most want to occupy. In some decades, writing about rain is a refusal to be conscripted. And the book doesn't teach indifference; it shows its cost — the photograph, the small deaths are right there for the reader to see.

One fact to weigh for yourself: Pessoa was far from a political blank. In 1928 he published a pamphlet defending the military dictatorship, and near the end of his life he wrote satirical poems against Salazar. Bring it home: when your friends are arguing heatedly about some public matter and you choose to stay silent — what are you protecting, and what are you avoiding? Can you tell which?

Three · Is loving only in the imagination a betrayal of love, or its best protection?

Soares is blunt about it: he would rather love someone dreamed, because a real person coarsens love — she gets tired, says foolish things, is absent when you want her most. It's not that he lacks longing; he has done the arithmetic and found the imagined version a better deal.

The strongest case for him: a great deal of ordinary love is love for the version we carry in our heads; people just don't admit it, and when the real person stops matching the version, they say the other one changed. Soares is at least honest — he says the quiet part out loud, and he hurts no one. A person who doesn't love never disappoints anybody.

The strongest case against: the entire point of love is that the other person is not your invention. They surprise you, let you down, change you; an imagined person can only hand back what you already had. Loving only the dreamed is a lifetime of talking to yourself. And the deepest loneliness in this book is exactly that: endless speech, and no one ever answering.

Pessoa's only known romance was an on-and-off one with Ophélia Queiroz, whom he met at a firm where both worked; it began twice and came to nothing both times. Bring it home: have you ever been in a relationship where what you really missed was the stage of imagining how it would be? Were you protecting love then, or yourself?

The obsession: what Pessoa kept writing

Pessoa's recurring fixation fits in one sentence: the "I" isn't a thing but a crowd of voices that won't yield to one another. All his work is a set of different solutions to that sentence.

Caeiro's cycle The Keeper of Sheep is one way out: look at the world through the eyes of a man who doesn't think, reduce the "I" to almost nothing but seeing. Campos's long poem "The Tobacco Shop" (1928) is another: a man at a window, watching the tobacconist's across the street, announcing that he is nothing and will never be anything, while also saying he holds all the dreams of the world inside him — nearly the same scene as the Book, rendered as poetry, except that Campos shouts and Soares only murmurs on paper. And the short poem Pessoa wrote under his own name, "Autopsychography" (written 1931), says the poet is a feigner who feigns so completely that he even feigns the pain he really feels — which takes the question of sincerity versus performance as far as it goes.

Where does the Book fit? It's the foundation of the whole heteronym system, the most tired face underneath all the masks. The other heteronyms are Pessoa's surplus selves; Soares is the self he subtracted. Perhaps that's why he couldn't close the book in twenty years: a character can be finished, but a man minus part of himself can't be written to an end.

Criticism and controversy

Is it a novel at all? The bluntest objection. No plot, no dialogue, hardly a second character, one voice from start to finish. Filing it under "novel" is itself a publishing and critical decision; some critics argue it should be read as prose poetry or philosophical notebooks, and that reading it as a novel sets readers up for disappointment and the book for misreading. This site treats it as a novel because it does have an invented narrator, a street, and a slowly shifting inner arc — but the classification is open to argument.

Whose book is it? If editors decide both contents and order, every edition is, to some degree, the editor's work. The differences are large: some include the long early symbolist pieces and some move them to an appendix; some exclude the Guedes material and some keep it. Critics point out that part of the feeling readers invest in this "masterpiece" is invested in an editor's cut. That's not an accusation against any editor; it's the book's built-in condition.

Self-absorption and pose. Many readers find it monotonous, narcissistic and misanthropic, hundreds of pages circling the same fatigue; the superiority of "I see more than men of action do" grows wearing. Its handling of women draws criticism too: real women barely appear, and when they do they are mostly an outline to be watched and imagined — a reason not to love.

Myth inflation. Pessoa's posthumous reputation in Portugal is close to national-monument status, and the heteronym story is made to be told as legend. Some scholars warn that the 1935 letter is a late self-narrative with obvious theatrical elements, and that taking it at face value as the official manual of the heteronyms is falling into exactly the game Pessoa played best. On politics, both the 1928 pamphlet defending the military dictatorship and the late satirical poems about Salazar are on record; scholars still read his actual position in different ways, and we won't adjudicate it here.

Crossovers

  • Philosophy · Hume's bundle of perceptions: In the Treatise, Hume says that when he looks inward he never catches a "self," only one perception after another; the self is nothing but that bundle. Soares's hundreds of fragments read like a live transcript of the claim. The difference: Hume left the study, dined and played backgammon, and found the conclusion harmless; Soares lived it as an illness.
  • Buddhism · Non-self and the five aggregates: Buddhist teaching analyzes a person as five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness — and treats clinging to any of them as "me" as a root of suffering. Splitting oneself into heteronyms has the same shape as taking the self apart. But Buddhism takes it apart in order to let go; Pessoa took it apart and gave each piece a voice and a book — non-self turned into a noisier self.
  • Psychology · "A wandering mind is an unhappy mind": Killingsworth and Gilbert's 2010 study in Science pinged over two thousand people at random by phone, found minds wandering nearly half the time, and found people generally less happy while wandering. Soares insists daydreaming is his happiness; the study stands against him — though it measured everyday drift, not a deliberately cultivated dream written down as prose.
  • Psychology · Maladaptive daydreaming: The Israeli psychologist Eli Somer proposed the term in 2002 for people absorbed in elaborate, ongoing fantasies to the point of damaging work and relationships. Soares's "trade of dreaming" looks similar from outside; the difference is that he never loses control — he dreams lucidly, almost professionally, and keeps examining the dreaming itself.

Ten lines to take away

① An autobiography without facts — so it doesn't even keep the fact of its own order.

② He chose a job that asked him to be no one, and then became no one.

③ "To travel, it's enough to exist" — said most beautifully by a man who never left his street.

④ The same Vasques, first jailer, then Life: it wasn't the boss who changed, it was the admission.

⑤ One group photo was enough: the camera can't see an inner life, and he had assumed it would show on his face.

⑥ He said he believed in nothing, then wept over a sentence three centuries old.

⑦ "My homeland is the Portuguese language" — a man who abdicated everything kept the words.

⑧ The stranger at the other table in the preface is the author taking his most tired self out to dinner.

⑨ The other heteronyms are Pessoa's surplus; Soares is the part he subtracted, which is why the book couldn't end.

⑩ A trunk of loose pages, several editors, several orders — and the reader ends up like him, opening each day to one much like the last, and not quite the same.
