Novels, Deep-Read · Boris Pasternak · No.113

Doctor Zhivago

Doktor Zhivago · Boris Pasternak · first published 1957 (in Italian) · sixteen parts of prose + "The Poems of Yuri Zhivago"

中文 →

Moscow, around Christmas 1911, a night well below freezing. A sledge runs down Kamergersky Lane carrying a young medical student and the girl he will marry, late for a Christmas party.
He looks up. In a window crusted with frost, the warmth inside has melted a small black circle, and through it a candle is burning. Its light seems to be looking out at the street, almost on purpose.
Half a line of verse comes to him: a candle burned on the table. He doesn't finish it. The sledge goes by.
Behind that window sits a girl he hasn't met yet. She has just asked her boyfriend to light the candle. There is a revolver in her pocket.

Full spoilers ahead. This covers why Lara fires a gun at a Christmas party, how a gentle young schoolteacher turns into Strelnikov, the commander whose name means "the Shooter," how Zhivago uses a lie to put the woman he loves on the sledge of the man who ruined her, which tram he dies beside and whom he sees in his last minutes, where Lara ends up, and whose daughter is the rough girl working in an army laundry.

In one sentence

A doctor who is also a poet lives from 1905 to 1929, through two revolutions, a world war and a civil war. He neither opposes the revolution nor joins it. He just keeps loving, looking and writing, and history grinds him down a little at a time. When he dies, almost nothing of his life is left except a notebook of poems, and the last part of the novel is that notebook.

Where it stands

Boris Pasternak (1890–1960) was one of the great Russian poets of the twentieth century. He studied music and then philosophy before giving everything to poetry. In the 1930s and 1940s, when writing his own verse became dangerous, he lived by translating Shakespeare and Goethe. Doctor Zhivago is his only full-length novel. He began it around 1945, finished it in 1955 and regarded it as the most important thing he ever wrote.

Its place in literary history comes half from the text and half from what happened to it. The Soviet journal Novy Mir turned it down in 1956. An Italian Communist journalist, Sergio D'Angelo, carried the manuscript out of the country, and the Milan publisher Feltrinelli brought out an Italian translation in November 1957. In 1958 Pasternak won the Nobel Prize in Literature and, under a campaign of attacks at home, was forced to turn it down. The book was banned in its own country for thirty years and only appeared officially in the USSR in 1988. It is a poet's attempt to prove that a private life is bigger than history.

The Story

Act I · Two children and a lawyer

The novel opens with a funeral. Yura Zhivago, ten years old, walks behind his mother's coffin. When she is buried he climbs onto the fresh mound, lifts his head, and looks as if he is about to howl like a wolf cub. That night, in a monastery, a blizzard roars outside his window and whitens the whole world, and he feels it has come for him. His father left the family long ago, a man who ran through a fortune of millions.

The boy is first looked after by his uncle Nikolai Vedenyapin, a former priest who left the Church, a thinker who says things like "history is man's second universe." Soon afterwards, on a train heading out to the provinces, a boy called Misha Gordon is travelling with his father. The train lurches to a stop: a man has thrown himself from it and lies dead by the track. It is Yura's father. Seed ①The man who had been travelling with him, keeping him company over the bottle all the way, was his lawyer. At this point it reads as no more than an orphan's backstory, and the lawyer is a passer-by.

Yura is raised in Moscow by the family of Professor Gromeko, a chemist, and grows up alongside their daughter Tonya. In the same city another child lives a very different life. Lara, Larisa Guichard, is the daughter of Amalia, the widow of a Belgian engineer. Her mother runs a dressmaking shop under the protection of a lawyer named Viktor Komarovsky, who is her lover and her "business adviser." When Lara is sixteen, this polished, worldly man, decades older than she is, starts taking her out to dinner and dancing, and then makes her his mistress. She feels soiled and cannot get free. She hates him and she is in his power, and she knows that both are true at once, which is worse than being simply a victim.

Winter 1905. The railwaymen are on strike and there is shooting in the streets. Pasha Antipov, son of a railway worker, a clean, earnest, almost transparent boy, falls in love with Lara. Lara's mother swallows iodine in their rooms and survives; the book hints that she has sensed something. Professor Gromeko is called in to help, and he brings the two boys, Yura and Misha. In that chaotic room Yura sees Lara for the first time. She and Komarovsky exchange a look, and Yura understands it: the puppeteer and the puppet. Misha tugs his sleeve and whispers that this is the man who was on the train with Yura's father. Payoff ① ↑The man who walked Zhivago's father toward his death first appears standing beside the woman Zhivago will love most. The drinking companion mentioned in passing on the train becomes a thread. The same man took his father, and later he will take Lara. The coincidence doesn't save the plot any work. It is the first moment the reader feels an invisible web running between these people.

Act II · A candle and a gun on Christmas night

Six years later, Christmas 1911. Lara is working as a governess and paying for Pasha's university studies out of her own pocket. But to get free of Komarovsky for good she needs money and she needs a reckoning. That evening she goes to Pasha's student room in Kamergersky Lane, says she needs to talk to him seriously, and asks him to put out the lamp and light a candle; she likes to talk by candlelight. She tells him they must marry at once. Seed ②Then she leaves for the Sventitskys' Christmas party with the gun in her pocket.

This is the opening scene. Yura and Tonya, on their way to the same party, ride past that window. The reader knows who is inside. Yura doesn't. Half a line of a poem comes to him and he forgets it.

At the party the grown-ups are dancing around the tree. Lara fires at Komarovsky, misses, and wounds someone else, who happens to be Kornakov, the deputy prosecutor who once prosecuted the railway strikers. Komarovsky makes sure the affair is hushed up. That same night Tonya's mother, Anna Ivanovna, dies. During her illness she had called Tonya and Yura to her bedside and asked them to stay together. In his grief Yura accepts the match. He loves Tonya the way you love someone you grew up with.

Lara marries Pasha. They go to teach in Yuriatin, a small town in the Urals modelled on Perm, and have a daughter, Katenka. On their wedding night she tells him everything about her past. Pasha forgives her, but from then on he feels the marriage has a floor he can never reach. He suspects that her love for him is more pity than passion: someone who has been soiled looking for redemption in someone clean. When war breaks out in 1914 he enrols in officers' school and goes to the front. Before long word comes back that he has been captured, or is missing.

Act III · War, Meliuzeevo, and a "splendid piece of surgery"

Lara trains as a nurse and goes to the front to look for her husband. Yura, now Dr Zhivago, whose wife Tonya has just given birth to their son Sasha, is called up as an army doctor and wounded in Galicia. In the small rear town of Meliuzeevo he and Lara work in the same hospital. An officer named Galiullin tells Lara that Pasha is dead; he says he saw him fall.

The February Revolution of 1917 arrives, and the town is all meetings and speeches. Zhivago is billeted in the old house of a countess, where an aged Swiss woman, Mademoiselle Fleury, also lives. She is very old and hard of hearing, and at night she wanders the corridors with a lamp. Seed ③Turn the page and you will have forgotten her.

Zhivago falls in love with Lara. One evening he almost says so. She stops him and tells him not to. He doesn't say it again. He goes back to Moscow.

On the day of the October Revolution there is heavy snow. He buys a special edition at a street corner and reads the first Bolshevik decrees standing in a doorway. A wave of elation goes through him: what splendid surgery, cutting out the whole stinking old ulcer at a stroke (that is the gist). It is the closest Zhivago ever comes to the revolution, and he means it.

Then come hunger and cold. He goes out at night to tear down other people's fences for firewood, and he catches typhus. In his delirium a young stranger appears by the bed with flour and butter and a piece of advice: leave Moscow and go to the Urals. This is Evgraf, his half-brother, his father's son by another woman. He is a mysterious figure who seems to have connections in the new regime. He turns up only a few times in the whole book, and always when Zhivago is about to go under.

Act IV · Strelnikov, Varykino, and a rowan tree

In the spring of 1919 the family boards a freight train that takes weeks to crawl east to Varykino, the old estate of Tonya's grandfather near Yuriatin. The train is packed with men being shipped off for forced labour. At a station called Razvilye, Zhivago gets off to stretch his legs, is arrested as a spy and taken to an armoured train to face its commander. This is Strelnikov, a Red commander whose name means "the Shooter." He is notorious for his ruthlessness, and people tremble wherever he goes.

He is young, pale and courteous, and he talks like a lecturer. After a few questions he lets Zhivago go. Only later does Zhivago learn that this man is Pasha Antipov. He didn't die at the front. He was captured, escaped and threw away his old name. He came to see his own life as a mistake that needed correcting, and the revolution as a chance to correct all the world's mistakes at once. When everything was done, he would go back to Lara. It is the most frightening transformation in the book. A man becomes a machine because he is too pure and wants too badly to deserve one woman.

The first year at Varykino is the quietest stretch in the novel. They plant potatoes by day, and at night he writes in his notebook. Ordinary life seems to him a miracle. The next spring he goes into the library in Yuriatin, looks up, and sees Lara reading by the window.

Two months of an affair follow. Tonya is pregnant again. Zhivago can't bear being split in two. He resolves to go home, tell his wife everything and break with Lara. On the ride home from town he is stopped by a band of forest partisans and conscripted as their doctor. He never gets the chance to tell anyone.

They hold him for more than a year. Their leader, Liberius, is the son of the Varykino estate manager; he takes cocaine and loves giving political lectures. Zhivago dresses wounds, amputates limbs, sits through the lectures and can say nothing. At the edge of the camp stands a rowan tree, half buried in snow, with frozen red berries on its branches that birds come to peck. An old peasant woman with something of the witch about her sings a song about a rowan. When Zhivago looks at the tree he thinks of Lara. Seed ④When he finally escapes the camp he walks up to the tree, puts his arms round it and talks to it the way you would talk to a woman: I'll see you again, my beauty, my rowan. At this point it reads as a man half mad with longing, pinning it on a tree.

The darkest thing in the partisan years is Palykh, a fighter so afraid the Whites will take revenge on his family after his death that he kills his wife and children with an axe, and then goes mad. Every word Reds and Whites use to justify killing weighs the same in front of a father holding an axe.

Act V · Tonya's letter, Komarovsky's sledge

He walks back to Yuriatin in rags, so ill he is close to death, and Lara nurses him back. She gives him a letter from Tonya. Tonya, her father, their son and the newborn daughter Masha are being deported abroad as members of the old intelligentsia and are leaving for Paris. In the letter Tonya says she knows he loves Lara, that Lara is a good person, that she has always loved him and he has not loved her, and that she does not blame him. Zhivago reads it and faints.

The political wind in town has changed. Strelnikov has fallen, and his wife Lara and her lover could be arrested at any moment. Then Komarovsky turns up again. He is on his way to become minister of justice in a new government in the Far East, and he offers to take them both with him. Zhivago throws him out.

He and Lara take Katenka and flee to Varykino, long since abandoned, and live there for a few weeks as though it were the end of the world. Snow piles up to the windowsills, wolves howl in the distance at night, and he writes poem after poem by lamplight, all of them about her.

Komarovsky follows them a second time and tells Zhivago that if Lara and the child stay, they will die. Zhivago believes him. What he does next is kept for the slow-motion section below. He lies to Lara that he will follow soon, watches her leave on Komarovsky's sledge, and stays behind.

A few days later someone pushes open the door at Varykino: Strelnikov. He is being hunted and has nowhere left to go, and he has come looking for his wife. The two men talk all night, about Lara and about the revolution. Zhivago tells him that Lara always loved him (that is the gist). At daybreak Zhivago wakes to find that Strelnikov has gone out onto the path and shot himself. He lies in the snow, and the drops of his blood have frozen into little beads, like frozen rowan berries. Payoff ④ ↑The tree Zhivago embraced as if it were Lara ends up growing out of the blood of Lara's husband. One man who loved Lara whispered endearments to a tree; another fell in the colour of its fruit. Pasternak explains none of it. He just lets the red come back once.

Act VI · Moscow, a tram, the same room

In 1922 Zhivago walks back to Moscow with a village boy, Vasya, for company. Seven years of decline follow. He stops practising medicine, writes thin little pamphlets, and lives with Marina, the daughter of the building's caretaker, who bears him two daughters. His old friends Gordon and Dudorov urge him to "re-educate" himself, and he feels they are reciting a lesson.

Evgraf reappears, gives him money and rents him a room in Kamergersky Lane. Zhivago leaves Marina, moves in to write, sorts through his old papers and writes his last poems.

One sultry morning at the end of August 1929 he boards a tram that keeps breaking down. The car is stifling, his heart is weak, and he pushes his way to a window to open it. On the pavement outside, an old woman in a lilac dress is walking. Each time the tram stops for repairs she gets ahead; each time it moves off it passes her again. The old woman is Mademoiselle Fleury from Meliuzeevo, Payoff ③ ↑on her way to the Swiss consulate to apply for permission to go home. Zhivago thinks of the school arithmetic problem about trains leaving at different times and speeds, and of how lives run alongside one another like that, some ahead and some behind. He forces his way off the tram, collapses on the cobbles and dies. She has overtaken him once more, and she will never know it. In Act III she was a lamp wavering down a corridor. She comes back not to move the plot but so that time itself, at an old woman's walking pace, can pass Zhivago by.

His body is laid out in the room on Kamergersky Lane. Lara, back in Moscow from the Far East on some errand, drifts by chance into this street, this building, this room, and only then recognises it. It was Pasha's student room. On Christmas night 1911, this is where she asked Pasha to light that candle. Payoff ② ↑She stands beside the coffin and talks to him. She says their love was not a human affair but something heaven and earth wanted, and that she cannot understand why everything has ended. Zhivago never learned whom the candle he saw from the sledge had been lit for. In Act II it was half a line in a student's head. Here we learn that it lit the point where every fate in the book crosses. Lara decided to shoot in that room, Pasha decided to marry her there, and Zhivago's body ended up there. A few dozen pages later, among the poems, there is one called "Winter Night," and its refrain is that half-line. He finished it after all.

A few days after the funeral Lara goes out and never comes back. The novel gives it one sentence: she was probably arrested in the street and died in one of the countless camps in the north, a nameless number on a list that was later lost.

The epilogue jumps to the summer of 1943, at the front. Gordon and Dudorov, who has just come out of the camps, meet a coarse young woman, Tanya, who works in an army laundry. They hear her story: her mother was the woman of some "minister" and left her with an old crossing-keeper by the railway. They realise she is the daughter of Zhivago and Lara. The man looking out for her now is Evgraf, who has become a major-general. After the war the two old friends sit at a window leafing through a collection of Zhivago's writings that Evgraf has put together. They sense a foretaste of freedom in the air, although it hasn't come yet.

Then comes Part Seventeen: twenty-five poems.

The fuse

Here is the whole book traced back to its fuse. It wasn't the revolution that destroyed Zhivago. It was a look a lawyer gave a sixteen-year-old girl in a hotel room in the winter of 1905. Because of that look Lara wanted someone clean to rescue her, and so there was Pasha. Pasha felt unworthy of the kind of love she gave him, so he went to war and became Strelnikov. Lara went to the front to look for Pasha, and so she met Zhivago. Strelnikov's fall put Lara in danger, and that gave Komarovsky his second excuse to take her away. History is the backdrop, the weather, the shove. But in this book every turn that really changes someone's life is something one person did to another.

Three slow-motion scenes

1 · The sledge passes the window

All the force of this scene comes from who knows what. Pasternak first shows Lara going in and having Pasha light the candle, and only then sends Zhivago's sledge down the same lane. The reader is inside the window and Zhivago is outside it. All he sees is a black hole that a flame has melted in the ice. The light seems to him to be "looking" at the street, a half-line of poetry comes to him, and then the party, Tonya and the cold take his attention away. Two people pass within a pane of ice of each other, and neither knows.

The author gives Zhivago no premonition. He simply puts the candle and the gun in one room and the poem in the head of the man going past. The most important moment in a poet's life is often one he doesn't recognise as important. The scene acts that out, and the reader has to wait hundreds of pages, until there is a coffin in that room, to understand what they saw.

2 · Shooting at a dead tree

The partisans clash with the Whites. Across a clearing in the forest the attackers advance: schoolboys and teenage volunteers in uniform. As they come closer, Zhivago thinks their faces look like the children of families he knows. A doctor isn't supposed to fight, but the telephonist beside him falls, and he picks up the rifle. He doesn't want to hit anyone, so he aims at a charred dead tree in the middle of the clearing and fires at it, shot after shot. Some of his bullets hit people anyway.

Afterwards he checks the bodies. Round the neck of the dead partisan telephonist hangs a little amulet with a scrap of paper sewn inside: Psalm 91, "He that dwelleth in the secret place of the most High," copied out full of mistakes, passed down among the people as a charm against bullets. Then he looks at a young White soldier he wounded, who is still breathing. On the boy's chest is a small gold locket, and inside it is the same psalm. Zhivago saves the boy, dresses him in partisan clothes to hide him, and later lets him go.

The method is symmetry. Men on both sides go out to shoot one another wearing the same prayer. The author offers no comment and simply sets the two amulets side by side. Shooting at the tree comes from the same logic: a man who refuses to take sides can't even manage "not choosing" on a battlefield.

3 · The sledge goes over the hill

Varykino. Komarovsky has convinced Zhivago that if Lara stays she will die, and that if he stays he will die too, but only Lara can leave. Zhivago knows she won't go without him, so he lies to her. Go on ahead, he says, I'll get the horse ready and follow, I'll be there in a day or two. Lara believes him, or chooses to.

The camera stays on Zhivago. He stands on the porch and watches the sledge go down the slope, round a copse, and reappear far off on a hillside. Over and over in his head he says goodbye, and only afterwards realises he never even waved. He goes back inside and walks among the things she touched before she left. Then a kind of fever takes hold of him: he has to write her down.

The rhythm is hold, then release. The dialogue of the lie is short, cold, almost businesslike. Once the sledge is gone the sentences suddenly lengthen, and landscape, light and heartbeat all pour in. Pasternak lets Zhivago gain his poetry in the very moment of loss. That is the most beautiful thing in the book and the most unsettling: the poet's work is paid for with the person he let go. Did he do it to save her, or so that he could sit down alone and write about her? The novel doesn't say.

The core device: a novel that ends in a book of poems

What sets Doctor Zhivago apart isn't its plot but its structure. Sixteen parts of prose tell one man's life, and the seventeenth is twenty-five poems he wrote. It isn't an appendix. The prose is the raw material and the poems are what that material became. Things the reader has seen in the prose (the Christmas candle, the wolves at Varykino, partings, autumn, Holy Week) come back in the poems, distilled into another language.

The logic runs like this. By any ordinary measure Zhivago's life is a failure. He didn't save Lara, he couldn't keep his family together, and he died beside a broken-down tram. If the novel stopped at Part Sixteen it would be a tragedy about a man crushed by history. Part Seventeen does the accounts again: his life was finally converted into a few dozen pages of poetry, and those poems have outlived Strelnikov's armoured train and every one of Liberius's lectures. In the first poem, "Hamlet," an actor walks onstage and feels a thousand opera glasses trained on him in the dark, and he asks his "Father," if it is possible, to let this cup pass from him. Hamlet and Christ in Gethsemane become the same figure.

Two features of the book that critics keep complaining about belong to this device. The first is coincidence. Far too many people run into each other by chance across the vastness of Russia. Pasternak meant it. The web is the subject. Life, for him, is not a chain of cause and effect but a matter of running in parallel, and the arithmetic problem on the tram is its parable. The second is his view of history. At one point Zhivago reflects (this is the gist) that no one "makes" history; it is like a forest turning green in spring, and you never see it grow, you only see that it has grown. Tolstoy came close to this idea, Zhivago thinks, without saying it outright. That is why the book has no hero and no mover of history. Strelnikov thought he was pushing history along, and history crumpled him up and threw him away like a used sheet of paper.

What holds up is the refusal to treat a person as an instrument of history. What is literary need rather than argument is this: setting "life" against "history" takes resources. Zhivago has a professor for a father-in-law, an estate to retreat to and a half-brother who protects him. The first question below takes this on directly.

What it makes you think about

One · People around you are starving, and you say life matters more than revolution. Is that clear sight, or a luxury only the well-fed can afford?

Strelnikov and Zhivago talk all night at Varykino. Stretch that conversation into a question. Someone who wants only to live well, love well and write poems faces a movement that aims to change the lives of millions. Do they have the right to opt out?

The case for Strelnikov. The strongest argument isn't ideology. It's where he comes from. He is a railwayman's son. The shooting of 1905 happened on his doorstep. He has seen how workers live, and he has seen how a man like Komarovsky can treat a sixteen-year-old girl as a toy. And Zhivago? He grew up in a professor's house. In the worst winter his family can burn their bookshelves, and at the darkest moment a brother turns up with flour. He says people are not raw material to be remade, but the old world always treated most people as raw material. "Leave my private life alone" is always said first by the people who already had a private life under the old order. You can condemn the revolution's methods, but you can't pretend that what it overthrew was good. Zhivago himself cheered on the day of October.

The case for Zhivago. The strongest argument is how it turned out. Strelnikov turned himself into an executioner for the sake of a clean future, and the machine he served ended up hunting him as a traitor. Liberius lectured for a year and a half, and what happened in his camp was Palykh's axe. Zhivago isn't claiming the old world was good. He is saying that any argument that asks you to set aside love, conscience and pity for the person in front of you, for the sake of some larger goal, will in the end devour the larger goal as well. He doesn't ignore hunger. He's a doctor, and all his life he treats the wounded, Red and White alike. What he refuses isn't responsibility. It's a language that turns particular people into abstract numbers.

Where does the author stand? With Zhivago, but he doesn't make Strelnikov a villain; on that night Strelnikov is one of the most sincere people in the book. Bring it home: the "bigger picture" at work, the "for the good of the family" at home, both ask you to put your small things aside first. The question isn't whether to care about large things. It's whether, when the large thing asks you to be ruthless with the person in front of you, you still recognise yourself.

Two · You're certain that if the person you love stays with you she will die. Her only way out is to leave with a man who once hurt her, and she won't go of her own accord. Do you lie to her?

At Varykino Zhivago decides to lie Lara onto the sledge. Is it love, or a weak man finding himself an exit?

The case that he was right. The danger is real. With Strelnikov fallen, Lara is certain to be hunted as his wife. Komarovsky has transport, papers and an official post in the Far East; he is the only person who can get her and the child out. If Zhivago tells the truth, Lara will stay and wait to die with him, and so will Katenka. Sometimes loving someone means making a decision for them that they can't make themselves, and then carrying it alone. He gets nothing out of the lie except the poems.

The case that he was wrong. Lara is a grown woman. The deepest wound of her life came at sixteen, when a man decided what would happen to her body and her life, and that man was Komarovsky. Now Zhivago, with a lie, hands her back to that same man, without asking her a single question. He believes he is saving her, but he is taking away, for the second time in her life, her right to say no to her own fate. Worse, the lie spares him from saying goodbye to her face and from making the decision while looking her in the eye. How much of that is sacrifice and how much is cowardice? And what came after (losing her child, dying in a camp) suggests that the "way out" didn't take her anywhere good.

The author passes no verdict. He only has Lara say at the coffin that she doesn't understand why everything ended this way. Bring it home: hiding a diagnosis from your parents, making a choice for your child "for your own good." When you say "I did it for her," it's worth asking one more question: was it for her, or so that I could bear it?

Three · Is a book worth having the people closest to you sent to prison? The question Pasternak himself faced

This one lies outside the novel. When Pasternak handed his manuscript to a foreigner, he knew the consequences. His lover Olga Ivinskaya, widely seen as one of the models for Lara, had already been arrested in 1949 because of her connection with him and spent several years in the camps. Soon after his death she and her daughter were arrested again, on charges connected with the book's foreign royalties.

The case that he should have published. The book was his life's testimony, one of the very few accounts of that era from his generation that no censor had touched. Unpublished, it would have died in a drawer, the author would have died, and the private memory of a whole period would have had no one to speak for it. If a society demands that writers stay silent forever to protect the people around them, the one making those people suffer isn't the writer. It's the society.

The case that he shouldn't have. He didn't bear the cost alone. He was a world-famous poet, and international fame was a kind of shield; in the end he was never arrested. The one who went to the camps was a woman with no such shield. Buying your own immortality with someone else's freedom leaves an account that won't balance, however fine the thing you bought.

Where did the author stand? He answered with what he did: he published. After his death the bill went to Ivinskaya. Bring it home: should a whistleblower speak up if colleagues will lose their jobs too? Should a founder bet everything if the family has to share the risk? The hard part isn't whether you're willing to pay for your convictions. It's whether you have the right to make other people pay for them.

Motifs: what a poet keeps returning to

All his life Pasternak wrote about one thing: life itself is a miracle, larger than any theory about life. His 1922 collection My Sister, Life is about the summer of 1917, and what it describes is rain, gardens and falling in love, with the revolution only in the air. Thirty years later, Zhivago's notebooks are still about the same things.

The second obsession is the place of the artist. His autobiographical prose Safe Conduct (1931) describes how he turned from music and philosophy to poetry. Zhivago is his final answer: a poet is neither prophet nor soldier but a witness for life. Pasternak translated Hamlet, and the prince in the first poem of the cycle is not hesitating. He has accepted a script someone else wrote and still means to play his part to the end.

The third is Christianity, but not the Church's kind. He came from a Jewish family, and in later life he identified more and more with a Christianity of the individual and of sacrifice. In the novel, Uncle Vedenyapin says, more or less, that with Christ people stopped being numbers. The last poems in the cycle are about Holy Week.

Controversy and criticism

Publication and the Cold War. From the start, the novel's publication in the West and the Nobel Prize were tangled up in the Cold War. CIA documents declassified in 2014 confirmed that the agency secretly paid for a Russian-language edition, which was handed out to Soviet visitors in 1958 at venues including the Brussels World's Fair, to make an impact inside the USSR. There is no evidence that the CIA arranged the Nobel, but the question of whether the book owed its fame to politics still gets asked.

The Soviet campaign. After the award Pasternak was expelled from the Union of Soviet Writers, and the papers were full of denunciations, many by people who openly admitted they hadn't read the book. He first cabled the Swedish Academy that he was "infinitely grateful, touched, proud, astonished, abashed," and a few days later sent a second telegram declining the prize. These facts can be verified. Memoirs differ on the motives and conversations behind them, so we won't go into those here.

Literary criticism. The sharpest attack came from Nabokov, who dismissed the book more than once as clumsy, trite and melodramatic (the gist). Milder criticism makes three points. There are too many coincidences, like a nineteenth-century popular novel. Characters keep delivering long monologues and sound like loudspeakers for the author. And Lara is turned into something close to myth: earth, Russia, life itself. She is loved and explained by every man in the book but rarely written as a person with a will of her own, a point feminist critics often make. Some also find Zhivago too passive and self-pitying, and the author too fond of him.

Translations and the film. The 1958 English translation by Max Hayward and Manya Harari was done in a hurry and later judged to have flattened the original in many places. Richard Pevear and Larissa Volokhonsky published a new translation in 2010. David Lean's 1965 film was a huge success, but it changes a great deal. It adds a frame story in which Evgraf searches for his niece, and many of its scenes, its dialogue and its famous theme belong to the film, not the novel. For many readers, half of "Zhivago" is Omar Sharif.

Crossovers

  • Political philosophy · Arendt on the private realm: In The Origins of Totalitarianism (1951), Hannah Arendt argues that totalitarian rule destroys private life as well as public life, leaving people as isolated atoms. That private realm is exactly what Zhivago defends. The difference is that Arendt prized public action and would not necessarily have approved of a retreat into private life.
  • Moral philosophy · Bernard Williams on moral luck: In a 1976 paper, Williams takes the case of Gauguin, a painter who leaves his family to paint, and suggests that whether the choice was justified seems to depend on whether he succeeds. Pasternak's risky publication and Zhivago's trade of a parting for poems pose the same problem. The difference is that Gauguin carried the bet himself, while Zhivago's cost and Pasternak's fell on the people they loved.
  • Daoism · Zhuangzi's Hundun: In the Zhuangzi, two kindly friends set out to give Hundun, a being with no openings in his face, the seven openings everyone else has, boring one a day. On the seventh day Hundun dies. Strelnikov's urge to "correct" life is that kind of well-meant boring; Zhivago's insistence that life is not raw material is close to Zhuangzi. The difference is that Zhuangzi counsels withdrawal, while Zhivago keeps practising medicine and keeps loving deeply.

Ten lines to take away

① From a sledge, a medical student sees a candle, half a line of verse comes to him, and he forgets it.

② The candle was lit at the request of a girl with a gun in her pocket.

③ The man who shared his father's last bottle later took the woman he loved.

④ The purest boy in the book, wanting to deserve one woman, became "the Shooter."

⑤ He sincerely cheered in October, and the book doesn't pretend he didn't.

⑥ Men on both sides of the battle wore the same prayer around their necks.

⑦ He whispered love to a rowan tree; later another man who loved her fell in blood the colour of its berries.

⑧ He sent her away with a lie, and spent the nights that followed writing poems.

⑨ An old Swiss woman on the pavement overtook his tram and never knew whom she had passed.

⑩ Almost nothing of his life survived except Part Seventeen. He finished that half-line after all.
