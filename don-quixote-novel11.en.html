Novel Deep-Read · Miguel de Cervantes · No.11

Don Quixote

Don Quijote de la Mancha · Miguel de Cervantes · Part I 1605 · Part II 1615

中文 →

A country squire in his fifties sells off part of his land to buy books, reads chivalric romances day and night, and dries his brain out.
One morning before dawn he digs a rusted suit of armour out of the attic. The helmet has no visor, so he builds one from pasteboard, tests it with his sword, and it shatters. He builds a second one. This time he does not test it.
He gives his broken-down horse a name, gives himself a name, and gives a third name to a girl in the next village he has never spoken to. Then he leads the horse out the back gate onto the plain of La Mancha.
The happiest thought he has that morning is that someone will one day write all this down — and as he rides he drafts the opening paragraph on that future author's behalf.

This page spoils the entire novel—what happens after the windmills, whether Sancho's island is an island, who finally defeats him, and what he says on his deathbed. There is no suspense here to protect: for four hundred years everyone has known that a man once charged a windmill. What almost nobody knows is that he is brought down by his own neighbour with a well-meant hoax—and that at the very end he recovers his sanity, which is the cruellest page in the book.

In one sentence

A country gentleman who reads chivalric romances as history decides to become a knight-errant himself; he rides out three times, taking along a farmer who has been promised the governorship of an island. In Part I the world beats him black and blue; in Part II everyone he meets has already read the book about him and stages elaborate theatre for his benefit. Finally a neighbour disguised as the Knight of the White Moon unhorses him and sentences him to a year at home with his weapons laid down. He goes back to his village, falls ill, wakes up sane, announces that he is not Don Quixote but Alonso Quijano the Good, curses the books of chivalry, dictates his will, and dies—while the man weeping at his bedside, begging him not to be sane, begging him to get up and go be a shepherd, is Sancho.

Coordinates

Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra (1547–1616) had almost nothing in his life go smoothly. He fought at Lepanto in 1571, took three gunshot wounds, and lost the use of his left hand. In 1575, on the voyage home, Barbary corsairs captured him; he spent five years as a slave in Algiers, organised four escape attempts, failed all four, and was ransomed in 1580. Back in Spain he requisitioned supplies for the Armada and later collected taxes, and was jailed at least once over his accounts. He badly wanted to be a playwright, and the Madrid stage of his day belonged to Lope de Vega.

He was fifty-eight when Part I appeared in 1605, and Europe began pirating it immediately. In 1614 a writer using the name Avellaneda rushed out a spurious sequel whose preface mocked Cervantes for being old, poor and one-handed. Cervantes published his own Part II in 1615 and wrote that whole business directly into the novel—the most astonishing move in the book. He died in April 1616, days before Shakespeare.

Its place is usually summarised in one phrase: the first modern novel. That claim deserves a discount (see the criticism section), but one thing is not in dispute: before this book, characters in fiction did not know they were in a book; after it, the craft has never managed to forget.

The story

Act I · A man reads himself into somebody else

La Mancha is a high plateau in central Spain: windy, flat, thinly villaged. The book opens with the most famous refusal in literary history—the narrator declines to name the village.

"In a village of La Mancha, whose name I have no wish to recall…"Part I, ch. 1, opening (translations here are my own)

The village holds a hidalgo—minor gentry, noble rank, very little money—of about fifty, whose surname the book gives in several versions. He has a housekeeper, a niece, a bony horse and a hound, and his entire life consists of libros de caballerías, the bestselling pulp of the age: knights-errant riding out, killing giants, rescuing princesses, keeping faith with a lady. He does not merely read them; he argues seriously with the village priest and barber about which knight was the better fighter.

Then he crosses the line: he stops considering these stories and starts considering them history—and concludes that the profession is currently vacant and somebody ought to take it up again.

The first thing he does is give out names. The horse becomes Rocinante (roughly, "formerly a nag, now first among them"); he becomes Don Quixote de la Mancha, appending his region the way the romances do; and since a knight without a lady is a tree without leaves, he remembers a good-looking farm girl called Aldonza Lorenzo over in El Toboso, a girl he has never once spoken to, and renames her Dulcinea del Toboso. This heroine of the novel never appears in it and never says a word.

He rides out alone the first time. At dusk he finds a roadside inn and sees a castle: the two prostitutes in the doorway are noble ladies, the swineherd's horn is a fanfare. He asks the "lord of the castle" to dub him a knight. The innkeeper is a shrewd old operator who can see the man is harmlessly mad and good entertainment for the guests, so he plays along: in the stableyard, using the feed ledger for a holy book, he mutters something nobody can follow, taps the man twice on the shoulder with a sword, and one of the women buckles on his belt. The first deception in the book is not malice, it is accommodation—partly to save trouble, partly for the fun of it. The innkeeper also advises him to carry money and clean shirts, and to get himself a squire.

Riding on at daybreak he hears a child crying in a wood. A prosperous farmer has tied a fifteen-year-old shepherd boy, Andrés, to a tree and is whipping him; the boy is shouting about nine months of unpaid wages. Don Quixote levels his lance, orders the boy untied, and makes the farmer swear to pay every last coin. The farmer swears; the boy is released. The knight rides away delighted, congratulating himself the whole way on having settled a great matter on his very first day in business.seed ①

That afternoon he comes to grief: he demands that a party of silk merchants concede that Dulcinea is the most beautiful woman alive, they reply that they have never seen her and could he produce a portrait, he charges, his horse stumbles, and a muleteer breaks the lance and beats him with the pieces. A neighbour finds him, lays him across a donkey, and carts him home.

The household is already in an uproar. The priest and the barber go through his library shelf by shelf—the celebrated scrutiny of the books: the guilty are thrown into the yard and burned, a few are reprieved. Then they brick up the doorway of the room and plaster over it.

Which raises a problem: what do they tell him when he wakes up and goes looking for his library? They settle on a story. An enchanter came riding a dragon and carried the room and the books away. Don Quixote does not merely accept this without suspicion; he supplies the enchanter's name.seed ②

Act II · Two men on the road: windmills, sheep, and a helmet that is not a helmet

He has remembered the innkeeper's third piece of advice. He recruits a poor, honest, proverb-spouting farmer from his own village, Sancho Panza, on these terms: come with me, and when I conquer an ínsula—an archaic word for island, which Sancho never does quite work out—you shall govern it. Sancho leaves his wife and children and rides off on his grey donkey.seed ③

Not far outside the village, thirty or forty windmills appear on the plain.

"Look there," says Don Quixote, "thirty monstrous giants." Sancho says those are windmills, the arms are sails, they turn when the wind blows. The knight replies: you are not versed in this profession; they are giants; if you are frightened, go and say your prayers. He spurs his horse, shouting at them not to flee. The wind rises, the sails begin to turn, the lance goes into one of them, and horse and man are lifted and flung. Sancho arrives to find him flat on the ground and the lance in pieces.

Sancho says: didn't I tell you they were windmills? Don Quixote's answer is the engine of the entire book. The same enchanter who stole my library turned those giants into windmills at the last moment, to rob me of the glory of defeating them. He does not concede, does not admit error, is not even downcast. He simply promotes his opponent.

Then come the escalating battles: two friars mistaken for magicians abducting a princess; two distant flocks of sheep taken for two armies (he charges in, spits seven or eight of them, and has several teeth knocked out by a shepherd's sling); a rhythmic pounding in the dark by a river that turns out at dawn to be six water-driven fulling hammers. Every time, Sancho states the plain fact first, and every time it makes no difference. In the middle of one duel, with both swords raised, the narration simply stops—"the author of this history found no more written down"—and Cervantes leaves the reader hanging for a full chapter before producing a provenance: he bought a bundle of Arabic manuscript in the Toledo market, written by the historian Cide Hamete Benengeli. The swords then come down.

On a wet road they meet a man riding with a brass basin on his head to keep the rain off. Don Quixote identifies it instantly as Mambrino's helmet, the invulnerable prize of the romances, and charges; the man, a village barber on his rounds, drops the basin and runs. The knight claps it on his head and wears it from then on. Sancho circles him for a while and dares only to say that it does look remarkably like a barber's basin.seed ④

Act III · Penance in the mountains, and a knight carried home in a cage

Dodging the authorities, the two head into the Sierra Morena. Up there Don Quixote realises he has neglected an essential piece of business: every great knight in history has run mad for his lady, and he has not. So he goes mad on the spot—strips off his breeches, turns somersaults, batters himself against rocks, sings laments—and sends Sancho off to El Toboso with a love letter. Sancho loses the letter on the way and simply invents a reply.

The priest and the barber, out searching the hills, run into them there, along with Dorotea, a well-off young woman abandoned by a faithless lover. Together they assemble a plan for getting the madman home: Dorotea will pose as the dispossessed princess of the faraway kingdom of Micomicón and beg the greatest knight in the world to escort her home and kill the usurping giant. Don Quixote agrees at once—he is brought home not by being refuted, but by being handed a bigger mission.

Coming down out of the hills they meet a boy leading a donkey. It is Andrés.payoff ① ↑ He is skin and bone, and he starts swearing the moment he sees the knight: the minute you rode off, that man tied me straight back up and whipped me twice as hard, counting the strokes out in your honour, saying that since a knight had won me justice he might as well give me a full measure of it—and I have not seen a penny to this day. His last words are a request: sir, if you ever again see me being beaten to death, do me the kindness of leaving me alone. Don Quixote is still standing there explaining that he was deceived that day when the boy is already out of earshot. Sancho digs bread and cheese out of his bag; the boy takes it and runs.

The seed is planted in chapter four and paid off in chapter thirty-one, with most of a volume of comedy in between. At the time of planting it is pure farce: a mad old man solemnly setting something right. What makes Cervantes merciless is that he sends the beneficiary back on his own legs to present the bill in person—the consequence is not something the reader is asked to infer, it walks back into the scene hungry and swearing.

The inn where the party stops becomes the busiest stage in Part I: several sets of separated lovers settle their accounts there, and one night Don Quixote sleepwalks into the loft and hacks open some wineskins, taking them for giants. Then the owner of the basin turns up.payoff ④ ↑ The barber recognises his property and wants it back; Don Quixote maintains that it is Mambrino's helmet. So the room settles the matter in the most civilised way available: it takes a vote. The priest's party, straight-faced and stifling laughter, testify that the object is indeed a helmet and not a basin. Sancho coins the finest word in the book: baciyelmo, basin-helmet, both at once. What makes the scene beautiful is that it translates all the madness of Part I into one cool proposition: what a thing is depends on how many people in the room are willing to say so. The knight wins this round, and he wins it by mass perjury.

Finally the priest and the barber build a wooden cage, lift him into it while he sleeps, mount it on an ox-cart, and explain the arrangement with a prophecy: he must be carried home like this to fulfil his destiny. And so a self-declared knight-errant is hauled back to his village in a cage, drawn by oxen, in the name of a victory. Part I ends there.

Act IV · Part II: everyone he meets has read Part I

Part II opens with Don Quixote a month in bed. His visitor is Sansón Carrasco, a young graduate just back from Salamanca, bearing news: your deeds are in print, and twelve thousand copies are said to have been run off. Sancho asks anxiously whether he is in it. You are, says the graduate, including the business with your donkey.

This is a rare change of track in the history of fiction: in the world of Part II, Part I is a bestseller. From here on his reputation arrives everywhere before he does, and people no longer handle him by avoiding the madman—they build sets and stage scenes for him out of the book.

The third sortie begins at El Toboso, where a boast Sancho has been making for three years is about to come due: he has always claimed he carried the letter and met Dulcinea. In the event the village is pitch dark, all barking dogs and braying donkeys, and the two of them wander until dawn without locating the princess's palace. Sancho improvises: he parks his master in a wood, then points at three farm girls riding by on donkeys and swears the middle one is Dulcinea, kneeling in the road and praising her beauty in the most syrupy terms he can manage. The girls swear at him to get out of the way; one falls off her donkey.

Don Quixote stares and sees nothing—three peasant women.payoff ② ↑ So he draws the only inference his logic permits: the enchanter who persecutes him has struck again and turned Dulcinea into a coarse-handed village girl, and only he can see the transformation. He is heartbroken. The enchanter the priest and the barber invented on the spot to fool him has now been picked up by his own squire and turned against the thing he cares about most—and from this moment the entire remainder of his journey has one purpose: to disenchant Dulcinea.seed ⑤

This is the structural pivot of the book. For a thousand pages the division of labour has been fixed: Don Quixote sees the illusion, Sancho states the fact. Now it inverts—Sancho manufactures a miracle in his master's own idiom, and Don Quixote, clear-eyed in front of three farm women, is forced to explain away what he is actually seeing. And the lie is not told to harm anyone; it is told to avoid being caught out—the motive behind most deceptions is not malice, it is embarrassment.

A Knight of the Mirrors appears on the road, his armour covered in little mirrors, claiming to have beaten Don Quixote before. In the event his horse refuses to move and Don Quixote knocks him flat with one pass. The visor comes up: the face belongs to Sansón Carrasco.seed ⑥ His plan had been to win and then, on his knightly authority, sentence the old man to a year at home—to cure him. Instead he lost. He gets up with several teeth loosened and a score to settle.

Then comes the heaviest stop of all: a Duke and Duchess who have read Part I, are bored, and decide to bring these two live specimens into their estate and mount a full production out of the book—a false countess, a false enchanter, a false bearded duenna, and a wooden horse the pair are blindfolded onto while servants work bellows and hold torches to their faces so they believe they are flying through a region of fire.

The cruellest scene in the production is the announcement of the terms of disenchantment: a servant costumed as an enchanter declares that Dulcinea can be restored only if Sancho gives himself three thousand three hundred lashes on the bare backside.payoff ⑤ ↑ Sancho refuses flatly. It is the most elegant piece of accounting in the novel: the enchantment was something Sancho made up on the spot to fob off his master, and now other people have taken the lie as fact and legislated on it, and the bill is payable out of his own skin. He haggles over it for the rest of the book—so much per stroke—and finally slips into a wood at night and lays into a tree trunk, howling louder than anyone.

Then the Duke makes good on the promise from Act II: Sancho is sent off to govern.payoff ③ ↑ The posting is a town of fewer than a thousand people on ducal land, passed off as the isle of Barataria—the root of the name suggests cheapness, a thing done on the cheap—with the entire population briefed to play along. It is designed as a joke, and the joke gets away from them: Sancho judges superbly. A woman accuses a man of rape; Sancho makes the man hand over his purse, then tells the woman to take it back from him, and when she clamps onto it with both hands and cannot be shaken off, he rules: if you had defended yourself with half the strength you just used on that purse, he could not have touched you.

The one exception is dinner. A court physician stands by the table and, with a whalebone wand, sends every dish away—the fruit is too damp, the stew too hot, the partridge unwholesome—all of it in the governor's own interest. He goes hungry for ten days. On the tenth night a staged enemy attack has him strapped between two shields like a parcel, tipped over, and trampled by a crowd. At dawn Sancho goes to the stable himself, gets his donkey, takes off the governor's clothes, puts his own back on, and delivers a startlingly lucid farewell: naked I came to this government and naked I leave it; I have gained nothing and I owe nothing; governing is not work for a man like me; take your town back, I am going home to my fields. In ten days, a man everybody had cast as the fool has done the most human thing anyone does in this book.

Act V · The Knight of the White Moon, and a man who gets his mind back

Then something happens with no precedent in the history of the novel. At an inn the two of them overhear guests reading a book aloud—Avellaneda's spurious sequel, in which Don Quixote goes to a tournament at Zaragoza. He denies the version of himself in that book and changes his itinerary on the spot: since the false book says I went to Zaragoza, I shall not go—I shall go to Barcelona. Later he meets Álvaro Tarfe, a character out of the fake sequel, and has him sign a statement before a notary that the Don Quixote he used to know is not this one. Cervantes does not abuse his rival in a preface; he makes his enemy's book into a prop and has his own hero legally void it.

On the beach at Barcelona a Knight of the White Moon blocks his way: concede that my lady is more beautiful than Dulcinea, or fight; if you lose, go home and lay down your arms for a year. Don Quixote's reply is the least mad sentence in the book: I can die, but I will not say she is not beautiful. The other man's horse is faster and his frame heavier, and one pass puts horse and rider on the sand with a lance point at his face. Flat on his back, his voice muffled inside the helmet, he says: Dulcinea is the most beautiful woman in the world and I am the most worthless knight in it; drive it home.payoff ⑥ ↑ The visor comes up—Sansón Carrasco again. He lost the first time; this year he came with a fast horse and heavy armour, and the only thing he has wanted for twelve months is to cure his neighbour. His explanation afterwards is that the whole village wants the old gentleman to stay home.

The road back is slow. He plans to spend his sentenced year as a shepherd and has already picked pastoral names for himself and Sancho; a herd of pigs runs over him in a meadow; entering the village he hears two boys quarrelling over a cage—"you'll never see it again as long as you live"—and takes the words for an omen. The first thing he does at home is go to bed with a fever.

When he wakes he says: God has had mercy on me; my judgement is clear. He sends for the priest, the graduate and the barber and announces that he now detests the books of chivalry, that he is not Don Quixote but Alonso Quijano the Good—the name the village used to call him by—and that he wants a confessor and a notary.

The three men at the bedside are appalled. Everyone who has spent two volumes trying to cure him spends the moment of his actual cure trying to talk him out of it. The graduate protests that this is no way to talk when they are about to set off as shepherds. Sancho weeps hardest of all: get up, we'll go out to the fields and be shepherds, the lady Dulcinea may be waiting behind any hedge; and if it's the defeat that has done this to you, blame me, I didn't tighten the saddle girth. Don Quixote only says:

"In last year's nests there are no birds this year."Part II, ch. 74

Two clauses of the will are worth recording: Sancho's accounts are wiped clean and he is left extra money; and the estate goes to the niece, on condition that before marrying she establishes that her husband does not know what a book of chivalry is—if she marries a man who reads them, the inheritance is revoked and given to charity. He dies three days later. The notary makes a point of recording that he had never found, in any book of chivalry, a knight-errant who died in his own bed like this, quietly.

On the last page Cide Hamete hangs up his pen and lets it speak, warning off anyone who might want to move it.

"For me alone Don Quixote was born, and I for him; he knew how to act and I to write."Part II, ch. 74, closing lines

Put the five acts together: where is the fuse? Not in the reading that drove him mad—the madness is the premise, not the event. The fuse is that night at the inn, when the innkeeper judged the man harmlessly mad and decided not to contradict him but to perform a dubbing ceremony instead. From that moment nobody in the book ever tells him the truth in earnest: the priest fobs him off with an enchanter, Dorotea plays a princess for him, a roomful of people vote a basin into a helmet, Sancho conjures up a Dulcinea, a Duke and Duchess erect an entire theatre, and Carrasco puts on armour twice to act the part of a knight. He is never once refuted; he is simply humoured all the way to the end. And the deceptions get more expensive as they go: the first costs the innkeeper half an hour, the last costs him his life—the only hoax that works is the one that cures him, and it kills him.

Two slow-motion replays

1 · The windmills: why this page outweighs the rest of the book

The scene runs barely a page, and the whole technique lies in having both pictures present at once. Cervantes does not write "he mistook windmills for giants"—that is commentary. He lets Don Quixote name the giants first, so that thirty flailing monsters stand up in the reader's mind, and only then lets Sancho say windmills. The reader is therefore looking at two things simultaneously and has to decide which one to stand next to. That is the machine this book invented: one scene, two realities, and a man in between who will not give ground.

The pacing is calculated too: charge, wind, sails turning, lance in, horse and rider thrown—each clause shorter than the last, keeping time with his acceleration; then an immediate cut to Sancho plodding up on his donkey and one flat sentence to let the tension out. And the finest move is the last one: he does not concede, he revises the specification. Which is what allows the comedy to run indefinitely—only a man who can never be defeated can be knocked down over and over.

One widespread misconception is worth flagging: the English idiom tilting at windmills was distilled out of this scene by later readers. The slogan is not in the book, and not one line of it tells you that you are watching the tragedy of idealism—that is four centuries of readers painting coat after coat.

2 · The deathbed: the one moment of clarity nobody can stand

The technique on this page is to reverse every position and offer no explanation.

For two volumes, "cure him" has been the consensus of every sane person in the book. Now he has cured himself, in the most thorough way available, handing back even the name. And every single person at the bedside argues against it: the graduate dangles the shepherd plan, Sancho takes the blame for the defeat onto himself, insisting he had not tightened the saddle girth. The people who most wanted him sane cannot tolerate a second of his actual sanity. Cervantes adds no comment; he simply sets their speeches down one after another.

There is also a nearly silent piece of design here: the protagonist of this book dies under a name that is not the protagonist's. Don Quixote does not die—he is revoked. What dies is Alonso Quijano, a countryman we glimpsed for a few lines in chapter one and whose surname was never even settled. And the maddest-sounding clause in his will concerns his niece's marriage: the man who has just renounced the books of chivalry uses his estate as a weapon to stop somebody else from reading. Sanity has not made him gentler; it has only redirected the force.

As for Sancho's line—we'll go be shepherds, the lady Dulcinea may be waiting behind some hedge—it is the last transfer of faith in the book: what the master has put down, the squire picks up. Critics call this the quixotisation of Sancho, and it is not accomplished in a moment; it is walked into over two volumes.

The book's core device: a novel about readers

If the engine of a novel like The Three-Body Problem is a physical premise, the engine here is a set of narrative devices, and by 1605 this book had already spent most of the inheritance later fiction would live on.

First, the author demotes himself to a relayer. The "true author" is Cide Hamete, the manuscript is old paper bought in a market and rendered by a Moorish translator, and the narrator takes care to remind us that people of that nation are habitual liars, so the praise of Don Quixote should probably be discounted. Nothing in the book is therefore underwritten by anyone—what you are holding announces itself as a translated, dubiously sourced, second-hand history. It also stops mid-duel in Part I, sword in the air, because the manuscript ran out, which is another way of telling you that you are reading a text somebody assembled, not events that occurred.

Second, the lethal move: Part II writes Part I's readers into the story. In 1615 this was invention out of thin air, and it brings more than novelty; it brings a new predicament. When everybody has read the book about you, you will never again meet anyone without a script. The ducal estate is crueller than every beating in Part I because Part I's world merely knocked him over, while Part II's world builds sets from his book in order to watch him perform. Fame is written here as a superior form of imprisonment.

Third, it hands "what is true" over to a head count. The basin-helmet is the coolest statement of the device: an object's properties are settled by a vote of those present, and the voters cast their ballots knowing perfectly well that it is false—because it is amusing, because everyone else is doing it, because nobody wants to spoil the fun.

Two honest footnotes. None of this was invented from nothing: the found-manuscript trick belonged to the chivalric romances themselves, and Cervantes is inverting it; Lazarillo de Tormes (1554) was already writing low-life realism. And terms like "metafiction" and "unreliable narrator" are twentieth-century gifts—he did these things, but not from a theory.

What it makes you think about

Question 1
Should he have been cured, if the price is that he is dead three days later?

Everyone can recite the romantic answer: leave him alone, let him dream. Take the opposite side first, and it is harder than it looks.

The dream is not his alone, and neither is the bill. List the actual damage: Andrés whipped twice as hard and never paid; a barber's basin stolen; sheep speared; a niece watching the family land sold off year by year; and Sancho, who left his wife and children, has been stoned, trampled and beaten, and now owes three thousand three hundred lashes. "Let him dream" is an easy sentence to say when you are not the one settling the account. Life works the same way: the person who pays for a grand plan is usually not the person who proposed it.

Further: aestheticising madness is a privilege of people who have not lived with a madman. The only character who never finds any of it romantic is the niece, who is simply watching the estate disappear. Carrasco put on armour and took two beatings not to humiliate anyone, but because the village concluded that the old gentleman had to be kept at home. That is the one time in this book that somebody actually does something instead of standing around enjoying the show.

Now the other side, at full strength and without sentiment. First, look at the method of the cure: Carrasco does not use an argument, he uses a better hoax. He does not persuade Don Quixote—he dresses as a knight, defeats him under the rules of chivalric romance, and binds him with a knight's word of honour. He concedes the legitimacy of the fictional world first, then wins a round inside it. The therapy is self-contradictory: it destroys the illusion by reinforcing it. What actually gets interrupted is not the delusion but the capacity to act.

Second, look at what being cured gets him. A bed, a will, three days. In the year before it he covered hundreds of miles, saw the sea, walked through a printing house, and got an illiterate farmer ten days of genuinely competent government. The last act of the lucid Alonso Quijano is to legislate against his niece marrying a man who reads for pleasure. If that is what normal means, this book's opinion of normal is fairly savage.

Bring it home: you know somebody who is doing something you are certain will fail—quitting to write a novel, changing careers at forty, sinking their savings into a shop that will close. The hard question is not whether to intervene, it is by what method. Reasoning usually fails; what works is cutting off the resources, dismantling the stage, or doing what Carrasco did—entering their logic and closing the door from inside. And almost every method that works requires deceiving them once. This book lays the trade openly on the table: you have a good chance of pulling the person back, and a good chance that once back, they will have nothing left to do.

Question 2
Playing along with somebody's delusion: kindness or cruelty—and how would you tell them apart?

The book runs the same action five times, like an experiment: the innkeeper to save trouble, the priest to get the man home, Dorotea partly to solve her own problem, Sancho to avoid being caught in a lie, the Duke and Duchess for amusement. Identical action, five different alloys.

Take playing along at full strength first. The case for it is not cowardice, it is efficacy: arguing with a true believer generally makes the belief grip harder. Dorotea's princess got a dangerous man home safely without ropes, a fight, or the authorities. The standard practice today in dementia care—sometimes called therapeutic accommodation—runs along exactly this line: rather than correcting an error that cannot be corrected, you enter the person's world and lead them somewhere safe inside it. You can call that dishonest; the suffering it prevents is real.

Now the other side. Everything the Duke and Duchess do looks, from the outside, exactly like what Dorotea did: accommodation, staging, not one true word spoken—and they are more attentive, more generous, more thorough about it, with banquets, servants, a whole castle, an actual town. The difference is a single thing: they want the laugh, not the man's return. And that difference is invisible while it is happening—Sancho on Barataria is waited on like a real governor, right up until somebody starts removing each dish from his plate. Cervantes delivers an unsparing verdict in Part II, to the effect that people who go to such lengths to make fools of two fools are not far from being fools themselves.

So how do you tell? The book offers one criterion, and it is only usable afterwards: look at where the performance leads the person. Dorotea's led him home. The Duke's led him onto a stage, into the laughter of the household staff, and into the night when Sancho stood whipping a tree. Kind accommodation has a destination; cruel accommodation has only a programme of events.

Bring it home: the last time you did not correct somebody to their face, was it because correction would not have worked, or because it was more interesting to watch them go on? A friend's obviously unworkable business plan, a relative holding forth at dinner, a proposal everyone at work knows will die—when you nod, are you planning to get them home, or waiting for the next episode? The two are nearly indistinguishable in the moment, and there is only one crude test: whether you intend, at some point, to put on the armour and be the Knight of the White Moon yourself.

Question 3
Is it a basin or a helmet—who decides what a thing is?

Take "the room decides" at full strength first, because it is not relativism, it is how civilisation runs. Money is a piece of paper, a border is an imagined line, and whether a person is guilty is settled by twelve strangers raising their hands. These things are true precisely because enough people treat them as true—and that is not a defect, it is the only known way to get millions of strangers who will never meet to cooperate. If everyone redefined the objects of the world by private conviction, as Don Quixote does, all that would be left is hitting each other over the head. Consensus is not truth, but consensus is the precondition for getting anything done together.

Now the lone holdout, at full strength. Everyone at that inn knows it is a basin—they vote it a helmet to bait a madman. That is what makes the scene frightening: calling a false thing true never requires that everyone be deceived, only that nobody cares. Most historical unanimities are made of exactly this: not collective error but collective accommodation, collective reluctance to make trouble, collective unwillingness to be the one who spoils it. And every time such a thing has later been reversed, it was reversed by the person in the room who would not raise their hand—who at the time looked no different from Don Quixote. The problem is that standing in the room, you cannot tell whether the man refusing to yield is a prophet or a lunatic.

Bring it home: in daily life this is the proposal in the meeting that everyone privately doubts and unanimously approves; the agreed family version of an old story; the claim in the group chat you know is wrong but too expensive to dispute. The hard part is never judging right from wrong, it is pricing the act of saying so out loud. And the book supplies the other half: not one of the people raising their hands believed it was a helmet either.

The motif: what Cervantes kept writing

Lay his work out and the same structure recurs: a person taken captive by a language, who then either lives with dignity inside it or is destroyed by it.

His central personal experience was captivity: five years in Algiers, four escape attempts, a ransom. Part I contains a "captive's tale"—a Spanish soldier escaping Algiers with a Moorish woman—whose detail does not read like invention, and he wrote a play on the same material. And Don Quixote is also about a captivity, of a man taken by a cabinet of books; the only difference is that this prisoner walked into the cell himself.

The second obsession is the lucid madman. In the Exemplary Novels (1613), "The Glass Graduate" tells of a young man who, after being drugged, becomes convinced his whole body is glass and will shatter at a touch; he refuses stiff clothing, sleeps in straw, and becomes so wickedly quick-witted that the whole city follows him around to hear him talk. Then he is cured, takes his real name back, and tries to earn a living by his abilities—and nobody will listen to him any more. His interlude The Marvellous Puppet Show is harsher still: two swindlers unveil a tapestry that only people of pure blood can see, and the entire village duly reports seeing it—not one person dares to be the one who cannot. That is the same author having the same thought as at the inn.

The third is the world books promise against the world on the invoice. He wrote a pastoral romance himself, La Galatea (1585), and his last novel, The Trials of Persiles and Sigismunda (published posthumously in 1617), is an idealised adventure romance. He spent a lifetime both writing the addictive stuff and exposing it. In the book-burning chapter the priest pulls a few volumes out of the fire that he cannot bear to lose—one of them by Cervantes. He is never mocking people who love stories; he is mocking the habit of treating a story as an instruction manual.

Criticism, honestly

  • Part I is stuffed with irrelevant stories. "The Ill-Advised Curiosity" occupies three chapters with no bearing on the main line, and with Cardenio, the captive and the shepherds on top of it, nearly every fashionable genre of the day gets a turn. This is not a modern complaint—Cervantes has his own characters concede in the opening of Part II that readers objected, and announces that this volume will not wander. The defence is that the interpolations rhyme with the main line (all of them about people ruined by a script they believed), which holds, and does not stop them dragging.
  • The tragedy we read may not be the book he wrote. Seventeenth-century readers took it for a joke book; the illustrations are all beatings and tumbles. Reading Don Quixote as a noble failure and his madness as idealism is largely a coat of varnish applied by the German Romantics and later Russian and Spanish writers such as Unamuno; a line of twentieth-century scholars pushed back, arguing for a "hard" reading that admits it is first of all a funny book. The dispute itself makes the point: a large share of this book's meaning is not in the book, it is in four hundred years of readers.
  • The beatings are hard to take now. The comedy rests on an old man being knocked down, having his teeth broken, and being tossed in a blanket. The defence is that Cervantes is himself, across Part II, gradually itemising what the joke costs (Andrés, the Duke and Duchess); but a substantial stretch of Part I really is beating for laughs, and that does not wash out.
  • On women the evidence cuts both ways. Dulcinea never appears and never speaks; she is a pure projection, and lower-class women are mostly comic material. Yet the same book contains the shepherdess Marcela, who, when a crowd blames her for a suitor's death, delivers a speech whose substance is that being born beautiful creates no obligation to anybody and that she has chosen solitude and freedom—and what Don Quixote does is draw his sword to see her safely away and forbid anyone to follow. Dorotea, likewise, solves her own problems throughout. Any verdict on the book's sexual politics has to put both sets of evidence on the table.
  • The Ricote passage: facts only. In Part II Sancho meets his old neighbour Ricote, a Morisco—a descendant of Muslims converted to Christianity. From 1609 Spain decreed the expulsion of the Moriscos; Ricote's family was expelled and he has slipped back in disguise. Cervantes gives him considerable space and a moving statement of homesickness, and also has him call the expulsion just. Scholars have long divided over this: one camp reads self-protection and irony under censorship, another warns against excusing the author too readily. No verdict here—only the record that both speeches are in the book.
  • "The first modern novel" is a European framing. Lazarillo de Tormes (1554) precedes it; by this site's own list, Murasaki Shikibu's The Tale of Genji precedes it by six centuries. The more accurate claim may be that it is the first long novel to write the act of novel-making into the novel.
  • What translation costs. This is a book built out of proverbs, puns and parody, and every translation loses a layer—Sancho's rapid-fire proverb strings are a rhythm in Spanish and usually survive as mere meaning. English readers should also know that the versions differ enormously in temperature, from the seventeenth-century renderings onward; the book you think you know is partly the translator you happened to read.

Ten sentences to take away

1 · He did not go mad from reading too many stories; he went mad from treating a story as an operating manual.

2 · The first deception is one the innkeeper improvises to save trouble, the last is one his neighbour improvises to save him—and in the four hundred pages between, nobody tells him the truth.

3 · The technique of the windmill scene is to stand two pictures in front of the reader at once and force a choice.

4 · After being knocked down he never concedes, he promotes his opponent—so he can never lose, and can therefore be knocked down forever.

5 · The enchanter the priest invented to fob him off became his only instrument for understanding the world; the instrument was installed by the people deceiving him.

6 · Andrés's second whipping is the lesson this book teaches everybody: good intentions that produce no result are charged to somebody else's account.

7 · At the vote on the basin-helmet nobody believed it was a helmet—calling a false thing true never requires that everyone be deceived, only that nobody cares.

8 · Sancho governed for ten days, judged fairly and left clean; the post staged as a joke is the one place in the book where somebody does the job well.

9 · The protagonist dies under a name that is not the protagonist's: Don Quixote does not die, he is revoked.

10 · The man weeping at the end, begging him not to be sane, is the one who spent two volumes telling the truth—the faith did not disappear, it changed shoulders.
