Novels, Deep-Read · Cao Xueqin · No.4

Dream of the Red Chamber

also known as The Story of the Stone · Cao Xueqin · manuscripts circulated mid-1700s / first printed in 120 chapters, 1791

中文 →

The boat has moored at a post station and it is snowing. Jia Zheng is in the cabin writing home. He gets as far as his son's name, looks up, and sees a figure standing on the bank.
The man's head is shaved, his feet are bare, and he is wearing a great red felt cloak. He says nothing. He drops to the ground facing the boat and bows four times. Jia Zheng runs out; by then a monk has taken one of the man's arms and a Taoist the other, and the three of them are walking away into the snow, laughing about something. The whiteness closes. They are gone.
Nineteen years earlier that man was born with a piece of jade in his mouth. The whole family had been delighted about it ever since.

This page contains complete spoilers — the register in chapter five that gives away every character's fate, how Daiyu dies, who Baoyu actually marries, how the family estate is confiscated, and those four bows in the snow. This novel never intended to play the suspense game with you; all of its effort goes into making you read on after you already know the ending. If you want the suspense, read the book first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

A stone rejected by the goddess who repaired the sky — good enough, but not needed — is born as the son of an immensely rich household, and spends a few years in a garden built to receive his imperial-concubine sister, among girls and maids, in a life that never requires him to grow up; he owes one of those girls a debt contracted in a previous existence, a debt to be repaid in tears; his family eventually tricks him into marrying someone else, and on that night the girl burns her poems and dies; then the imperial favour is withdrawn, the estate is confiscated, the women die or marry out or take vows, and he — having sat one last examination — kneels four times to his father in the snow and walks away.

Where it sits

Cao Xueqin (c. 1715–1763; his exact dates are still disputed) came from a family of hereditary imperial bondservants. His grandfather Cao Yin held the post of Imperial Textile Commissioner at Jiangning — not an ordinary provincial office but a position that ran silk production for the throne and served as the emperor's eyes in the south. Of the Kangxi emperor's six southern tours, four were hosted by the Cao family. In 1728, under the Yongzheng emperor, the family's property was confiscated and they moved back to Beijing. Cao Xueqin's later poverty was recorded by a friend in a poem: the household lived on rice gruel and bought wine on credit. He wrote this book in the western suburbs of the capital, and the first chapter says he worked over it for ten years and revised it five times. He died with it unfinished.

Its place in the history of the novel is worth stating precisely. Before it, the Chinese long novel was about events — the Three Kingdoms romance is about statecraft and war, Water Margin about outlawry, Journey to the West about a pilgrimage. Dream of the Red Chamber is the first long novel to make ordinary daily life the subject itself: a meal, a birthday, a poetry club, one sentence spoken a little too sharply — these are the plot. It learned that from The Plum in the Golden Vase (the commentator who signs himself Red Inkstone notes the debt explicitly), but it swapped out that book's engine. Instead of appetite destroying people, it shows people who know perfectly well that the good years cannot be kept, living them anyway.

The textual situation matters, because it determines which book you are reading. In Cao Xueqin's lifetime the novel circulated only in manuscript, under the title The Story of the Stone, carrying commentary signed by Red Inkstone and others, and it stopped at chapter eighty. In 1791 Cheng Weiyuan and Gao E printed a complete 120-chapter edition with wooden movable type. Where the last forty chapters came from, and how much of Cao Xueqin's own drafts survives in them, has been argued about for more than two centuries — an argument that is itself part of the book.

The story

Act one · A stone nobody used, and a debt to be paid in tears

It does not start on earth. The goddess Nüwa smelted 36,501 stones to patch a hole in the sky and used 36,500. The last one was left at the foot of a mountain. Its only problem is that it qualified and was not needed. It laments itself day and night until a monk and a Taoist pass by, and it begs to be taken down into the human world for a taste of the excitement. The monk shrinks it to the size of a fan-pendant, a bright translucent jade, and pockets it.

There is an older account to settle. On the bank of a river in the western sky grew a Crimson Pearl Flower, dying, until a being called the Divine Attendant began watering it daily with sweet dew. It lived, and took female form. When he later conceived a desire to be born as a man, she followed, saying: he watered me for a lifetime, and I have no water to give back — so I will repay him with every tear of my life. Seed ①

Then the camera drops to earth, onto a modest country gentleman named Zhen Shiyin — the passage readers skip, and the one that matters most. He is comfortably off and has a three-year-old daughter he adores. On the night of the lantern festival a servant carries her out to see the lights, and she is gone. The parents fall ill. A temple next door catches fire and burns the whole street to bare ground. His land is hit by famine and then by bandits, so he goes to live with his father-in-law, who cheats him out of the money he has left and tells the neighbours he is lazy. A respectable man is stripped to nothing inside a few pages. At the end a lame Taoist comes by singing a song whose refrain is that everyone knows immortality is good, but nobody can let go of office, or silver, or a beautiful wife, or children. Zhen Shiyin composes a gloss on the spot, turning the song into a string of pictures: the hall that yesterday was crowded with court tablets is empty today; the ground where there was music is under weeds. Then he walks off with the Taoist. Seed ②

Act two · A child enters the house, and a register settles everyone's fate

The Rongguo and Ningguo houses are a matched pair, two dukedoms from one line, founded on military service. The Rongguo title sits with the elder brother Jia She, but the household is effectively run by the second brother, Jia Zheng — because he has a daughter in the palace, and because the matriarch, Grandmother Jia, prefers him. The real seat of power is the old lady's room: she manages nothing, and nothing happens without her nod.

Our first way in is an outsider's eyes: Lin Daiyu. Her mother is dead and her father sends her to her grandmother. All the way in she reminds herself to watch every step and say nothing wrong. She and the old lady weep in each other's arms. Then Jia Baoyu comes in, looks at her, and says something strange: I have seen this cousin before. They have — in another life. That evening, told that Daiyu has no jade of her own, he tears his off his neck and hurls it down, shouting that a thing that cannot tell one person from another is no rare treasure and no spirit either. The room dissolves into chaos. Every relationship in the novel is set that night: the jade is the family's most precious object, and the first thing he wants to do on meeting Daiyu is destroy it.

On another thread, the scholar Jia Yucun — once helped financially by Zhen Shiyin — has passed the examinations, been impeached out of office, and got himself reinstated through the Jia family's connections. The first case on his desk is a killing: the Xue family's brutal heir, Xue Pan, beat a man to death over the purchase of a girl. A clerk slips him a memorandum listing the four great clans of the province — Jia, Shi, Wang and Xue — all intermarried, none of them touchable. Yucun disposes of the case and lets Xue Pan go. The girl they fought over is the child lost in act one; she will be called Xiangling from now on, and she is the most comprehensively destroyed person in the book. Yucun recognises her, and knows whose daughter she is, and says nothing.

The Xue family moves into the Rongguo house. The daughter, Xue Baochai, wears a gold locket. One day she asks to see Baoyu's jade, which is inscribed never lose me, never forget me, and your years will be long; her maid points out that this makes a matched couplet with the locket, which reads never leave, never abandon, and your bloom will last. Seed ③ That is the gold-and-jade match: a marriage certified in advance by two objects. Facing it stands the stone-and-flower bond from before either of them was born. The novel's central romance is a war between a marriage with documentation and a promise with none.

But before any of that, chapter five has already handed over the ending. Dozing in Qin Keqing's room, Baoyu dreams his way into the Land of Illusion, past a couplet at the gate saying that when the false is taken for true, the true becomes false as well. A fairy has him leaf through a register of paintings and verses about the girls around him: a jade belt hanging in a wood, a gold hairpin buried in snow, a bow with a citron hung on it, a woman weeping on a ship at sea. He cannot make sense of any of it. He is then made to listen to twelve songs, the last of which describes birds scattering to their separate woods and leaving the ground white and bare. He nods off. He remembers nothing. Seed ④

Next door at the Ningguo house, Jia Zhen's daughter-in-law Qin Keqing abruptly sickens and dies. Before she goes she appears in a dream to Wang Xifeng, the young wife who actually runs the household, and says something entirely clear-eyed: what looks now like oil poured on a fire is temporary, and when the tree falls the monkeys scatter; while there is still money, buy farmland near the ancestral graves and pay for the family school and the sacrifices out of that, because grave-estates were not subject to confiscation, so that if the family is ever convicted the children will still have food and schooling. Seed ⑤ Xifeng agrees in the dream, wakes to the death-bell and a cold sweat — and does not do it. She is busy making the funeral magnificent: the coffin is timber originally set aside for a prince, and a rank is purchased for the dead woman's husband so the procession will look better.

Then comes the summit of the book. Jia Zheng's eldest daughter, Yuanchun, is made an imperial consort, and the emperor permits her a visit home. To have her for half a day the family tears down the old garden, buys a troupe of child actors, hangs the lanterns, and builds the Grand View Garden. On the night itself everything blazes, and Yuanchun weeps in her sedan chair, and tells her grandmother and mother, from behind a curtain, that they have sent her to a place where she cannot be seen and her family cannot come. She must be back before dawn. The most splendid celebration in the novel is conducted, from beginning to end, in tears. The silver runs out like seawater, and the accounts acquire a hole that will never be filled.

Act three · The garden: a licensed adolescence

After the visit the garden stands empty, and Yuanchun sends word that it should not be wasted — let the girls and Baoyu move in. This is the strangest and most valuable arrangement in the book: a small territory, authorised by imperial power, where adult rules are temporarily suspended. Nobody inside has to earn, entertain or marry. They write poems, eat crab, argue. Cao Xueqin spends nearly forty chapters on this stretch of good years that cannot lead anywhere.

Baoyu and Daiyu read a forbidden romantic play together by the weir; he teases her with a line from it, casting himself as the lovesick scholar and her as the beauty, and she turns on him with red eyes. In that era, two teenagers reading a banned play together is the entire elopement available to them. On the day the garden holds its farewell to the flower spirits, Daiyu goes out alone with a hoe to bury fallen blossom, saying aloud as she works that the flowers at least have someone to bury them — and who will bury her? Baoyu, on the far side of the slope, hears it and goes down as if struck.

Things are going wrong all along, and nobody counts them. Baoyu jokes with one of his mother's maids, Jinchuan; Lady Wang slaps the girl and turns her out, and days later Jinchuan drowns herself in a well. Then a prince's household sends an officer to say that an actor under their protection has run off and has been seen with Baoyu. Then Baoyu's half-brother tells their father that Jinchuan died because Baoyu forced himself on her. Jia Zheng holds his son down and beats him with a rod until the servants cannot stop him, and it ends only when Grandmother Jia arrives and says: kill me first.

Then comes Granny Liu. This old countrywoman turns up the first time to beg a connection, and Xifeng casually gives her twenty taels of silver. The second time she brings vegetables to say thank you, and the old lady keeps her for a tour of the garden, where Xifeng and the head maid set her up as a joke; she stands at the table and announces in rhyme that old Liu eats like an ox, and the room laughs until food comes out of people's noses. What matters is that she knows exactly what is happening. She knows they are laughing at her and she plays along, because this trip will send her home with enough to feed her grandson. Cao Xueqin gets the full glitter of a rich house out of those two days, and keeps one genuinely poor person clear-eyed throughout.

The garden is not all play. After Xifeng miscarries, three young women take over the accounts, and Tanchun proves to be the only person of her generation with a political mind: she throws out an irregular payment claimed by her own relatives, then contracts out the garden's flowers, bamboo and orchards to the older servants who know the trade, on a profit-and-loss basis with a fixed annual payment in. The books improve immediately. She also knows this is nothing against the hole in them.

Act four · The search: the garden splits from inside

The cracks show first among the servants. Yuanyang, Grandmother Jia's chief maid, is wanted as a concubine by the elder master Jia She; she cuts off her hair in front of a roomful of people and swears she will never marry. It is the hardest refusal in the book, and it works only because the old lady is still alive. Two women die at the Ningguo house in the same stretch: Jia Lian secretly takes a second wife, You Erjie, and when Xifeng finds out she neither shouts nor weeps — she smilingly moves the woman into the compound and then arranges everything, a lawsuit, servants primed to be cruel, an incompetent doctor for the pregnancy, until You Erjie swallows gold and dies. Her sister, refused by the man she wants because he suspects anyone from that house of being unclean, draws the sword they had exchanged as a pledge and puts it to her own throat. Respectable families, it turns out, kill without spilling anything.

Then the small object. A simple-minded maid picks up an embroidered pornographic sachet in the garden, and it makes its way to Lady Wang. Her fear is entirely rational for the period: unmarried aristocratic girls live in that garden, and if a rumour of impropriety escapes, every one of their marriages is finished. Hence the night search of the Grand View Garden, courtyard by courtyard, box by box. In Tanchun's rooms she has her maids' boxes opened and set out herself, saying that if they are searching they will search hers first; when a senior servant, presuming on her patron, lifts the edge of Tanchun's robe, Tanchun slaps her. And then delivers the hardest judgement in the novel:

"A house like this cannot be killed from outside. It has to start killing itself from within — only then can it be brought down for good."— Tanchun, chapter 74 (my condensed rendering of the original)

The results: Siqi is expelled over a love affair with a cousin; Ruhua is expelled merely for keeping a few old things for her soldier brother, and the youngest daughter of the house refuses to protect her and insists she be sent away; and Qingwen — the maid with the best hands and the sharpest tongue, who once darned a peacock-feather cloak through a night of fever — has nothing found on her at all, and is turned out because she is too good-looking and too unwilling to grovel. She is dumped in her brother's hovel and dies within days, biting off her fingernails to give to Baoyu and trading him the shirt off her back. After that the losses come one after another: Yingchun is married off by her father like a debt payment to an officer who beats her, comes home once to weep, and does not come out alive. Note the design: Cao Xueqin brings in no external enemy. The garden is searched, emptied and torn open entirely from within.

Act five · White ground, swept clean

The last forty chapters are of contested authorship (see the criticism section), but their direction matches most of what the register in chapter five promised. First the support gives way: Yuanchun sickens and dies, and the family loses the rope holding it in the air. Baoyu then loses his jade and turns simple, smiling at everything. The household panics: a wedding might revive him, and in his condition only someone as steady as Baochai can carry it. Hence the substitution: he is told he is marrying Daiyu, the sedan chair arrives, and the veil comes off Baochai. Payoff ③ ↑ The gold-and-jade match is finally consummated by a trick — those two auspicious inscriptions look like destiny, but cashing them in required the whole family to conspire and required doing it while one party was out of his mind. Fate, when it reaches the ground, always needs somebody's hands.

The same night, in her own courtyard, Daiyu has run out of both illness and words. She has the brazier brought over and feeds it, one by one, the poems of her life and the old handkerchiefs Baoyu once sent her. Outside, the wedding music. Her last words are half a sentence:

"Baoyu, Baoyu, how could you —"— Lin Daiyu's death, chapter 98 (my rendering; the original breaks off unfinished)

The rest never arrives. The debt is discharged here: every tear of her life, given back to that one person. He kept her alive with dew in a previous existence; in this one she has emptied herself out crying. Payoff ① ↑ What makes it merciless is that the repayment is not a punishment but an agreement — from chapter one she was here to weep exactly this, and the reader has known all along.

Then the confiscation. Imperial guards search the Ningguo house; Jia She and Jia Zhen are convicted, the property goes to the state, and years of usury, seized farmland and bought verdicts all surface together. Grandmother Jia distributes her lifetime's private savings among her children and grandchildren and dies soon after. Wang Xifeng, running a funeral with no money left and nobody obeying her, dies in a cold room. Her verse in the register says that she outsmarted herself, and calculated her own life away — which is exactly this moment. Her cleverness was real, and every move was sound at the time, but every move was about the move in front of her. Qin Keqing's dream had offered her the one available exit, and she was busy making a funeral look impressive. Payoff ⑤ ↑

The rest scatter into the pages of that register. Tanchun is married away to the coast, going like a kite with a cut string — precisely the bow and paper kite painted beside her verse. Xichun cuts her hair and takes vows. The nun Miaoyu is carried off by bandits and never accounted for. Xifeng's small daughter Qiaojie is nearly sold as a concubine by her own uncle and cousins, and is saved because that same country grandmother hides her in a village — her verse had said it in advance: because she once helped old Liu, the child met her rescuer. Twenty taels handed over casually at the height of the family's wealth turn out to be the only return it ever collects. And Baochai, having married in, becomes the second half of that painted riddle: the gold hairpin buried in snow. She won the marriage and what she won was an empty house. Payoff ④ ↑

Last, Baoyu. He suddenly takes to his books, sits the provincial examination, places seventh — and never comes home from the hall. Until that snowy day, when his father looks up from the boat and sees him on the bank, shaved and barefoot in a red cloak, bowing four times before a monk and a Taoist walk him off into the snow. He settled the family's account with a degree, and left. The stone returns to the foot of its mountain with the whole journey carved into it, waiting for the next passer-by to copy it down. Payoff ② ↑ — and the man in chapter one who lost his daughter, lost his house to fire, was cheated by his father-in-law and finally walked away with a Taoist turns out to have been the whole 120 chapters in miniature. How a household comes apart, how a man leaves: Cao Xueqin performs it in five pages at the start, and then performs it again, slowly, over several hundred thousand words. The name Zhen Shiyin is itself the instruction — it puns on true events concealed, just as Jia Yucun puns on invented words preserved.

Put the five acts together and the fuse is not an event but a structure. Every penny of this family's income depends on the emperor still remembering it, and all of its dignity depends on continuous spending — and both fall due at the same moment. The military honours were eaten generations ago, the rents shrink every year, and the only real revenue left is a daughter in the palace; to have her home for half a day the family builds a garden and empties the reserves; inside the garden grows an adolescence nobody can keep; and when the daughter dies and the favour is withdrawn, every old account is opened at once. Nobody destroys the Jia family. The house has been paying for today with tomorrow's money the entire time, and its tomorrow was one woman's life.

Three slow-motion replays

1 · Burying flowers: let the heaviest line be overheard

Technically this scene turns on one decision: Cao Xueqin does not have Daiyu say any of it to Baoyu. He has Baoyu hear it from behind a slope. Said to his face, the speech becomes a confession, or a bid for sympathy; the listener has to respond, and the temperature drops back to two adolescents bickering. Spoken alone, believing nobody is there, the same words are not addressed to anyone — they are simply what this person is like in private. Overhearing converts it from a declaration into a fact.

The date is designed too. It is the day the garden performs its farewell to the flower spirits, because after it spring is over: she is not burying flowers in general, she is burying a season that has been officially declared finished. And the reason she is so wretched that particular morning is almost comically small — the night before, a maid at Baoyu's gate failed to recognise her voice and did not open, and she decided he had ordered it. Almost every disaster in this book starts that way: no villain, just something misheard once. Baoyu's reaction is not tenderness either; he is knocked flat, because he has followed her thought one step further — she will be gone, I will be gone, this garden and these flowers and these people will all be gone. It is a teenager running into time for the first time.

2 · The beating: one rod exposes the whole household's power map

The event is simple; the craft is in the two hours afterwards, where every member of the family reacts in turn and the reactions draw the org chart. Lady Wang weeps — but she weeps for her dead elder son, which is a way of reminding her husband in public that this is the only one he has left. That works better than pleading. Grandmother Jia arrives last and hardest, ends everything with "kill me first," because filial obligation is the one force in this house that outranks paternal authority. Jia Zheng had already stopped being able to swing; what actually frightens him is not injuring his son but the fact that this family has nothing to hope for except this boy.

Then three women react, and the three reactions are three character sheets. Baochai brings medicine, blushes, begins a sentence about how none of this would have happened if he had listened — and stops herself halfway, because she knows exactly which clause would cross a line. Xiren goes straight to the mistress of the house to suggest that the young people are all grown now and Baoyu should move out of the garden, and earns her own position by it. Daiyu simply arrives with her eyes swollen shut and finally manages one useless, entirely honest sentence: promise me you'll change. After this passage the author never needs to characterise anyone again.

3 · The search: a piece of evidence that is never resolved

The night is built by repeating the act of searching from courtyard to courtyard, so that the repetition itself accumulates weight, and then stopping dead in Tanchun's rooms: boxes open, speech finished, one slap. What is easy to miss is that the whole operation began with a sachet whose owner is never identified. The largest internal purge in the novel is conducted around a piece of evidence that never reaches a conclusion — not a loose end the author forgot, but the point: what a purge actually needs is never proof, only a reason to begin.

What this book runs on

This is not a novel of invented systems, but it does run on a few structural devices used with unusual nerve, and they are worth taking apart.

Device one · Turning the ending into a consultable archive

The register in chapter five is the boldest wager in the history of the Chinese novel: one-seventh of the way in, the author publishes the fate of every one of his twelve principal women. Conventional novels pull you along with "what happens next"; this one throws that engine away and buys something else with the proceeds — it converts "what will happen" into "how could this have happened." From then on the reader watches from something like a god's seat: you know Tanchun will be married away to the far coast, so every effort she makes to save the household hurts; you know Xifeng dies of her own calculation, so every brilliant manoeuvre goes on a ledger in your head. Foreknowledge does not weaken the tension; it adds mass to ordinary scenes, so that a family dinner acquires a countdown. The cost is equally plain: the first eighty chapters have almost no forward pressure, and many readers stall there. The book asks for a different kind of attention — not what happens, but how people live in front of an outcome they cannot change.

Device two · The two-layer structure of true and false

The novel hands over its own key in chapter one. Zhen Shiyin and Jia Yucun are transparent puns — roughly, true events concealed and invented words preserved. The couplet at the gate of the Land of Illusion makes true and false reversible surfaces. And there really is a second Baoyu, living in Nanjing, identical in face and temperament — except that he later comes to his senses and starts talking like a careerist. That is what Jia Baoyu would have become had he agreed to grow up, and the author builds the possibility into a separate human being and stands him next to the original.

Device three · The garden as a laboratory with an expiry date

The garden exists on the strength of one imperial instruction, and its function is to lift a group of people out of time for a while — no earning, no marrying, no outsiders. What Cao Xueqin wants to demonstrate can only be demonstrated under those conditions: first let us see what these people are like when life is not pressing on them, then let life back in. Which is why the night search does so much damage. It does not break a few boxes; it announces that the experiment is over.

What it forces you to think about

Question 1
Someone urging you to make something of yourself: are they loving you, or managing you?

Concretely: Baoyu is fifteen or sixteen, the family's entire hope rests on him, and he spends his days in the garden writing verse and mixing rouge for the maids. Baochai suggests he meet some serious people and take an interest in the business of an official career. Jia Zheng is blunter, with a rod. Baoyu's reaction to both is the same — revulsion. Daiyu never urges him to do anything, and the novel points this out specifically; it is exactly why he believes she is the only one who understands him.

Take the side that urges, at full strength. Nobody in this family produces anything: the rents fall every year, the spending does not, and the only reliable income is a daughter's position in the palace. Staking a clan's survival on whether one woman remains in favour is not conservatism, it is gambling. Baochai is one of the very few young people in the house who has actually looked at the accounts and knows where the money comes from. Her urging is not conformism; it is a clear-eyed person reminding another that somebody else is paying for his freedom. There is something real in the beating, too — what Jia Zheng is hitting is not his son's temperament but his own fear of what comes next. Events prove them right: after the confiscation, none of the poems written in that garden buys a peck of rice.

Now the side that does not urge, at full strength. Baoyu is not lazy; he has seen the finished product of that road. Jia Yucun is the complete specimen of making something of yourself — study, degree, office, impeachment, reinstatement — and the price was standing silent while his benefactor's daughter went to a slave trader and a thug. What Baoyu refuses is not effort but the process that turns people into administrative consumables. And Daiyu's refusal to nag is not indulgence; she is the only person in that house who treats him as a person rather than as an asset.

The dilemma is unchanged today, only the vocabulary moved: get the job stable first, then talk about what you want; don't you think it's time you had a plan. Usually true, usually sincerely meant. The difficulty is that care and management look nearly identical from outside, and the person speaking often cannot tell them apart either. Cao Xueqin passes no verdict, but he does leave a clue: he lets Baochai be impeccable and right about everything, and leaves her at the end sitting in an empty house.

Question 2
To protect a community, may you sacrifice the member of it with the least protection?

The search begins with one pornographic sachet. Ridiculous today; in context, Lady Wang's fear is real and lethal. Unmarried aristocratic daughters live in that garden, and if word gets out that there is sexual conduct inside it, every one of those marriages collapses and the family's standing in the capital goes with them. She is not facing moral squeamishness, she is facing a concrete risk that can wreck several dozen lives. Substitute any modern institution: a credible hint of scandal, immediate full investigation — that is the standard move, and it is not a stupid one.

Then look at where the cost lands. Nothing whatsoever is found on Qingwen; she is expelled for being good-looking, quick-tongued and unwilling to ingratiate herself. Ruhua was keeping a few old possessions for a brother in the army. The investigation removes only people with no protection, while the men who are actually hollowing out the household — Jia She, Jia Zhen, Jia Lian — lose not one hair. That is precisely Tanchun's line about a house killing itself from within: purges never travel upward.

The trap is that you cannot simply say Lady Wang was wrong. If the rumour had escaped, the cost really would have been several dozen girls' futures. This is the hardest shape a problem can take: on one side a calculable collective risk, on the other incalculable individual lives; the first comes with numbers, the second only with names, and most of the time the numbers win, and win with a clear conscience. Anyone who has worked inside an organisation has watched it: after something goes wrong, the first person handled is rarely the one most responsible — it is the most visible, least connected, most easily processed one. Qingwen's tragedy is not that she was falsely accused. It is that she was the cheapest available option from the start.

Question 3
When your family deceives you "for your own good," how is that account settled?

The marriage substitution is the easiest thing in the book to condemn: a rigged wedding that kills someone. But put back into its situation, the defence is thicker than you expect. Baoyu has lost his wits along with the jade, and the household genuinely believes a wedding may restore him. The consort is dead and the finances are collapsing; the Xue family's money is real support. Daiyu is gravely ill, both parents dead, without property or protector — married to Baoyu, in her condition, she might not have lasted a year regardless. Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang are not villains. They picked what looked like the least bad option out of a set of bad ones, and sincerely believed they were saving lives.

The prosecution stands too, and weighs more. What they falsified was two people's entire lives, and they executed it at the moment one of those people had lost the capacity to judge. The lethal property of "for your own good" is that it does not require consent — it has already assumed the other party is incapable of giving it. Worse is the aftermath: Daiyu dies, Baoyu comes back to himself, and nobody apologises. The matter is filed as done.

Bring it forward: choosing a treatment for an ailing parent, choosing a degree for a child, withholding a diagnosis from someone who is dying. Every one of these has an ample supply of good reasons, and every one is a decision made on someone else's behalf that was theirs to make. The only reliable line may be whether you seriously modelled "what would they choose" or only "what is best for them." The second is always easier.

Question 4
Baoyu walking away: enlightenment, or desertion?

Enlightenment, at full strength: he has seen the thing everyone else is pretending not to see, which is that all of it goes. The women are dead or dispersed, the estate is confiscated, and the values he was raised on — cleanliness, sincerity, refusal to be worldly — have no address in this world. He passes the examination, which settles the family's claim on him, and then takes nothing with him. In a world where everyone is gripping, he is the only one who genuinely lets go.

Desertion, at full strength: he leaves with Baochai pregnant, his mother newly bereaved of a daughter, the household just stripped. Of all the women who wept for him or died near him, he saved none. He stood there and did nothing on the day Qingwen was thrown out; he ran before Jinchuan went into the well. Almost all of his devotion is posthumous: helpless while they are being destroyed, then poems, offerings and weeping afterwards. Those four bows in the snow are beautiful, but they are a ceremony he is holding for himself.

The least forgiving formulation: renunciation is an option for him and not for them. Not for Daiyu, not for Qingwen, not for Yingchun, not for Xiangling. An aristocratic man gets to end his own story by seeing through the world; every woman in this book is ended by her circumstances instead. Which brings it home: making an aesthetic object out of suffering is a very deep temptation, and seeing through and running away look identical from outside — the difference is who has to stay and clean up after you go.

The obsession Cao Xueqin kept returning to

A special case has to be admitted first: Cao Xueqin left only this one book. There is no other novel to compare it against. So the recurring material has to be found in two places — what he returns to inside this book, and which books he is arguing with.

Three things recur. The first is time: nearly every scene is written as something already passing — the flowers are described falling while they bloom, the party is described dispersing while it eats, and the most magnificent night in the novel is conducted in tears. The second is an inversion of value: Baoyu's childish assertion that girls are made of water and men of mud is executed in earnest by the book, which gives its most dignified portraits not to masters and young gentlemen but to maids and one country grandmother. In an era when women's given names routinely went unrecorded, devoting a novel's finest writing to a group of young women with no social standing is by itself a position. The third is the self-image of being surplus: the stone's problem in chapter one is not that it was inadequate but that it was not needed, and Cao Xueqin was himself the son of a house the age had voided.

Which books he is arguing with is also clear. The Plum in the Golden Vase taught him how to make a novel out of a meal, a funeral, a loan; but that book is about how appetite ruins people, and this one is about how the unspoilt things go as well. On the other side stand the great romantic dramas — The Story of the Western Wing and The Peony Pavilion. The scene of the two cousins reading a banned play is his declaration of lineage: those plays gave him the idea that feeling can stand against propriety, and what he did with them was cancel their endings. In the play, the young scholar comes first in the examinations and marries the girl. Here, Baoyu passes his examination — and walks away.

Criticism and controversy

This book is praised so reflexively that several genuine problems rarely get an honest hearing. Plainly:

  • The provenance and quality of the last forty chapters is a two-century argument. When Cheng Weiyuan and Gao E printed the 120-chapter edition in 1791, Cheng claimed the final forty came from drafts he had collected piecemeal. Hu Shi and others argued Gao E wrote them; most scholars today lean toward Gao E as editor rather than sole author, and the real source remains unsettled. The quality objections cluster: the ending restores the family somewhat — Jia Zheng regains a title, a grandson passes his examinations, Baochai is pregnant — which sits badly against the chapter-five song promising bare white ground, swept clean; and having Baoyu sit an examination before renouncing the world does not follow from the character in the first eighty chapters. The defence deserves stating too: Daiyu burning her poems, Grandmother Jia's death and Baoyu's departure are very well written, and most readers' final impression of the novel is formed by exactly those scenes.
  • "Redology" sometimes overwhelms the novel. One school reads the book as a cipher for early Qing court politics or the fall of the Ming; another turned to the author's family history and to textual scholarship. Both produced important work, and both produced a great deal of reading that treats the novel as evidence and its characters as riddles.
  • Character judgements are still split. The pro-Daiyu and pro-Baochai camps have been arguing since the eighteenth century over whether Baochai is a calculating hypocrite or an intelligent woman trained too well by the rules; opinion on Xiren is similarly polarised. Mostly this indicates that the characters are complex enough — and it is also routinely flattened into a declaration about the reader.
  • Two faces of its writing about women. The book grants women interiority and grants servant girls dignity to an unprecedented degree, and that is what it is most admired for. The critique is that the whole thing rests on an aristocratic boy's gaze; "girls are made of water" is praise and also idealisation, relocating real women into another position defined by a man. And a great many female deaths are rendered beautifully, which tends to soften the question of what produced them.
  • A gap in class perspective. The garden's lyricism is underwritten by enormous quantities of off-page labour: kitchens, needlework, procurement, gatekeeping. The one occasion when someone from the bottom speaks directly is an old Ningguo servant cursing the family while drunk — and he is silenced with a mouthful of dung. The placement is clear-eyed; it also goes no further.
  • It was banned. In the later Qing the novel appeared on official lists of fiction to be suppressed in some jurisdictions (for example a Jiangsu proscription list of the Tongzhi period), on grounds of obscenity. That is a documented record, stated here as fact.
  • The translation gap is real. English has two systems: David Hawkes and John Minford's The Story of the Stone, which renders many servant names as English words (one maid becomes "Aroma") and reads superbly at the cost of considerable domestication; and Yang Xianyi and Gladys Yang's A Dream of Red Mansions, which stays closer to the original. Strictly speaking, Chinese and English readers are not discussing quite the same book — an unusual amount of this novel's work is done in forms of address, puns and verse, so the losses in translation run higher than for most fiction.

Ten lines that stay

1 · One-seventh of the way in it publishes everyone's ending, and then uses your foreknowledge to add mass to ordinary scenes: a family dinner acquires a countdown.

2 · The five pages about Zhen Shiyin in chapter one are the whole 120 chapters in miniature — lost child, fire, grasping relatives, walking away with a monk. Performed once, then performed again slowly.

3 · The stone-and-flower bond against the gold-and-jade match is a promise with no documentation against a certificate engraved on objects. The certificate wins — but only because the whole family conspires against a man who has lost his mind.

4 · The garden is not a garden. It is a room authorised by decree and revocable at any time. The night search does not discover anything; it announces that the experiment is over.

5 · Tanchun's line about a house that has to start killing itself from within is the novel's diagnosis: nobody knocks the Jia family down; it is torn open from inside, layer by layer.

6 · Every one of Xifeng's calculations is sound, and every one of them stops at the move in front of her. She was offered the single available exit in a dream, and was busy making a funeral look impressive.

7 · The purge removes only the unprotected, and the men who emptied the house lose nothing. This is not a peculiarity of the eighteenth century.

8 · The danger of "for your own good" is not impure motive. It is that consent is not required, because incapacity has already been assumed.

9 · Renunciation is an option for Baoyu and for none of the women. Seeing through and running away look the same from outside; the difference is who stays to clean up.

10 · What stays is not the great house. It is a shaved head and bare feet bowing four times in the snow — and the author's own aside in chapter one, that everyone calls him a fool, and asks who will taste what is really in it.
