Novels, Deep-Read · Wang Anyi · No.95

The Song of Everlasting Sorrow

Wang Anyi · 1995 · Three parts

中文 →

Shanghai, the 1940s, a film studio. A girl from a girls' middle school has tagged along with a classmate to watch, and what she sees is a bedroom with only three walls: a bed, an actress lying across it playing a corpse, and a bare electric lamp hanging over her.
The light is very bright. People walk back and forth, move furniture, shout cues. Nobody pays the slightest attention to the woman who is supposed to be dead.
The girl stands in the dark beyond the set for a long time, and can't say why it has caught at her. Her name is Wang Qiyao. Forty years later, under another lamp, she will remember this moment.

This piece contains complete spoilers: which man Wang Qiyao chooses, where the box of gold bars comes from and whom it eventually draws in, whose child she carries and who is made to take the blame, who dies in 1966, and on which night in 1986, at whose hands, she dies herself. The novel slips its ending into a picture in Part One. Without the ending there is no explaining the lamp.

In one sentence

A girl raised in the Shanghai lanes is crowned runner-up in the Miss Shanghai pageant, becomes the kept mistress of a powerful official, and, when he dies, carries the box of gold he left her through forty quiet years in the lanes of a new regime, keeping her old refinements and taking one man after another who can't or won't marry her. In the end the gold and the refinement together draw a greedy young man into her room, and she dies with his hands around her throat.

Coordinates

Wang Anyi was born in Nanjing in 1954 and moved to Shanghai as an infant with her mother, the writer Ru Zhijuan; she grew up in the lanes. At sixteen she was sent down to the Anhui countryside, and began publishing fiction after returning to the city in the late 1970s. She made her name in the 1980s with Baotown and a trio of novellas about desire known as the "Love" trilogy. The Song of Everlasting Sorrow was serialized in the journal Zhongshan in 1995 and won the Mao Dun Literature Prize, China's top award for the novel, in 2000. The English translation by Michael Berry and Susan Chan Egan appeared in 2008.

Its place on the map: it is the most thorough novel about Shanghai in contemporary Chinese, and although its nominal heroine is a woman, its real protagonist is a city. The title is borrowed from Bai Juyi's Tang-dynasty ballad about Emperor Xuanzong and his doomed consort Yang Guifei, a poem that ends on a sorrow that will never run out. Wang Anyi takes not the imperial romance but the long, unhealing grief. Stanley Kwan filmed it in 2005 as Everlasting Regret, with substantial changes; what follows is the novel only.

The story

Part One · Lanes, gossip, a film set: the making of a type

The novel doesn't begin with a person. For its first several chapters Wang Anyi is a camera slowly descending onto the city. First the lanes, the longtang: stone-gated terraces, newer alleyways, shanty rows, spreading block after block like the city's skin. Then the gossip that runs through them, slipping out of back doors and attic rooms and between bamboo laundry poles, petty and damp and the city's real breath. Then the inner chambers where the daughters of the lanes keep their thoughts. Last of all, the pigeons: Setup ①the flocks that rise from the rooftops every dawn, the only eyes that see the whole city from above, that see into every window and never say a word.

Only then does Wang Qiyao appear, and she appears as a type. Every lane in Shanghai has a Wang Qiyao: the most ordinary and the most proper sort of Shanghai daughter, who knows how to dress, knows her limits, and knows how to make a life elegant out of very little. Her family is ordinary. She attends a girls' school, and her best friend, Wu Peizhen, has a connection at a film studio and takes her there for fun. That is where she sees the bed and the lamp. Setup ②The studio people like her looks and give her a screen test, but in front of the camera she freezes, and nothing comes of it.

The test fails, but her picture ends up with Mr. Cheng, a quiet, gentlemanly photographer with a studio of his own. He shoots a series of portraits of her, and one of them runs in a magazine called Shanghai Life, captioned as a proper young lady of Shanghai. Overnight Wang Qiyao is famous in her lane. Another classmate, Jiang Lili, is drawn to that fame. The Jiangs are rich, Lili is impulsive, and she brings Wang Qiyao home to live with her, almost as if she were enshrining a dream. Jiang Lili is secretly in love with Mr. Cheng. Mr. Cheng has eyes only for Wang Qiyao.

In 1946 Shanghai holds a Miss Shanghai pageant, and Jiang Lili and Mr. Cheng push Wang Qiyao into it together. She doesn't win. She places third, and from then on everyone calls her "Third Miss." The narrator pauses here for a cool little reflection, roughly this: third place is exactly right, not the most dazzling, but the one best suited to everyday life. That is Wang Qiyao to the bone. She doesn't want the grand prize. She wants a solid, respectable footing.

After the pageant comes Director Li, a senior official in the Nationalist government who notices her around the time of the contest. He doesn't court her; he arranges things. He installs her in a flat in a building called the Alice Apartments and visits every so often, discreetly, coming and going without fuss. Mr. Cheng understands and withdraws from her life. Jiang Lili cools toward her. Wang Qiyao lives like a canary in a gilded cage and spends most of her time waiting. What she waits for isn't love. It's the sound of a man's footsteps.

By 1948 the country is coming apart. On his last visit Director Li leaves her a carved wooden box Setup ③full of gold bars and tells her it's hers. Then he gets on a plane, the plane crashes, and he is dead. Still very young, Wang Qiyao has already been a kind of widow, and all she has left is a box.

Part Two (i) · Wuqiao: hiding in stopped time

With Shanghai about to change hands, she leaves for Wuqiao, a water town south of the Yangtze where her grandmother lives: canals, stone bridges, days that barely move. There is almost no plot in this stretch. What Wang Anyi writes is a kind of stillness. Time in the town doesn't move, and Wang Qiyao's heart sinks to the bottom like a stone in a canal. A young local named A'er, who has read a few books, falls into a speechless adoration of the woman from Shanghai. She sees it and neither accepts nor refuses. She knows she has to go back. Wuqiao is a place a person can hide, but not a place a Shanghai woman can live.

Part Two (ii) · Ping'an Lane: a stove, mahjong, and a child that can't be born

In the 1950s she returns to Shanghai and moves into an ordinary lane called Ping'an Lane, the Lane of Peace. She qualifies as a nurse and hangs out a sign offering injections to the neighbors, and that is how she lives. In the new society everything belonging to old Shanghai must be put away, and so she puts it away. Her clothes go plain; she says less. The box goes into the deepest hiding place she has.

But old Shanghai can't be put away completely. In the lane lives Mrs. Yan, the wife of a capitalist whose fortune still holds and whose household still keeps up appearances. She recognizes Wang Qiyao as one of her own kind and starts dropping by. Mrs. Yan has a cousin, Kang Mingxun, whom the children call Uncle Maomao. He is the son of a concubine in a wealthy clan, clever and well-mannered and always aware that he is the son of the second wife. Later a fourth joins them: Sasha, half Chinese and half Russian, the son of an old revolutionary and therefore someone with standing in those years, though himself a bit of a drifter.

So, through the winters around 1957, four people gather in a small room in Ping'an Lane around a stove. They play mahjong, make pastries, toast rice cakes, simmer sweet rice-wine dumplings, and let afternoon after afternoon dissolve. All of them know what kind of era is going on outside. None of them mentions it. In those afternoons Wang Qiyao and Kang Mingxun fall in love.

She becomes pregnant. Kang's family will never accept a former beauty queen and kept woman, and he hasn't the nerve to defy them; he backs away. Wang Qiyao doesn't weep or make scenes. She does something almost frighteningly cool: she lets Sasha believe the child is his. At first Sasha accepts it. Later he seems to half understand, and before long he vanishes from Ping'an Lane, reportedly to the Soviet Union. Kang knows the truth and says nothing.

Then Mr. Cheng reappears. He has never married, and he has aged. When he hears what situation she is in, he asks no questions; he comes every day to look after her and sees her through the pregnancy. They very nearly become a couple, but Wang Qiyao never takes that last step, and Cheng never presses her. The baby is a girl, Weiwei. Over these years the once passionate Jiang Lili marries a Communist cadre who came south with the army, joins the Party, turns coarse and rigid, and eventually dies of liver cancer. In 1966 Mr. Cheng kills himself. Part Two stops dead there: the man who took the portrait of the proper young lady of Shanghai does not survive 1966. The novel then skips the next ten years entirely.

Part Three · The 1980s: old Shanghai comes back, as a counterfeit

When we see Wang Qiyao again it's the 1980s. She is in her fifties, still good-looking, still particular, and the young women of the lane copy the way she dresses. Weiwei has grown into a different kind of Shanghai girl, flashy and trend-chasing, both envious of her mother's refinements and impatient with them. Weiwei's friend Zhang Yonghong, the best-dressed girl in the lane, actually gets on better with Wang Qiyao than Weiwei does. Eventually Weiwei marries a man named Xiao Lin and goes with him to America, and Wang Qiyao is left in the flat alone.

The city is changing, and changing in an odd direction: people have begun to miss old Shanghai. Old records, old furniture, the style of the 1940s are suddenly fashionable again. Wang Qiyao's little room fills up once more with young people who come to drink tea, dance, and hear her talk about the past. One of them is twenty-six and is known as Old Colour, a bit of Shanghainese slang for a man devoted to old-fashioned style and old things. He is infatuated with the 1940s; he collects old records, wears old-style clothes, and talks about the old city with more rapture than anyone who actually lived there. And Wang Qiyao is that era's living specimen.

They become lovers, a woman in her fifties and a man in his twenties. But Old Colour soon discovers that the Shanghai he loves is real in her, and also old. She gets tired. She calculates. She fusses over small things. The genuine article is never as pretty as the reproduction. He starts avoiding her. Wang Qiyao panics, and she takes out the wooden box she has hidden for more than thirty years, pushes it toward him, and begs him to stay with her a little longer. He won't take it. He leaves.

The last to come is Long Legs, Zhang Yonghong's boyfriend: tall, smooth-talking, always boasting about his connections and his money, in fact an empty shell buried in debt. By some route he learns there is gold in Wang Qiyao's flat. One night in 1986 he slips in to steal the box, and she catches him. She doesn't scream. She talks to him in a tone of contempt, as if she has seen right through him, saying in effect that she has known plenty of his type and will report him. Long Legs panics and gets his hands around her throat.

Payoff ③ ↑Back in Part One the carved box looked like the last bit of decency a great man could leave his mistress, and the reader may even have felt relieved for her: at least she would never be poor. But it followed her for forty years, from the Alice Apartments to Wuqiao, to Ping'an Lane, to the flat of the 1980s, hidden again every time the sky changed. In Part Three it surfaces twice: once when she pushes it toward Old Colour and it can't buy her a man, and once when Long Legs comes for it and it costs her her life. What Director Li left her was not wealth but an ending deferred for forty years. The elegance of it is that the box is never anything but a quiet box. No one ever thinks of it as a weapon.

Payoff ② ↑As Long Legs's grip tightens, the lamp overhead is knocked swinging. In her last seconds of sight Wang Qiyao sees it and suddenly remembers the film studio forty years ago: the bed, the woman lying across it, the lamp above her. Only now does she understand that the woman was herself. When we read the opening, we took it as a schoolgirl's brush with how films get made. Wang Anyi had already set her ending into the frame. Then the lamp was a prop and the death was fake. Forty years later the set has been struck, and the death is real.

Payoff ① ↑As the novel closes, the pigeons are circling over the rooftops of the lanes as always. At the beginning, they were the city's only witnesses, seeing everything and saying nothing. At the end they have seen what happened behind this one window in Ping'an Lane, and still they say nothing, and still they fly. A woman's forty years have ended, and the city hasn't missed a breath. By welding the first page to the last with the same flock of birds, the novel makes plain that it was never only about Wang Qiyao.

If you want the fuse, it isn't Long Legs's greed, or Old Colour's fickleness. The fuse is third place in 1946. Being Third Miss is what makes Director Li notice her, and so comes the flat, the gold, and an identity she can never step out of: from then on she is a woman of old Shanghai. Under the new order she has to hide that identity; in the 1980s people come to consume it. Kang Mingxun is too afraid of it to marry her, Old Colour falls in love with it, and Long Legs kills her for the gold it brought her. Every man in her life, the ones who loved her, feared her, or wanted something from her, was responding to the girl of 1946. Only she had to live through the forty years after.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · The film set: a girl sees her own death and doesn't know it

Replay it: a three-walled bedroom, a bed, a lamp, a woman acting dead. Crew members bustle around, the director calls out, the woman doesn't move. Wang Qiyao stands in the darkness beyond the set and watches for a long time.

Why does it work? First, it is the novel's only prophecy, and it is written as an aside. No character calls it an omen; the narrator passes over it in a few plain sentences, wedged between a schoolgirl's outing and her screen test. Our attention is pulled toward whether she might become a film star, and nobody is watching the lamp. Second, the angle is ruthless. The one looking is Wang Qiyao; the one looked at is a woman playing a corpse. She first meets her fate as a spectator, and the rest of her life is spent being looked at: by the camera, by pageant judges, by Director Li, by the gossip of the lanes, by the nostalgic young people of the 1980s. Third, the lamp is electric, studio light, man-made. From the very start, old Shanghai's beauty in this book smells faintly of a stage set: bright, lovely, fake, and capable of killing you.

Two · Winter in Ping'an Lane: the world turns over, the dumplings simmer

Replay it: a winter afternoon in the late 1950s, a small room in Ping'an Lane, a gray sky at the window. Rice cakes toasting on the stove, sweet dumplings in a pot. Four people around a mahjong table, a former beauty queen, a capitalist's wife, the son of a concubine, and a half-Russian cadre's son, cracking melon seeds and making idle talk. The afternoon passes, it gets dark, everyone goes home. The next afternoon they come back.

Why does it work? Because Wang Anyi keeps history entirely out of the frame and lets only its pressure seep in. The book offers just a few scattered strokes about what is happening in those years, but the reader knows, and everyone in the room knows. Mrs. Yan's fortune could vanish any day, Kang's family background is a liability, and Wang Qiyao's past cannot be mentioned at all. The more finely they spend their afternoons, the more painstaking the pastries and the lighter the talk, the more the finesse looks like held breath. The rhythm is slow and repetitive, afternoon after afternoon, almost nothing happening. Yet it is precisely in this nothing that the feeling between Wang Qiyao and Kang grows, until she is carrying his child. What this passage captures is a person in a great upheaval guarding one small patch of private warmth, and how thin that warmth is.

Three · The last night: such a small death

Replay it: late at night, a tall young man rummages in the dark. The woman who lives there wakes, switches on the light, looks at him without fear, and says with a cold laugh that she'll turn him in. He lunges. The lamp swings. She remembers forty years ago.

Why does it work? Because Wang Anyi deliberately makes this death very small. The killer is not the evil of an era, not a political campaign, not the great hand of fate. He's a braggart in debt, a nobody, who panics over a box of gold and over being seen through by an old woman. Wang Qiyao survived 1949, the 1950s, the decade the novel won't show, only to die in a burglary that would barely make the city pages. The smallness is an anticlimax, and it's also the cruelest thing in the book: history didn't kill her; ordinary life did. And that last contempt is so completely Wang Qiyao. Forty years of keeping up appearances mean that even at the most dangerous moment she won't plead. She has to put him in his place. Her refinement protects her dignity one last time, and gets her killed.

The book's core devices

The Song of Everlasting Sorrow isn't a novel built on a speculative premise. Its core devices are two: a narrator who writes the city first and the person second, and a way of handling time that keeps history offstage.

First, the city is the protagonist and people are its expressions. The opening devotes long stretches to lanes, gossip, inner chambers and pigeons without a single character appearing, and many readers give up right there. But these chapters aren't background; they are the rules of the game. In this book the individual grows out of the collective, and "Wang Qiyao" is a type before she is a person. That gives the narrator an unusual voice. She seldom enters a character's interior monologue. She speaks instead like an old Shanghainese who knows the lanes inside out, commenting from a slight distance, and often turning one particular person straight into a generalization: this is how Shanghai women are, this is how minds work in the lanes. The effect is that every choice Wang Qiyao makes is at once her character and the city's. What holds up is that it truly captures a civic temperament: shrewd, practical, attentive to face, making a dignified life in the cracks. The price is that the characters' inner lives are often buried under commentary. Some readers feel Wang Qiyao is an object turned over and examined rather than a person who can be hurt.

Second, great events stay out of the picture and enter only as temperature. The change of regime in 1949 is, in the book, a spell of hiding in Wuqiao. The campaigns of the 1950s are the silence nobody breaks at the mahjong table. 1966 is Mr. Cheng's death and a decade of blank pages. The reform era is a nostalgia craze and Old Colour. Wang Anyi almost never writes politics head-on. She writes how people in a room adjust their breathing when politics lands in it. This is a choice, not an accident of avoidance. She means to show that a city's real life is not in the headlines but in daily meals, fabrics, gossip and afternoon tea. Literarily it is very persuasive, but honesty requires saying that it is one angle, not the whole of history. The people left out of frame, the ones whose homes were ransacked, who were denounced, who had no stove and no pastries, are almost invisible here.

What it forces you to think about

One · Wang Qiyao at twenty: a flat and a box of gold, yes or no?

You're barely out of school, you've just placed third in a beauty pageant, and your family is ordinary. A powerful man is willing to keep you, on the condition that you be the woman he can't acknowledge. On the other side is Mr. Cheng, sincere and attentive, a man with a photo studio. Whom do you choose?

First, the strongest case for going with Director Li. In Shanghai in 1946, what could a girl with no family name and no degree trade for a secure future? Sincerity doesn't pay rent, and a photo studio might not survive the chaos either. Li offers concrete things: a home, safety from being pushed around, a box of gold that holds its value under any government. In hindsight, that gold is exactly what gave her a little extra footing in the new era. People who say she "sold herself" usually assume she had better options. She didn't. She simply took the widest of the very few narrow roads her world left open to women.

The other side is just as strong. That widest road was a dead end from the first day. A mistress has no standing, and a powerful man's life is tied tighter than anyone's to the fortunes of a regime. She spent her best years waiting for a man's footsteps and the next forty hiding a past she couldn't speak of. And Mr. Cheng's devotion, the one thing in her life given without calculation, was something she set down with her own hands and never picked up again. She bought security, but what she paid with was a whole life lived under other people's eyes.

Where does the author stand? Wang Anyi never says a harsh word about Wang Qiyao; she even defends her, presenting the Shanghai woman's practicality as a survival intelligence. But she has Mr. Cheng die at the end of Part Two and turns the gold into a murder weapon. She doesn't judge the choice. She just lets the interest come due, payment by payment. Bring it home: a steady job you feel nothing for, a secure relationship without a spark, against something you truly want and can't be sure of. How many people have you watched choose the first, and how many of them later said they had no regrets?

Two · The mahjong table: is guarding one afternoon against the storm dignity, or hiding?

Outside, momentous things are happening; people are suffering, people are being denounced. You and a few friends sit indoors cooking sweet dumplings and playing mahjong, and nobody mentions the outside. What kind of life is that?

First, the strongest case that it is dignity. Ordinary people can't change their era, but they can decide how to spend an afternoon. In a time when everything is being redefined, insisting on fine pastries, neatly folded clothes, and the small tenderness between friends is a refusal to be swallowed. It shouts no slogans, but it keeps alive the idea that a person can still live for herself. If you can't even keep an afternoon, what can you keep? The patience and tenderness with which Wang Anyi writes these scenes are a testimony to that kind of resistance.

The other side: this warmth has an entry fee. The people who can sit around a stove eating pastries still have savings at home, haven't yet been searched, haven't yet had their backgrounds dragged into the open. Their not mentioning the outside is, to some degree, a privilege. More to the point, all this private care protects no one. Kang still doesn't dare marry her; Mr. Cheng still doesn't survive 1966. Burying your head in afternoon tea may not be resistance at all, just a little more pleasure before the storm reaches your own door.

Where does the author stand? She clearly loves these afternoons; they are among the most moving pages in the book. Yet she ends every one of the people at that table in separation, flight or death. She loves this daily life, but she doesn't pretend it's an umbrella. Bring it home: there is something awful in the news every day. Do you ever deliberately switch off your phone and simply eat a good meal? Is that meal self-protection, or looking away?

Three · Old Colour's love: is loving an era the same as loving a person?

A twenty-six-year-old falls in love with a woman in her fifties, or more precisely with the 1940s he never lived through, which she seems to embody. When he finds out that she, too, gets old and tired and petty, he leaves.

First, the strongest case for Old Colour. His infatuation isn't fake. In a rough age just crawling out of scarcity, he wants to hold on to a little beauty, a little refinement, a little depth of history, and Wang Qiyao is all of that in one person. Loving someone very often starts with loving a certain light in them; when the light dims and the heart cools, that's human, not a crime. Besides, he really was drawn to her, and he never tried to take her money. He wouldn't even take the gold, and in this book that counts as the cleanest exit anyone makes.

The other side: he never once saw Wang Qiyao the person. He saw a specimen of old Shanghai, a mirror for his own taste. The forty years she got through, the hunger, the fear, raising a child alone, held no interest for him at all. The cruelest thing about nostalgia is that it wants the past's beauty and none of its cost, and Wang Qiyao is precisely the one who had to pay the cost. When he left, he took his fantasy with him and left behind a woman he had just woken up and then abandoned.

Where does the author stand? Wang Anyi is plainly ironic about the 1980s nostalgia craze; the nickname Old Colour is itself a little mocking. But she doesn't make him a villain. She lets the genuine article and the reproduction look each other in the eye, and lets the reproduction blink first. Bring it home: when you love a city, an old object, a golden age someone else told you about, are you in love with the thing itself, or with the self you'd like to become?

Motifs: what Wang Anyi keeps writing about

Across more than forty years of writing, Wang Anyi keeps returning to a few things. First, Shanghai and its ordinary people. Her later novel Fu Ping follows a young woman from the Yangzhou countryside into Shanghai and the migrants around her, the nannies, boatmen and shantytown dwellers. Tianxiang goes back to the Ming dynasty to show how the women of one Shanghai household turned embroidery into a craft and a family fortune. Set beside The Song of Everlasting Sorrow, the three look like the same city photographed from three directions. Second, women's bodies and desire. The "Love" trilogy of the 1980s, about passion repressed and then erupting in small towns and on barren mountains, caused real controversy at the time. Wang Qiyao is far more restrained than those characters, but her choices are driven by desire and circumstance just the same. Third, the membrane between the individual and the collective. Baotown shows a village turning a child's death into an official model of heroism; The Song of Everlasting Sorrow shows a city turning a woman into a type.

In that body of work, this is the most complete link: it writes Shanghai as a person, and a person as Shanghai.

Controversy and criticism

Too long a start, too much commentary. This is the most common complaint from ordinary readers. The opening chapters on lanes, gossip and pigeons have almost no plot, and the narrator's commentary keeps cutting in throughout. Admirers call it the soul of the book; detractors say it turns the novel into an essay and drowns the characters in discussion.

Nostalgia as merchandise. The mid-1990s were exactly when the "old Shanghai" craze took off, with calendar-girl posters, qipao and colonial-era villas repackaged as cultural goods. Some critics argue the novel rode that wave and even amplified it, romanticizing a semicolonial glamour. Others answer that Old Colour is a satire of precisely that wave, and that Wang Anyi is writing about the emptiness of nostalgia, not indulging it.

Missing history. The political campaigns stay almost entirely offstage, and the Cultural Revolution decade is simply skipped. Critics see this as glossing over, letting us see the frustrations of a petty-bourgeois few but not the suffering of the many. Defenders call it a deliberate angle, the large seen through the small. Both have a point; readers can decide.

An abrupt ending. Wang Qiyao strangled by a small-time hustler strikes some readers as too much like a crime report, out of key with the long, finely woven prose before it. By Wang Anyi's own account in interviews, the novel actually began with a news item: a former Miss Shanghai murdered in the 1980s (as she tells it, in substance).

The "heir to the Shanghai school" label. After publication critics often placed Wang Anyi in a line with Eileen Chang, as an inheritor of the Shanghai tradition of writing about the city's women. Other scholars stress how different they are: Chang is cold, acid, a chronicler of decline; Wang Anyi writes the resilience of daily life itself. The label is still argued over.

The film. Stanley Kwan's 2005 adaptation changes a good deal and divided audiences; people who have seen the film but not read the book often mistake its plot for the novel's.

Crossovers

  • Evolutionary psychology · Dunbar's gossip hypothesis: Robin Dunbar (1996) argued that much of language exists to trade news about others, standing in for the grooming primates use to bind groups. The lanes' gossip is the city's breath. The difference: Dunbar's gossip binds people together; the gossip here mostly pins them down.
  • Sociology · Goffman's front and back stage: Goffman (1959) described people keeping up an image on the front stage and relaxing only backstage. Wang Qiyao is on the front stage for life, keeping up her station even as she is strangled. The difference: Goffman's backstage is safe; hers, the box, is what kills her.
  • Sociology · Simmel on fashion: Simmel (1904) argued that fashion satisfies two urges at once, to imitate and to stand apart. The lane girls copy Wang Qiyao's clothes; she keeps her distance through refinement. The difference: Simmel meant distinctions between classes, while hers had to be drawn quietly in an era that demanded everyone be the same.
  • Psychology · nostalgia research: Experiments by Constantine Sedikides and colleagues find that nostalgia raises a sense of meaning and belonging, and tends to surface in loneliness or unease. Old Colour seems to settle himself on someone else's past. The difference: the studies mostly measure longing for one's own past, and his is for an era he never lived.

③ Third place is exactly right: not the most dazzling, but the one best suited to everyday life.
