Novels, Deep-Read · Qian Zhongshu · No.89

Fortress Besieged

Qian Zhongshu · 1947 · Nine chapters

中文 →

Late July, 1937. The Red Sea is well behind them; a French liner is pushing across the Indian Ocean toward China. It is too hot to sleep, and the deck is strewn with bodies trying to catch a breeze.
Among the passengers is a Miss Bao who wears very little, and a few returning students have privately nicknamed her "Partial Truth": the truth is naked, and she is almost naked.
Watching her is a young man who has spent four years drifting around Europe and has a fake doctorate in his luggage. His name is Fang Hongjian. In a few weeks, Shanghai will be at war.

This piece contains complete spoilers: whom Fang loves, whom he marries, who turns out to own the twin of his fake diploma, and the night the marriage comes apart. The novel has no real suspense; its pleasure is all in how things get there, and without the ending there is no way to talk about the clock.

In one sentence

A returned student who is not bad, not stupid and not much use to anyone comes home from Europe to wartime China, and spends the book entering and escaping one enclosure after another (a Shanghai drawing room, a provincial university, his own marriage), each time sure the door on the other side is the way out. It is a comedy about wanting itself: whatever you get turns, the moment you get it, into a place you need to escape.

Coordinates

Qian Zhongshu (1910–1998), from Wuxi, studied Western literature at Tsinghua and then at Oxford, and taught at wartime universities in the interior. He was first of all a scholar; his later Limited Views and On the Art of Poetry cite thousands of texts in half a dozen languages. This is the only novel he finished. By Yang Jiang's account, he decided to write it after watching one of her plays in Japanese-occupied Shanghai, and she urged him to cut back his teaching so he could. He wrote it between 1944 and 1946, it was serialized from 1946, and it came out as a book in 1947.

Its place in literary history is unusual. Most Chinese fiction after the May Fourth movement wrote about suffering; this one writes about absurdity, specifically the absurdity of intellectuals. After 1949 it went essentially out of print on the mainland for three decades. C. T. Hsia's A History of Modern Chinese Fiction (1961) rescued it abroad, calling it the most delightful and most carefully wrought novel in modern Chinese literature. It was reprinted in Beijing in 1980, a television adaptation followed in 1990, and it became the book whose central line every Chinese reader can recite.

The story

Act I · The ship: a doctorate for sale

Fang Hongjian is the eldest son of a provincial gentleman who passed the imperial exams under the Qing. His father arranged his engagement to the daughter of Mr. Zhou, manager of a Shanghai bank. Fang wrote home from college asking to break it off and was scolded into silence; soon afterward the fiancée he had never met died of illness. Mr. Zhou took the money set aside for her dowry and used it to send his would-be son-in-law to Europe, as a gesture of mourning and respectability.

In London, Paris and Berlin, Fang switches schools and subjects so often that after four years he has sampled everything and finished nothing. As he prepares to sail home, letters arrive from both his father and his father-in-law, each hoping for a doctorate. He does not want to disappoint two old men, and he does not want to actually earn one. Conveniently, an Irishman is in business selling diplomas from a nonexistent American institution called Clayton University. Foreshadow ①Fang haggles him down and buys a Ph.D. in philosophy for a modest sum. His justification is a dignified one: it is filial piety, like telling your parents only the good news. The people deceived are the people who love him, and the point is their peace of mind. He privately vows never to flaunt it.

Life on board is hot and idle. Miss Bao, dark-skinned and shapely, has a fiancé waiting in Hong Kong. She and Fang drift into a shipboard affair, a few murky days on deck and in her cabin. Watching coldly from nearby is Miss Su Wenwan, a fellow passenger with a doctorate in French literature from Lyon, proper in background and proper in temper, set on marrying someone who matters. She despises Miss Bao and is rather interested in Fang. When the ship reaches Hong Kong, Miss Bao's fiancé, a dark and middle-aged doctor, comes aboard to collect her, and she walks off with him without a decent goodbye. Only then does Fang understand that he was something she used to pass the time before landing. He turns around, and Miss Su is still there.

Act II · Shanghai: love in a drawing room, and a French proverb at dinner

Back in Shanghai, Mr. Zhou is so proud that he has a notice printed in a newspaper, the "returned doctor" pictured beside his dead daughter. Fang is mortified but can't take it back. Visiting his hometown, he is invited to lecture at the county middle school on the influence of Western culture in Chinese history. He prepares carelessly, ad-libs, and announces that the only Western things that ever took lasting root in China are opium and syphilis. The students are delighted and the headmaster turns green. The story becomes a local joke, and it is Qian's first verdict on his hero: he isn't bad, he just never takes what matters seriously, and can never resist being clever where it doesn't.

In August, fighting breaks out in Shanghai and the Fang family flees into the foreign concessions. Fang lives with the Zhous and holds a sinecure at his father-in-law's bank. He becomes a regular at Miss Su's house. Her drawing room is a small Vanity Fair. There is Zhao Xinmei, back from America and working at a news agency, who regards Miss Su as his and Fang as his rival; there is Chu Shenming, a "philosopher" who claims to correspond with Bertrand Russell; there is Cao Yuanlang, who writes allusion-stuffed modern verse. Miss Su enjoys being competed for and blows hot and cold on Fang.

But in that room Fang meets someone else: Miss Su's young cousin, Tang Xiaofu. She is barely twenty, wears no makeup, is quick without being cruel, and is the one person in the book who is not performing. Fang falls for her on the spot. To stay near the cousin he has to keep courting the older one, and the longer this goes on, the more Miss Su believes he is courting her.

To humiliate his rival, Zhao throws a dinner full of brilliant talkers and sets out to get Fang drunk. After several rounds, the talk turns to marriage. Chu Shenming invokes Russell and an old English saying: marriage is like a gilded birdcage; the birds outside want in, and the birds inside want out. Miss Su caps it. The French, she says, have a similar saying, except it is not a cage but a besieged fortress: those outside want to storm in, and those inside want to escape. Foreshadow ②The table nods appreciatively and moves on to the next witticism. Fang does get drunk that night, and is spectacularly sick on the way home.

Act III · Rain: a letter, an old ledger, and a breakup he won't contest

Fang finally writes to Miss Su, telling her he is unworthy and she should look elsewhere. She reads the letter and understands whom he actually wants. She does not make a scene. She does something worse: she hands her cousin Fang's entire file, the affair with Miss Bao, the diploma of uncertain origin, the newspaper notice printed beside a dead fiancée's photograph.

Tang Xiaofu summons Fang and goes through the charges one by one. He could explain: the diploma was to comfort two old men, Miss Bao was a lapse, the notice was his father-in-law's doing. He explains nothing. He agrees that she is right and walks out. It is raining. He stands across the street from her house in the rain for a long time, soaked through. Upstairs, Tang watches him and has already softened. All she wanted was for him to argue his case once, and he didn't think he deserved even that. So they miss each other. It is the closest Fang ever comes to the thing he actually wants, and the last time.

Soon Miss Su marries Cao Yuanlang. Zhao, who has spent years as her backup, is left with nothing either, and the two rivals become friends; Zhao jokes that they are brothers in the same lost cause. Fang falls out with the Zhous and moves out, losing the bank job too. Then Zhao is offered a post at a newly founded university in Hunan and, almost in passing, gets Fang a teaching job as well.

Act IV · The road and Sanlü University: a city smaller and more crowded than Shanghai

The journey takes over a month: a boat from Shanghai to Ningbo, then inland stage by stage, in buses packed like sardine tins, sleeping in inns where the beds have lice, short of money, everyone calculating. There are five of them. Besides Zhao and Fang there is Li Meiting, the incoming head of Chinese literature, who hauls along an enormous iron trunk, half full of his "scholarly index cards" and half full of Western medicines he plans to sell at a markup in the interior; there is Gu Erqian, who flatters everyone the whole way; and there is Sun Roujia, a new graduate going to be an assistant in the English department, quiet, apparently timid, forever asking Mr. Fang or Mr. Zhao what she should do.

On the road Zhao looks at Fang and gives him a sentence he will probably remember for the rest of his life, roughly: "You're not unpleasant. You're just completely useless." (Chapter 5, paraphrased.) Fang laughs. It isn't an insult. It's a diagnosis.

Sanlü University sits in a small county town in western Hunan. Its president, Gao Songnian, is a biologist turned administrator, slippery as a bar of soap. He had promised Fang a full professorship; in person he downgrades it to associate, because Fang's résumé lists no degree. Fang kept his vow never to use the fake diploma, and here it costs him.

Then he meets Han Xueyu, chair of the history department: taciturn, married to a White Russian whom he introduces to everyone as an American, and holder, according to his résumé, of a Ph.D. from Clayton University in the United States.

Payoff ① ↑The piece of paper Fang quietly hid on the ship in Act I has just met its twin. The same fake diploma from the same diploma mill: one man is too ashamed to show it and gets demoted; the other hangs it on the wall and becomes a department chair. Qian buries a joke in the opening and, three hundred pages later, turns it into a mirror. The problem was never whether the degree was real; it was whether you have the nerve to bluff. Fang knows Han's secret, Han soon guesses that Fang knows, and two guilty men spend the year watching each other on a small campus.

Sanlü is Shanghai in miniature: factions, banquets, matchmaking, informing. Wang Chuhou, head of Chinese, tries to marry off both Zhao and Fang. Zhao instead grows too close to Wang's young and pretty wife; they are spotted walking together one night, and he packs up and leaves overnight, asking Fang to look after Sun Roujia. Li Meiting becomes dean of discipline and spends his time catching others out. Fang's English students don't think much of him, and since he never picks a side, nobody speaks up for him.

Sun Roujia, in this town, is a young woman with no protector. A male colleague sends her love letters; she brings them to Fang and asks what to do. Later she tells him someone has written to her family claiming she and Fang are romantically involved, and her father has heard about it. Fang, guilty and sympathetic, wants to shield her. One evening they are out talking it over; she is frightened and upset, he tries to comfort her, one thing leads to another, and he says a few things that amount to a proposal. By the time he has caught up with himself, they are engaged, and soon the whole campus knows. In his own mind he did not ask for this marriage; he slid into it. Looking back at her timidity on the road, at every "what should I do?", the reader starts to wonder whether it was fear or something cleverer.

At the end of the year, President Gao does not renew Fang's contract, though he does renew Sun's. She declines it. The two of them leave Sanlü together for Hong Kong.

Act V · Hong Kong and Shanghai: the people inside, and a clock that runs slow

They marry in Hong Kong, with Zhao's help. The quarrelling starts before the wedding: she resents how much he leans on Zhao and how small he looks next to him. In Hong Kong they run into Miss Su, now Mrs. Cao, who is cool to Fang and cooler still to his bride, and Sun takes it out on her husband afterward.

Back in Shanghai they rent a small room in the concession. Through Zhao, Fang becomes head of the reference library at a newspaper; Sun works at a factory where her aunt holds a senior post. On his side, Fang's father writes diaries and poems while two sisters-in-law gossip that Sun is stuck-up and never pays her respects; on hers, the aunt looks down on Fang as a man going nowhere. Every family dinner is a diplomatic mission. For the new household, old Mr. Fang sends over an heirloom striking clock as a gift. It doesn't keep good time; it runs slow. Foreshadow ③Nobody gives it a second thought.

The newspaper begins to tilt toward the Japanese-backed collaborators; the editor-in-chief resigns, and Fang resigns with him. Then a letter comes from Zhao in Chongqing, offering Fang work there. Fang wants to go; he has had enough of this city. Sun refuses: she has a job and an aunt, so why should she follow to Chongqing a man who can't support himself?

The last fight starts over something trivial and goes straight for the bone. He insults her aunt; she insults his family, his uselessness, his Zhao Xinmei. She hurls a comb at him and it strikes his face; in the scuffle he shoves her away. He slams out and walks the cold streets for hours, telling himself he will leave for Chongqing tomorrow and give up everything. Worn out, he softens, and heads home meaning to talk to her properly.

When he gets back, she has gone to her aunt's. He is so tired that he falls asleep at once.

Payoff ② ↑At that dinner in Act II, "the besieged fortress" was a bon mot tossed between two show-offs; Fang was there and too drunk to hear it. So was the reader, more or less, assuming it was about someone else's marriage. Only now does it come clear: the whole novel is that one line unfolded. The ship, the drawing room, the university, the marriage: he entered every fortress with the urge to storm in and left it with the urge to escape. The best touch is who delivers it. Qian hands the key to the entire book to its two least reliable mouths, a bluffing philosopher and a vain doctor of letters, so the reader takes it from them with no guard up.

Late in the night, the old clock begins, unhurriedly, to strike. Six times. Payoff ③ ↑Six o'clock was five hours ago, when Fang was on his way home meaning to be kinder to his wife, and Sun was at home waiting for him to come to supper, hoping he'd make peace with her aunt. At the same hour, both of them were thinking about making up, and neither waited for the other. A slow old thing sent over as a wedding gift ends up tolling the hour for the marriage. The book's last sentence says the outdated timepiece unintentionally holds an irony and a sadness about life deeper than any words, any laughter or tears.

If there is a single fuse here, it isn't Sun's scheming, Miss Su's revenge or even the war. The fuse is the fake diploma on the ship, or more exactly the reasoning Fang used when he bought it: keep others satisfied first, sort out the real thing later. He used that method on his father and father-in-law, then on Tang Xiaofu (no defense, because defending himself was too much trouble), then on Sun Roujia (no refusal, because refusing was too hard). He never actively chose anything in his life, so his life was chosen for him. He didn't storm a single one of those fortresses; the crowd carried him in.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · The dinner: hiding the key to the book inside a farce

The dinner itself is pure comedy. Zhao is plotting to get a rival drunk, Chu boasts of his friendship with Russell, Cao shows off his reading, and Fang gets steadily woozier. The fortress line appears in the middle of a string of quips, weighted exactly like the nonsense around it, and is buried by the next joke.

Why does it work? It's a conjuror's move: you pass the important object while the audience is laughing. Put the line in the mouth of a trustworthy character at a quiet moment and readers would file it as The Theme, then check every later chapter against it, and the novel would become an illustration. Qian has two ridiculous people let it slip offhand while the hero is too drunk to notice. The reader only catches up at the end, and what they feel isn't "there's the theme" but "I heard this ages ago." Which is exactly Fang's situation.

Two · Clayton's twin: one prop, used twice

When Han Xueyu enters, Qian doesn't rush the reveal. First the man's reticence and apparent simplicity, then his wife's background, then his résumé, and only at the end does the word "Clayton" jump off the page at Fang. Fang's first reaction isn't outrage but guilt. What frightens him isn't that Han is a fraud; it is that Han might know he is one too.

In craft terms, this is the mirror character: give your protagonist a shadow who differs by a single step. Han is who Fang would be if he had hung that paper on the wall. It means the reader can't comfortably stand with Fang and laugh at the con man, because Fang is the same sort of article, only with less nerve. The satire's blade turns here, from "them" back toward "us."

Three · The clock: turning a time lag into an ending

The ending has no reconciliation, no divorce, no summing up. There is a sleeping man, an empty room and a clock striking the wrong hour. At the last moment Qian takes the camera off his characters, gives the scene to an object, and then spells out, in a single sentence, what the six strokes correspond to: what each of them was thinking five hours earlier.

The power lies in turning an information gap into a physical fact. Both of them wanted to make peace at the same moment, separated by streets and pride, and the reader is the only one who sees both sides at once. The clock is that gap made audible: it strikes the past to a listener living in the present. A book full of wisecracks stops laughing only on its final page and ends on a sentence that is no longer a joke.

The book's core devices

Fortress Besieged isn't a novel built on a speculative premise. Its devices are two: one in the structure, one in the sentences.

The structural device is the besieged fortress itself: a shape that can be copied indefinitely. The rule is simple. Any position, seen from outside, glows because you can't reach it; once inside, it suffocates because you can't leave. Qian applies the rule to every setting: the ship (longing for port), his in-laws' house (longing to move out), the drawing room (longing to get in), the university (to escape Shanghai, then to escape the university), the marriage (see the table). What holds up is its precise grasp of a psychological illusion: we mistake changing places for changing ourselves. What doesn't hold up, or rather what the fiction needs rather than what life shows, is that every fortress is the same. In reality some people do get in and settle; some marriages and some jobs really are a way out. To make the shape visible, the novel trims away the exceptions. Tang Xiaofu is the one door that might have opened onto somewhere else, and the author shuts it early.

The sentence-level device is the simile. Qian's similes are famously abundant and famously barbed, one or two to a page: something compared to something absurdly remote that is, on reflection, exactly right, usually with a little dig attached. They aren't ornament; they set the book's key: the narrator always stands a head taller than the characters, seeing through every petty calculation and pinning it with a wicked analogy. The reward is that every page is funny. The cost is that the reader is trained to look down along with the narrator, and finds it hard to truly sympathize with anyone. In his preface, Qian says that in writing these people he never forgot they were human beings, sharing the basic nature of the featherless biped. The sentence is itself a specimen of that gaze: not hatred, but a lofty, almost zoological pity.

What it forces you to think about

One · The fake diploma: is lying to the people who love you kindness or cowardice?

Your father and father-in-law are old men. One needs face in his hometown; the other spent his dead daughter's dowry to send you abroad. You have nothing to show for four years, and all they want is a piece of paper. What do you do?

First, Fang's case at its strongest. The paper is useless to him, and he has no plan to use it to get a job; its only function is to put two old men at ease. The truth would have cost other people (his father's standing at home, his father-in-law's sense that the money had been wasted), while the lie costs only himself. A great deal of kindness looks like this: telling someone in a hospital bed that they'll be fine, telling your parents you're doing well. And in that era "degrees" were half real anyway; everyone was buying one sort of paper or another. To treat this as a grave moral question is a little naive.

The other side is just as strong, because the novel has already played out the consequences. The paper didn't just fool two old men; it taught Fang a way of living: satisfy others first, deal with the real thing later. His inability to speak up in front of Tang Xiaofu and his vulnerability to Han at Sanlü are both interest paid on that paper. And Han's existence shows more: once a society tacitly allows the purchase of credentials, the honest are forced to choose between buying one too or losing. A "kind" lie feeds the system a little more.

Where does the author stand? He never punishes Fang for buying the degree. He punishes him for not daring to use it, and rewards the man who does. What Qian satirizes is less lying than lying halfheartedly, which is a crueler and more pessimistic judgment. Bring it home: the bit of "polish" on your résumé, the figure you quote to your parents. Can you say exactly whom it's protecting?

Two · Sun Roujia: scheming, or the only move open to someone with no protector?

She is in her early twenties, alone on a small campus full of middle-aged men. One sends her love letters; someone writes gossip to her family. She settles on a soft-hearted, indecisive man and, step by step, lets the engagement "just happen."

First, her defense, made better than she could make it. Her resources are zero: no family connections, no position, no one who will stand up for a young woman being talked about. In that world a ruined reputation lasts a lifetime, and her only option is to get herself somewhere safe, fast. A man can go to Chongqing, to Hong Kong, to another school; she can't. And the man she picks isn't a bad one: Fang is one of the few people on that campus who doesn't calculate. That isn't scheming. That is playing a rigged hand rather well. After the marriage she wants a job, her own income, and the right not to drop everything and follow her husband. By today's standards, not one of those demands is excessive.

The other side: her method uses the very rigged system against him, turning gossip into leverage and timidity into a weapon. A marriage that exists because he didn't react in time carries both people's resentment from day one. He feels trapped, and she knows he feels it. It's hard to believe the cruelty of their later fights didn't grow from that root. And Fang is a person too; being steered into marriage one step at a time is a grievance that also deserves to be taken seriously.

Where does the author stand? The narrator is plainly unkind to her, often using small gestures to hint at calculation. Yet he also lets us see the Fang family's cold shoulder, the aunt's snobbery and the husband's helplessness. Half of Sun's sharpness was ground into her by those things. The character is more complex than the author's tone. Bring it home: in a relationship, is the one who speaks first and arranges things the more calculating one, or just the one with fewer exits?

Three · "Completely useless": is Fang's softness a flaw or a kind of cleanness?

Zhao says he isn't unpleasant, just completely useless. He can't network, won't take sides, doesn't defend himself and never goes in for the kill. In the end he has nothing.

First, the case that it's a flaw, at full strength. An adult is responsible for his own life. Every one of Fang's refusals to act is, at bottom, avoidance: he doesn't defend himself because it's tiresome, doesn't say no because it would give offense, doesn't choose because it's easier to leave choice to others and to luck. Plenty of people are hurt by his softness: Tang Xiaofu, Sun Roujia, even the two old men he deceived. In a time of upheaval, being useless isn't purity; it's a kind of laziness that hands your life to others to arrange.

But look at the "useful" people in the book: Gao Songnian, Li Meiting, Han Xueyu, Miss Su, all expert operators, all calculating. Fang is the only one in the whole fortress who doesn't calculate, and he is useless because he refuses to learn the useful skills. Every game he loses was rigged by someone else. If the price of usefulness is becoming Han Xueyu, then uselessness may be a pride he never quite gets to voice.

Where does the author stand? Qian treats Fang with affection laced with contempt: he gives him the most wit and self-mockery in the book, and not one real action. He seems to be saying that in a fortress like this, a clean man can only be a ridiculous one. Bring it home: the colleague who never takes sides and never gets promoted. Do you admire them, or are you relieved you aren't them?

Motifs: what Qian kept writing about

Qian wrote little fiction, but a few obsessions run through all of it. First, the vanity of intellectuals: "Cat," in his story collection Men, Beasts, Ghosts, depicts a Beijing literary salon whose celebrities flatter and despise one another, a dry run for Miss Su's drawing room; Fortress Besieged expands that salon into an entire nation's intelligentsia. Second, watching life from the margins: his essay collection is titled Written in the Margins of Life, and its stance, a sardonic onlooker annotating the edge of the page, becomes the narrator's position in the novel. Third, the gap between learning and character: people who have read a great deal can still be petty. This pessimism runs from the novel all the way to Limited Views, which uses thousands of citations to show, again and again, how little human nature has changed across times and cultures.

He is said to have started a second novel, The Heart of the Artichoke, whose manuscript was lost in a move around 1949 and never rewritten. So Fortress Besieged stands as the only finished link in that chain: he staked all his acid and all his pity on one useless man.

Controversy and criticism

Showing off, and too many similes: the most common criticism, and the most defensible. Similes arrive one after another, and the allusive witticisms are dense enough to leave you breathless. Some readers feel the author is performing, and that the characters drown in the narrator's cleverness. Admirers say that is simply Qian; detractors say a novel's narrator shouldn't steal more scenes than any of its characters.

Acid without sympathy: almost nobody in the book is treated with real generosity. Some critics see this as the price of satire; others as the author's limit, since he sees through everyone's petty calculations but seldom shows anyone's genuine constraints. Tang Xiaofu is the sole exception, and she is also the thinnest character, and the first to leave the stage.

The women: Miss Bao is a body to be looked at, Miss Su's doctorate is played for laughs, Sun Roujia is the calculating wife, and Tang Xiaofu is too pure to be real. Feminist readings in recent decades argue that the women are either traps or ideals and rarely as complicated as the men; others reply that the men are, if anything, even more ridiculous.

Where is the war? The story runs from 1937 to around 1940, the worst years of the war against Japan, yet the war is mostly background noise. As early as the late 1940s, critics complained that amid a national catastrophe the book dwelt on intellectuals' trivia. In his preface Qian says he set out to depict one part of society and one kind of person, not a panorama. Whether that answer suffices is for the reader to judge.

Real-life models: readers have long tried to match characters to particular scholars. Yang Jiang later wrote about the characters' origins, saying they were mostly composites rather than portraits of real people, but the talk of caricature has never quite died down.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · Hedonic adaptation: in 1978 Brickman and colleagues compared lottery winners with accident victims who had been paralyzed and found both groups drifting back toward their earlier levels of happiness. The thrill of storming in gets adapted away, which is the fortress's psychological engine. Where it differs: the theory says people return to baseline, while Fang ends up a little lower after every fortress.
  • Sociology · Randall Collins's "credential society": in The Credential Society (1979), Collins argued that degrees often certify not skills but entry into a status group, so credentials keep inflating. Clayton University is that logic pushed to its end: if the gatekeepers only check tickets, someone will sell fake ones. The book's version has nearly the same shape: what separates Fang and Han isn't learning but the nerve to hold the ticket up, because the people at the gate check tickets, not knowledge. Where it differs: Collins describes institutional inflation, while the novel adds a layer of shame, with the same fake ticket brandished by one man and hidden by another.
  • Philosophy · Schopenhauer's pendulum: in The World as Will and Representation, Schopenhauer says life swings like a pendulum between pain and boredom: pain while we lack, boredom once we have. The thirst outside the walls and the stuffiness inside are two ends of the same swing. Where it differs: for Schopenhauer this is the metaphysical nature of the will; Qian writes it as social comedy and draws no conclusion.
  • Buddhism · The suffering of not getting what you want: among the eight sufferings Buddhism names are not obtaining what you seek and being bound to what you dislike. Fang suffers the first outside the walls and the second inside his marriage. Where it differs: Buddhism points to an exit, the extinguishing of craving itself; the novel offers none, only a clock striking the wrong hour.
