Frankenstein

Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus · Mary Shelley · 1818 · Three volumes

中文 →

Ingolstadt, one in the morning, November. Rain ticks unevenly against the glass, and the candle is nearly out.
A student barely past twenty crouches beside a table. On it lies a body he has assembled himself: eight feet long, with yellow skin so thin the muscles and arteries show through, lustrous black hair, teeth as white as pearls. For this moment he has spent two years shuttling between charnel houses, dissecting rooms and the slaughterhouse.
Just as the flame gutters, a dull yellow eye opens. The chest heaves. A limb twitches.
He does not step forward. He turns and runs out of the room.

This piece contains complete spoilers: whom the creature kills first, who hangs for it, what becomes of the promised companion, who dies in the bridal bed, on which ship Victor dies, and where the creature goes at the end. The weight of this book sits entirely on cause and effect. Without following how each debt was run up, there is no way to argue about who owes whom.

In one sentence

A young man builds a living being out of dead parts, takes one look at it and flees; the abandoned creature teaches itself to speak, to read and to long for love, and after every outstretched hand is beaten back, it begins killing, one by one, the people its maker loves. This is not a story about a scientist hunted by a monster. It is a story about a father who refuses to be a father, and a child who comes to collect on that refusal.

Coordinates

Mary Shelley (1797–1851) was the daughter of the anarchist philosopher William Godwin and of Mary Wollstonecraft, author of A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, who died about ten days after giving birth to her. In the summer of 1816, eighteen-year-old Mary was living by Lake Geneva with the poet Percy Shelley, later her husband; their neighbours were Lord Byron and his physician, John Polidori. The Tambora eruption of the year before had left Europe with almost no summer, and in the endless rain Byron proposed that each of them write a ghost story. Mary's kept growing. It was published anonymously on New Year's Day 1818, in three volumes. The revised edition of 1831 added an introduction in which she describes "seeing", half asleep, a pale student kneeling beside the thing he had put together; it is the text most people read today.

Its place in literary history is double. It is a late masterpiece of the Gothic novel, and the critic Brian Aldiss, in Billion Year Spree (1973), made the case that it is the first true science fiction novel, because what makes the monster is neither magic nor a pact with the devil but a laboratory.

The story

Act One · On the ice: a man bound for the Pole hauls aboard a man coming back from it

Before the novel begins, the title page carries three lines from Milton's Paradise Lost, Adam's complaint after the Fall: Setup ①"Did I request thee, Maker, from my clay / To mould me Man?" (1818 title page; Paradise Lost, Book X). The reader turns the page, taking it for a suitably gloomy epigraph.

Then come letters. Robert Walton, an Englishman of twenty-eight, is writing to his sister, Mrs. Saville, back home. He grew up on the voyage narratives in his uncle's library, failed as a poet, came into money, and resolved to do what no one had done: sail through the Arctic, uncover the secret of the magnetic pole, find the passage to the Pacific. He keeps telling her the same thing: I have no friend.

The ship sails from Archangel and is soon locked in pack ice. One afternoon the crew see, far off across the ice, a sledge drawn by dogs racing north, driven by something shaped like a man but of gigantic size. The next morning a floe drifts up to the ship carrying another sledge and a man nearly frozen to death. Before he will come aboard, he asks where they are going.

His name is Victor Frankenstein. As he recovers, Walton quickly decides he has found the friend he has been longing for. Setup ②When Walton eagerly says he would sacrifice anything, his own life included, for the success of the voyage, Victor's face changes and he cries out, "Do you share my madness?" (Letter IV). He decides to tell this young man his story, not to pass the time but as a warning: look at what becomes of a man who pursues knowledge all the way to its end.

Act Two · Ingolstadt: a spark of being, and an escape

Victor comes from a distinguished Genevese family. His father, Alphonse, is a respected public man. The household takes in a fair-haired girl, Elizabeth (Victor's cousin in the 1818 text; in 1831, an orphan adopted from a poor Italian family), and his mother quietly intends her as Victor's future wife. There are two younger brothers, Ernest and William, a servant girl named Justine whom the family has taken in, and Victor's boyhood friend Henry Clerval, a lover of poetry and tales of chivalry.

At thirteen, at an inn at Thonon, Victor happens upon a volume of the Renaissance occultist Cornelius Agrippa and falls for alchemy. His father glances at it and calls it sad trash, without saying why. Victor later reflects that if his father had taken the trouble to explain, his life might have gone differently. At fifteen he watches a bolt of lightning reduce an old oak near the house to ribbons of splintered wood, and his interest turns to electricity.

At seventeen he is due to leave for the university at Ingolstadt in Bavaria. Just before he goes, Elizabeth catches scarlet fever; his mother nurses her, Elizabeth recovers, and his mother catches it and dies. On her deathbed she joins the two young people's hands. Victor carries the grief with him to university.

At Ingolstadt, Professor Krempe sneers at the old books he has read; Professor Waldman sets him alight. The ancient masters, Waldman says, promised impossibilities and did nothing; the modern masters promise very little, yet they have actually got inside nature's hiding places. Victor throws himself into chemistry and anatomy and for two years barely goes home. He has one question: where does life come from? To understand life, he studies death first, watching how bodies rot, how worms move into the eye and the brain. At last, he says, he discovered the cause of generation and life, and became able to animate lifeless matter.

He will not tell Walton how; he does not want him to follow the same road. He tells only what came next. Because the minute parts of the human frame were too fiddly to work with, he decided to build a giant, eight feet tall. His materials came from vaults, the dissecting room and the slaughterhouse. He grew emaciated, loathing the work and unable to stop, held up by one thought: a new species would bless him as its creator, and no father could claim the gratitude of his child as completely as he would deserve theirs.

Then comes "a dreary night of November" (chapter 5 in the standard text). Rain, the dying candle, the opened yellow eye. Two years of dreaming collapse in a second into horror and disgust. He rushes out of the workroom and throws himself, still dressed, onto his bed. Setup ③He dreams that he sees Elizabeth in the streets of Ingolstadt, glowing with health; delighted, he embraces and kisses her, and as his lips touch hers they turn livid, her features change, and he is holding the corpse of his dead mother, grave-worms crawling in the folds of the shroud.

He wakes in a cold sweat. Setup ④By the yellow moonlight coming through the shutters he sees the creature standing at the bed, holding up the curtain, its eyes fixed on him. Its mouth opens on a few sounds that are not yet words, and its face creases into a grin. One hand is stretched out, as if to hold him there. Victor escapes downstairs and spends the rest of the night pacing the courtyard.

In the morning he runs into Clerval, just off the coach. When he returns to his rooms, the creature is gone. Victor feels relief, and then collapses into a nervous fever that lasts for months, nursed by Clerval alone. He treats the illness as an ending, as if what he no longer sees has stopped existing.

Act Three · Geneva: a child, a miniature, a servant who hangs

Once he has recovered, a letter comes from his father: William, the youngest, is dead. The family was walking at Plainpalais outside the city; William ran off to play hide-and-seek and was found strangled. The miniature of their mother he had been wearing, which Elizabeth had let him put on, was gone.

Victor hurries home and, at night, walks to the place where William was killed. A storm breaks over the lake, and a flash of lightning shows a gigantic figure behind a tree. He knows at once: it is the creature. He watches it climb the cliffs of the Salève faster than any human could.

At home he learns that the killer has already been found: Justine. The miniature was discovered in her pocket. She had come back too late that night, found the city gates shut, and slept in a barn, and she cannot explain how the picture came to be on her. Soon she confesses.

Victor knows the truth and says nothing. His reasoning: no one would believe him; they would take him for a madman. Before the execution, Justine tells Elizabeth and Victor that her confessor bullied her into confessing, threatening her with excommunication and hell. The next day she is hanged. Later Victor tells Walton that he was not the hand but was, in effect, the true murderer. On that day, all the same, he chose silence.

Act Four · The sea of ice: the one who was left behind speaks

Crushed by guilt, Victor goes alone into the Alps, to the glaciers above Chamonix. At Montanvert, on the Mer de Glace beneath Mont Blanc, a figure comes bounding toward him across the crevasses. Victor curses it and wants to fight. It eludes him easily: you are my creator, you owe me. First, listen.

So in a shepherd's hut on the glacier, by a fire, the novel hands the microphone to the creature. These six chapters sit at the exact centre of the book, and they are its greatest achievement.

Its first memories are a confusion of sensations: light too bright, cold, hunger, berries and stream water in the woods. At a fire left by beggars it burns its hand, then learns that fire also warms. When it walks into a village, people scream and throw stones; it takes refuge in a lean-to built against the back of a cottage, and through a chink in the wall it sees a family.

They are the De Laceys: a blind old father and his grown children, Felix and Agatha, once a family of standing in France, now poor in the German countryside. The creature watches them all winter. When it realises how hungry they are, it stops stealing their food. At night it cuts firewood and leaves it at their door, then listens the next morning as they wonder at it. From this family it learns, for the first time, what gentleness is.

The turning point is the arrival of Safie, daughter of a Turkish merchant whom Felix had once helped escape from a Paris prison, a rescue that cost the De Laceys their fortune and drove them into exile. She has come after Felix against her father's will. She speaks no French, and as Felix teaches her word by word, the creature learns behind the wall. The textbook is a history of the rise and fall of empires, so as the creature learns to speak, it also learns the full rulebook of human society: property, birth, rank, and what a being with none of these counts for in human eyes.

In the woods it finds a leather case holding Plutarch's Lives, Goethe's The Sorrows of Young Werther and Milton's Paradise Lost. It reads Milton as true history, and the comparison grows more painful the further it reads: Adam had a creator who protected him, and a companion. Payoff ① ↑The creature puts it most cruelly: "I ought to be thy Adam; but I am rather the fallen angel" (chapter 10). The three lines on the title page, which we took for decoration, turn out to be a petition this creature has filed on its own behalf. The book opens in its voice; we just have to read halfway in to learn who is asking.

In the pocket of the coat it took from the laboratory it finds Victor's notes, and reads how it was assembled and how much it disgusted the man who made it. In a pool it sees its own reflection, and starts back in terror.

It decides to take one risk: if the blind old man accepts it first, his children will not judge it by its face alone. When the young people are out, it knocks, sits beside the old man and tells him it is an unfortunate creature whom everyone has abandoned. The old man answers kindly. Just as it falls to its knees and seizes his hand, Felix, Agatha and Safie return. Agatha faints, Safie flees, and Felix tears it away from his father and beats it violently with a stick. It could have torn him limb from limb. It does not strike back; it runs.

The family leaves in the night and never returns. That night the creature sets the cottage on fire. From that moment, it says, it declared war on the whole human species, and above all on the one who had made it and thrown it into this condition.

Following Victor's notes, it travels toward Geneva. On the way it pulls a girl out of a fast river, and the man with her, assuming the worst, shoots it in the shoulder. Near Geneva it meets a little boy in the woods and has a thought: a child this young will not yet have learned to judge by looks; I could take him and raise him as my companion. The boy screams the moment he sees it, calls it an ugly monster, and shouts that his papa is M. Frankenstein and will punish it. At the name, the creature takes the boy by the throat.

It takes the miniature from the child's body and gazes at the lovely woman painted there, first softened, then enraged: no such woman would ever smile at it. In a barn it finds a young woman asleep on the straw and slips the miniature into the folds of her dress. That woman is Justine.

The story told, it makes its one demand: make me a female like myself. We will go to the wilds of South America, live on acorns and berries, and never be seen by human beings again. If I am loved by one living being, I will be at peace with all of mankind; if not, I will make you feel every grain of my loneliness. Moved by the argument and frightened by it, Victor agrees.

Act Five · Orkney and Évian: a mate torn apart, a wedding night

He puts it off for a long time, then pleads the need to consult scholars in England and travels with Clerval down the Rhine, to London, and north into Scotland. At last he goes on alone to a barren island in the Orkneys with a handful of fishermen's huts, and begins the second creature.

Halfway through, one night, he stops and reckons: she has promised nothing; she might be far crueller than her mate; she might refuse to go into exile; she might turn in disgust from his ugliness. And even if they loved each other, they would have children, and a whole species of demons would spread across the earth, a curse on every generation to come, and it would be his doing. He looks up and sees, in the moonlight, the face pressed to the window, grinning; it has followed him the whole way. In front of it, Victor tears the nearly finished female body to pieces.

The creature gives a howl of despair and vanishes. Hours later it opens the door and calmly delivers the most frightening sentence in the book: Setup ⑤"I shall be with you on your wedding-night" (chapter 20). Victor understands it to mean: on that night I will kill you. He feels almost relieved. Let it come, then.

That night he rows out to sea, sinks the remains in a basket, and falls asleep in the boat. When he wakes, the wind has driven him to the coast of Ireland. The townspeople march him to the magistrate: last night a young man was found strangled on the beach. It is Clerval.

He lies ill in prison for two months; the magistrate, Mr. Kirwin, writes to his family. A grand jury establishes that he was still in Orkney when the body was found, and he is released. This is the third person dead because of him, and once again he has told no one the truth.

Elizabeth writes to ask whether he loves someone else; if he does, she will release him. He replies that after they are married he will tell her a dreadful secret. After the wedding they cross the lake by boat to Évian. At the inn Victor patrols the corridors with a pistol, waiting for the thing that has promised to kill him, and sends Elizabeth to their room ahead of him.

A scream. He bursts in. Elizabeth lies flung across the bed, her head hanging over the edge, her face half hidden by her hair, the marks of those fingers on her throat. Payoff ⑤ ↑From the first, Victor heard "I shall be with you on your wedding-night" as "I will kill you," and the life he stood guard over in the corridor was his own. The creature never said it would kill him. What it wanted was for him to become, like itself, a being whose mate had been destroyed: he tore my bride apart, so I take his. It said exactly what it meant from the start. Victor's self-absorption is what made him mishear it.

Payoff ③ ↑The dream of Act Two comes true at this moment: he kisses Elizabeth and she turns into a corpse in his arms. At the time we filed it under Gothic atmosphere. Mary Shelley handed Victor the ending two volumes early: from the night his creature opened its eyes, his bride was already bound to death. On the night he made a life, he dreamed of the woman he loved turning into a dead thing.

At the window, the face is grinning, a finger pointing at the corpse. Victor fires; the creature leaps into the lake and swims away. Within days his father, broken by it all, is dead too. Victor finally goes to a magistrate and tells everything. The magistrate listens politely and believes none of it. It is too late.

Act Six · The Arctic: hunter and hunted, neither able to let go

At the graves he swears revenge, and a laugh answers from the darkness. From then on he pursues the creature across the Mediterranean, the Black Sea, the wastes of Tartary and Russia. The creature always stays a little ahead, carving messages into trees and stones, sometimes leaving him a dead hare to eat: mocking him and leading him on, because this chase is the only relationship it still has with its maker. One behind the other, they go out onto the frozen sea, and the story arrives back at Act One.

The crew demand that the moment the ice breaks they turn for home. The dying Victor raises himself and harangues them: you came here to be heroes; will you run home at the first danger? Yet when the ice opens, Walton gives way and orders the ship south. Payoff ② ↑In Act One, Victor asked Walton whether he shared his madness, and we assumed the whole story was told to turn him back. At the decisive moment, the one urging the crew northward is Victor himself. He has told a book's worth of lessons and still not learned them. On his deathbed he urges Walton to look for happiness in a quiet life and give up ambition, even the harmless-looking wish to make a name in science, and then, in the next breath, takes it back: I have failed, but another may succeed.

Victor dies. That night Walton hears a sound from the cabin. Payoff ④ ↑He opens the door and sees a huge form hanging over Victor's body, its face hidden by long, matted hair, one huge hand reaching out. The night in Act Two has been turned around. Then the maker lay in bed and the made thing stood at the bedside, lifting the curtain, reaching out, and the maker fled. Now the maker lies dead and cannot run, and the creature stands beside him still, its hand still outstretched, with no one left to take it.

The creature speaks to Walton one last time. It asks no forgiveness; it hates itself as much as anyone could; with its creator dead, its work is done. It will go to the northernmost point of the earth, build its own funeral pyre and burn itself to ashes, so that no one can learn from its remains how to make another. Then it leaps from the cabin window onto an ice raft beside the ship and the current carries it off into the dark.

If the whole book is to be gathered into a single fuse, it is not the lightning and not the secret formula. The fuse is the step Victor took that November morning, turning and running from the room. He could have stayed and given the newly opened eyes a name, a word. He fled, and every later choice repeats that movement: the creature reaches out and is beaten back; Justine goes to the gallows and he keeps quiet; the creature asks for a mate and he promises, then destroys her; warned of his wedding night, he thinks only of himself. William, Justine, Clerval, Elizabeth, his father: five deaths, all aftershocks of that one flight.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · The eye opens: two years of longing, one second of revulsion

Run it again. Rain, the stub of a candle, an eye opening. Victor tells us he chose the limbs to be proportionate and the features to be beautiful; but the skin barely covers what lies beneath, the watery eyes are almost the colour of their sockets, the lips are straight and black. He runs.

Why does it work? Because the most frightening thing in the scene is not the creature but how fast Victor reacts. We stand inside his point of view and share his disgust, yet we cannot help noticing that the thing on the table has not yet done anything at all. The information is held extremely tight: not one word about how it was brought to life, only an eye and a man turning away. The most famous creation scene in fiction runs less than a page, and its weight falls entirely on the abandonment. Every film since has pointed the camera at switches and lightning. The novel points it at a man's face.

Two · The chink in the wall: one being in the dark, watching a family's light

Run it again. On the far side of the wall, the old man plays the guitar, and his son sets the best of their food before his father and goes without. On this side, a huge being presses against the boards for an entire winter, learning words like "father", "fire", "bread", learning to grieve at someone else's tears.

Why does it work? First, the point of view is inverted: humans become the observed, and through the creature's eyes we see the most ordinary human tenderness as if for the first time. Second, the pace is deliberately slowed; the reader saves up a whole winter of hope alongside the creature, so that when the stick comes down it has weight. Third, the device of blindness: the only person who might accept it is the one who cannot see it. The arrangement is itself a verdict.

Three · The window: a grinning face, twice

Run it again. The hut in Orkney: Victor looks up and the face at the window is grinning. Months later, the inn at Évian: Elizabeth has just died, and there at the window is the same grin, a finger pointed at the bed.

Why does it work? Because it is the same picture twice, and its meaning has flipped completely. The first grin is anticipation, as it watches its bride being made; the second is revenge, as it watches Victor's bride lie dead. Mary Shelley never spells out the symmetry; she simply lets the same composition appear twice. And both times there is a window between them. Victor never once stands in the same room as his creature and talks to it as an equal.

The idea, worked through: making a human, and how the book tells it

First, the "science." The novel has no laboratory switches and no cry of "It's alive!"; those belong to the 1931 film. Victor says only that he could infuse a spark of being into lifeless matter, and refuses to say how. In the 1831 introduction Mary Shelley recalls hearing Byron and Percy talk that summer about galvanism, and thinking that perhaps a corpse might be reanimated. Luigi Galvani had shown that electricity could make a dead frog's leg twitch, and in 1803 his nephew Giovanni Aldini publicly ran current through the body of a hanged man in London, making the face convulse and one eye open.

Follow the logic link by link. (1) Life is a discoverable physical cause rather than a soul: a live debate at the time, and fair enough as a premise. (2) That cause can be applied to dead tissue stitched together: this is what the story needs, and dead tissue undergoes rapid, irreversible cell death; patched-together remains cannot be turned back into a single living organism by any "spark". (3) He makes it huge because small parts are hard to work with: the creature's monstrousness begins as an engineering shortcut. (4) An adult body with an empty mind learns from sensation upward: closer to a thought experiment in the spirit of Locke's blank slate than to neuroscience. This is a novel's premise, not biology. Its value lies not in whether it could be done, but in who is responsible once it is.

Now the book's real core device: the nested narrative. The story passes through three mouths: Walton relays Victor, Victor relays the creature, and the creature relays the De Laceys' history. Two effects follow. First, every layer is unreliable: Victor keeps excusing himself and blaming destiny, and the creature's words reach us filtered twice, through Victor and then Walton, yet remain astonishingly eloquent. Second, the voice everyone in the story refuses to hear is placed at the dead centre of the book. Inside the plot, no one will listen to it; in the structure, everyone has to sit quietly through six chapters of its speech. And at the end Walton sees the creature with his own eyes, which gives the tale an outside witness.

Victor's reasons for destroying the mate also deserve to be taken seriously. Every one of his fears is logically sound: a new being with free will is not bound by a promise someone else made on her behalf. But the same logic applied to the first creature. He learned on the second attempt the caution he should have had on the first, and Elizabeth paid for it.

What it forces you to think about

One · Should you make it a mate?

A being you made has killed your brother and caused an innocent woman's death. Now it sits across from you, tells you a story that moves you, and says: give me a companion and I will leave for good. Do you agree?

First, the strongest case for yes. It does not ask for power. It asks for what anyone would want: a companion who does not recoil from it. It became what it is after being abandoned, beaten and shot; refuse, and you abandon it a second time, and you have already seen what the first time produced. You could even withhold the power to reproduce; Victor never considers it. Above all, you are the only one who can give it this. To make a being capable of needing love and then refuse to meet that need is morally worse than never making it.

The other side is just as strong. You would be trading a risk to every future generation for the promise of a creature that has already killed. She is not a gift; she is a separate person who agreed to nothing, her fate bargained over privately by the two of you on a glacier. What if she is stronger and hates humanity more? You would not be solving a problem but copying it, and you have already seen once that what you make does not grow up the way you planned. Refusing costs the people around you; agreeing might cost everyone not yet born.

Where does the author stand? She has Victor refuse, then makes him pay with Elizabeth's life. Both prices are written as real, and she does not rule between them. Bring it home: someone says, "Just agree to this one thing and I'll never bother you again," and the one thing involves a third person who isn't in the room. By what right do you agree on that person's behalf?

Two · Who is guilty of William's death?

A child is strangled in the woods. The hands belong to the created being; the one who made it and then abandoned it is the child's own brother. The law will try only one of them. Which would you try?

First, the strongest case against Victor. The creature says it plainly: "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend" (chapter 10). What right does anyone have to expect a being that was left alone from its first moment, and stoned wherever it went, to keep human rules? Victor is its parent, and a parent's obligations do not vanish because the child is ugly. He knew it was out there, never looked for it and warned no one; after William's death he watched Justine go to the gallows. If abandonment is not a crime, what is?

The other side: it is not an infant. It has read Paradise Lost; it knows what murder is. Strangling William may have been a flash of rage, but slipping the miniature into a sleeping woman's dress was cold calculation, done only because she was beautiful and would never smile at it. Misery can explain its anger; it cannot explain why it chose a child, a servant and a bride as its targets. Laying everything at the maker's feet insults everyone who has been hurt and has not gone on to hurt others.

Where does the author stand? She gives the creature six whole chapters to plead its case, and at the end has it say, in its own voice, that it hates itself. She makes both of them guilty, and lets each know it. Bring it home: when someone raised in a terrible home grows up to hurt people, would you sentence them more lightly for their childhood?

Three · Should Walton turn back?

You are one step from a place no human has ever reached. The ship is trapped in ice, and the crew want to get home alive. The dying man beside you says the pursuit of knowledge will destroy you, then shouts at the sailors not to be cowards. North or south?

First, the strongest case for north. Everything humanity knows was bought by someone who did not turn back when told to. The crew signed on knowing the voyage was dangerous; the ice may open tomorrow, and the passage may lie just ahead. Victor's tragedy lies not in seeking knowledge but in fleeing what he made; to confuse the two is to condemn everyone's curiosity for one man's dereliction.

The other side: yours is not the only life on board. Victor's whole story says one thing: one man's ambition is always paid for by others. William, Justine, Clerval and Elizabeth took no part in his experiment. Gambling a crew's lives to get your name on a map has the same structure as his gamble.

Where does the author stand? Walton turns back; his letter is full of regret, but he lives to carry the story home to England. She writes neither his remorse nor his relief. Bring it home: that plan of yours that will surely work if you just hold on a little longer: is part of its cost being charged to your family, your team, people who were never asked?

Motifs: what Mary Shelley kept writing about

Mary Shelley returned again and again to a few things. First, the loneliness of the only one of its kind: the creature has no fellow on earth, and in her 1826 novel The Last Man a plague wipes out humanity until the narrator, Lionel Verney, is the last person alive, left alone in an empty Rome. Second, the failed responsibility between parent and child: her 1819 novella Mathilda tells of a father's incestuous love for his daughter, ending in his suicide. Godwin held back the manuscript, and it was not published until 1959.

These obsessions were tangled up with her life: her mother died giving birth to her; her first child, born prematurely when she was seventeen, died within days; by 1822 she had lost two more children, and Percy had drowned off the Italian coast. Frankenstein is the first link in that chain: the first time she put making a life and losing one into the same scene.

Controversy and criticism

Who wrote it? The manuscripts show that Percy Shelley revised and added to the text. Charles E. Robinson, who edited the manuscripts for publication, estimated his contribution at a few thousand words, mostly stylistic polishing. A small number of writers have gone further and claimed that Percy wrote the whole book; mainstream scholarship has not accepted this. The consensus is that the conception, the structure and the great majority of the words are Mary's.

Contrivance: the creature learns fluent French through a crack in a wall and just happens to find three classics in the woods; Victor is blown to Ireland and lands precisely where Clerval's body is found. These are arrangements forced to move the plot along.

Victor's self-pity: he falls into a fever whenever things go wrong, and while he insists on the enormity of his guilt, at every decisive moment he chooses silence. Defenders say this is precisely the point; even so, the long passages of self-pity really do slow the narrative.

Women and the "East": Elizabeth, Justine and Caroline are all meek and blameless, waiting to be protected or sacrificed; Safie's story carries a typical European fantasy of the Orient, the daughter taught by a Christian mother who flees her Muslim father for European freedom. Feminist criticism (Ellen Moers, in 1976, read it as "female Gothic" about the terrors of birth) has given the book another layer, and also raised the question: why, in a novel about giving birth, do women have almost no voice?

The adaptations have buried the book: the 1823 London stage play Presumption already made the creature mute, and James Whale's 1931 film with Boris Karloff fixed the flat head, neck bolts and grunts. So "Frankenstein" has become the monster's name in popular speech (in the novel it has none), and the eloquent being who reads Milton and argues for six chapters is almost forgotten.

Crossovers

  • Developmental psychology · Harlow's monkeys: In 1958 Harry Harlow found that infant monkeys clung to a cloth surrogate mother rather than a wire one that held the bottle: feeding is not the same as raising. The creature never lacks food in the woods; what it lacks is someone to hold it. The difference: the infant monkeys understand nothing, while the creature breaks only after it has understood its rejection.
  • Philosophy · Condillac's statue: In his Treatise on Sensations (1754), Condillac imagines a statue given one sense at a time, to watch ideas grow out of sensation. The opening pages of the creature's tale read like a narrative version: first light and cold, later an "I". The difference: the statue is a calm exercise, while each new sense the creature gains arrives with hunger, pain and fear.

Visiting the blind old man is the creature's attempt to slip past that. The difference: Goffman's subjects usually have some small circle that accepts them; the creature has none.
  • History of science · Asilomar, 1975: When recombinant DNA first appeared, a group of molecular biologists called for a pause on certain experiments, then met at Asilomar to set their own safety rules. Victor tearing up the mate on Orkney is a kind of one-man moratorium. The difference: theirs was public and collective, while Victor keeps everyone in the dark from start to finish.
