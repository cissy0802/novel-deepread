Novels, Deep-Read · Arundhati Roy · No.73

The God of Small Things

Arundhati Roy · 1997 · Booker Prize the same year

中文 →

December 1969, Kerala, the south-western tip of India. The river is high. In the middle of the night a small wooden boat turns over in the current; two seven-year-old twins are pushed to the far bank, and the third child — their nine-year-old cousin, over from England for Christmas — does not come up.
Before dawn a squad of policemen reaches the abandoned house on that far bank. A carpenter is asleep inside, and they wake him with their boots. The twins, hidden in a corner, watch the men take apart, unhurriedly and almost conscientiously, the adult they love most in the world.
The reason all this happens goes back a few thousand years, to a rule about who may be loved, and how, and how much.

This page contains complete spoilers — how Sophie Mol dies, how Velutha dies, the single word Estha says, where Ammu ends up, what the twins do twenty-three years later, and the last word of the book. The novel itself tells you the outcome in its first chapter. What it asks you to follow is never what happened, only how.

In one sentence

A divorced Syrian Christian woman falls in love with an Untouchable carpenter she has known since childhood; her family, her church, the police and the Communist Party close on them together, and within two weeks the man is dead in a lock-up, one of her children has been sent away and the other has stopped growing up — and twenty-three years later the twins come back to the old house and find themselves still standing in that day.

Coordinates

Arundhati Roy was born in 1961 and grew up in Aymanam, a village in Kerala; she trained as an architect in Delhi and wrote screenplays. The God of Small Things was her first novel. It came out in 1997 and won the Booker Prize that autumn. She then did not publish another novel for twenty years, writing instead polemics against nuclear weapons and big dams, on Kashmir and on caste, until The Ministry of Utmost Happiness in 2017.

Its slot in literary history: the post-colonial Indian novel in English that turned caste into a murder case inside a family tragedy. It is often shelved next to Rushdie's Midnight's Children, but it runs in the opposite direction — Rushdie writes a nation; Roy writes a river and an afternoon.

The story

Act I · 1993: back to Ayemenem

June, the monsoon. Rahel, thirty-one, flies home from America to the family house in Ayemenem because she has heard that her twin brother Estha has been "Re-Returned". Twenty-three years ago he was Returned to their father like a parcel; now the father is emigrating to Australia and has posted him back.

They are two-egg twins — they don't look alike — but as children they thought of themselves together as a single Me. Estha no longer speaks. He isn't mute; he is Quiet, a quietness that began at seven and spread until it took up the whole of him: he washes his own clothes, walks for miles, says nothing. Rahel is not in much better shape: expelled from a string of schools, an architecture course in Delhi, an American husband married and divorced, odd jobs in the States. Both are thirty-one, and both look as though a piece is missing.

The house now holds great-aunt Baby Kochamma — past eighty, lipsticked, glued to a satellite television — and an old cook. Grandmother Mammachi is dead, Uncle Chacko has gone to Canada, the family firm, Paradise Pickles & Preserves, is long shut. Their mother Ammu is dead too. She died at thirty-one.

The first chapter lays out every piece of bad news: cousin Sophie Mol drowned one December; someone called Velutha died; Ammu was thrown out and died somewhere else. It even states the book's thesis — "Things can change in a day" (chapter one). The next three hundred pages take that day apart, an inch at a time.

Act II · The Love Laws: how this family got this way

To understand the day you have to understand the family. They are Syrian Christians — an old Keralan church that traces itself to the apostle Thomas: landed, respectable, educated on English lines. They are Christian and they keep caste as strictly as their Hindu neighbours: you are born into a layer and you stay in it, and at the bottom are the Untouchables, whose very shadow is held to pollute.

Grandfather Pappachi was an Imperial Entomologist under the Raj, and the grievance of his life was a moth: he discovered a new species and someone else's name went on it. In retirement he beat his wife with a brass flower vase every night until his son Chacko came home from Oxford and caught the vase in mid-air. After that Pappachi never spoke to her again, but he sat on the verandah every day in a well-pressed suit. The family's first lesson: respectability is worn outside; violence lives indoors.

His daughter Ammu was not allowed to go to college — Pappachi thought education wasted on a girl. To escape the house she married a Bengali assistant manager on a tea estate in Assam, who drank, and who eventually tried to hand her over to his English boss in exchange for keeping his job. Ammu came home with twins. In the family's view a divorced daughter had no place in her parents' house; in the lawyers' phrase she had no locus standi — which the twins, overhearing, file away as "Locusts Stand I". The phrase a child mishears turns out to be the most exact description of a woman's position: she is standing here, and here has no place for her.

Chacko is a Rhodes Scholar, Oxford-educated, a self-declared Marxist, who comes home, takes over his mother's pickle factory and starts calling it "my factory" and her pineapples "my pineapples". He sleeps with women who work there; his mother calls this Men's Needs and quietly slips them money. The same thing done by Ammu would be the family's disgrace.

And then there is Velutha. He is a Paravan, from an Untouchable community whose members, in his grandfather's day, were made to crawl backwards with a broom, sweeping away their own footprints so that no one of higher caste would step in them. Velutha means "white" in Malayalam; he is very dark. He can make anything and fix any machine. Mammachi employs him in the factory and pays him far less than a Touchable carpenter, and considers this a kindness. He is the twins' favourite adult; Rahel likes to find the leaf-shaped birthmark on his back. He is also a card-carrying Communist — Kerala was one of the first places on earth to elect a Communist government.

This is the thing behind the title: the Love Laws, which decide who may be loved, and how, and how much — older than any statute and sturdier than any person. Everyone in this family is caught in that net; some of them are the net, and some are the fish.

Act III · To Cochin: a film, and the Orangedrink Lemondrink Man

December 1969. Chacko's English ex-wife Margaret is bringing their nine-year-old daughter Sophie Mol for Christmas; Margaret's second husband has just been killed in a car crash. The whole family drives to Cochin airport to meet them in Pappachi's old sky-blue Plymouth.

On the way, each adult gives the children a lesson. Chacko gives one on history. History, he says, is like an old house at night, Seed ①the lamps lit, the ancestors whispering inside; we want to go in and listen, but we're locked out, and all we can do is look through the windows at shadows — because we are prisoners of war, our dreams have been doctored, we have fallen in love with our conquerors. The children keep only the name: the History House. There really is such a house, across the river — the ruin where an Englishman who "went native" once lived, and shot himself.

At a level crossing the car is caught in a Communist march. Thousands of workers stream past with red flags, and one of them opens the car door and makes Baby Kochamma wave a little red flag and shout Inquilab Zindabad — long live the revolution. She does it, white to the lips. Seed ②In that moment Rahel spots Velutha in the crowd, in a red scarf, carrying a flag, and calls his name out of the window; Ammu snaps at her to be quiet. Baby Kochamma files all of it away: the public humiliation, and the Paravan she is sure she saw in the march.

They stay the night in Cochin and the children are taken to see The Sound of Music. Estha sings along too loudly, gets shushed by the rows around him, and is sent out into the lobby. Behind the refreshments counter stands a man the children will call the Orangedrink Lemondrink Man. He offers the boy a free drink on condition that the boy hold something for him. The novel is extremely restrained; the reader understands exactly what happened. Afterwards, when the film lets out, the same man chats pleasantly with Ammu and learns that the family has a pickle factory in Ayemenem.

From then on Estha has one thought: he knows where we live; he'll come. He gives himself two rules. Anything can happen to anyone. It's best to be prepared.

That night at the hotel, Rahel says the man seemed nice and then says something cheeky to her mother. Ammu looks at her and says, flatly: do you know what happens when you hurt people? Hurt people, and they start to love you a little less. Seed ③Rahel feels a moth settle on her heart — Pappachi's moth, the one that never bore his name — cold-footed, opening its wings. From then on, whenever Ammu's love seems to shrink a little, the moth comes back.

Act IV · Welcome Home, Our Sophie Mol

Once Sophie Mol arrives the whole house revolves around her, and Baby Kochamma fawns on her while treating the twins as half-Hindu hybrids. Sophie herself is a good child, and soon attaches herself to the twins.

On the day of the welcome, Ammu watches Rahel run to Velutha, watches him toss the child up and catch her, watches him laugh. She remembers being a girl: Velutha used to come to the house with his father and make her little things — tiny windmills, a box, a boat — Seed ④and hold them out on the flat of his palm so that she could take them without touching him. His father had taught him that. Now Ammu looks at the man, sees his shoulders, sees that he is looking back. Two things are in that look at once: she wants him, and she knows what it will cost. Perhaps the cost is part of what she wants: for a woman this family considers to have no place, the sharpest revenge available is to love the one person the family would never allow.

Estha, meanwhile, is busy being prepared. In the long grass by the river the twins find an old wooden boat and carry it to Velutha, who mends it. They will use it to cross to the History House: that is the refuge Estha is fitting out for himself and his sister, in case the Orangedrink Lemondrink Man comes.

Ammu and Velutha meet by the river. For thirteen nights after that, she crosses the yard and goes down to the water, and he is there. They know it has no future, so they agree to stick to Small Things: a spider that lives in a crack in a wall, an ant-bite, a light on the river. The Big Things — caste, family, Party — they never touch. The God of Small Things is enshrined in those thirteen nights: as long as you don't look at the big things, you can live inside the small ones.

Act V · The day: the river, the History House, "Save Ammu"

The informer is Velutha's father, Vellya Paapen, an "Old World" Paravan with a glass eye that Mammachi paid for; he owes the family a debt he can never repay. He comes to the verandah one rainy night, weeping, and tells them everything about his son and Ammu, and offers to kill his son with his own hands.

Mammachi and Baby Kochamma trick Ammu into her bedroom and lock it. Mammachi summons Velutha, abuses him in the filthiest language she has, and throws him out. Velutha goes to the local Party secretary, Comrade Pillai — he has paid his dues, he has marched — and Pillai tells him in Party language that the Party cannot take up a member's lapses of discipline in his private life. Thrown out by his employer and shut out by his comrade: both doors close on him in a single evening.

The twins know only that their mother has been locked in. They go to her door to ask why. From behind it, in despair, Ammu screams at them: it's because of you; if it weren't for you I'd be free; I should have dumped you in an orphanage. Payoff ③ ↑The moth on Rahel's heart opens all its wings. When it first appeared it was attached to a scolding, and a reader takes it for a small sting between mother and daughter; now it returns as the only conclusion a child can draw — we hurt her, so she loves us less, so we should go. A moth has joined a passing remark to a death, and Ammu never knows how deep that remark went.

That night the twins decide to run away to the History House. Sophie Mol insists on coming: if all three children vanish, she argues, the grown-ups will really be sorry. They push out the boat Velutha mended and set off across the river. It has rained; the river is up; in the dark the boat hits something in the water and turns over. The twins are washed to the far bank. Sophie Mol does not come up.

Soaked, the twins find their way into the History House and fall asleep on the back verandah. They don't know that Velutha, thrown out, has also swum the river and is sleeping in the same house.

The same night, Baby Kochamma goes to the police. She tells Inspector Thomas Mathew that a Paravan tried to rape the family's daughter and, when driven off, took revenge by abducting the three children. In her version the family's respectability can still be tidied up. Payoff ② ↑Now the reader sees why the level crossing was there: the little red flag Baby Kochamma waved in that march has been sitting on Velutha's account ever since. At the time it played as comedy — a genteel old lady made to shout slogans — and now it is the emotional first draft of a police complaint. A humiliation suffered in public will look for the lowest person available, the one who can't hit back, to pay it off.

Before dawn a squad of Touchable policemen comes into the History House and finds Velutha asleep. The twins wake and watch from the dark. The beating is not angry. It is methodical, almost conscientious: bones broken, kneecaps smashed, like a procedure being carried out. They don't hate him; they are keeping order, and in that order he never fully counted as a person. Payoff ① ↑Chacko's passing metaphor in the car — history as an old house we can't enter, only peer into at the shadows — has turned into a building. The children have actually walked into the History House and, for the first time, seen what goes on inside. In the car it sounded like an Oxford man musing in his study; here it is cashed out as a broken body. The shadows they had been watching through the window turn out to be this.

Velutha is dragged away and dies in the lock-up that night.

It isn't over. Ammu, let out of her room, hears what has happened and goes to the police station to tell the truth: Velutha raped no one; it was her choice. The Inspector taps her breasts with his baton, calls her a whore and sends her away. But he now understands that the complaint was false, that Baby Kochamma is liable for it, and that a Party member dead in custody could make trouble — except that Comrade Pillai has already let it be known that this man has no Party protection.

Baby Kochamma is frightened. She takes the seven-year-olds aside and tells them: you killed Sophie Mol; you will go to jail, and so will your mother — unless you say that man kidnapped you. She hands two children a choice: save Ammu, or send Ammu to prison. The Inspector takes Estha to look at the unrecognisable thing in the cell and asks him: was it him? Estha says yes.

Act VI · Afterwards, and "Tomorrow"

At Sophie Mol's funeral Rahel is certain her cousin is still alive in the coffin, doing cartwheels. Chacko, half mad with grief and helped along by Baby Kochamma, decides Ammu has ruined everything; he breaks down her door and orders her out of his house. Ammu has no money and no job and cannot keep both children: Estha is Returned to his father in Calcutta, and mother and daughter watch him leave through a train window.

Ammu goes looking for work and loses one job after another. She falls ill; on her last visit to Ayemenem there is a rattle in her chest that will not go away. A few months later she dies alone in a cheap lodge in the coastal town of Alleppey, aged thirty-one — "a viable die-able age", as the novel puts it (chapter one). The church will not bury her; Chacko has her put through an electric crematorium, and Rahel keeps the receipt.

It is 1993. Rahel is thirty-one now.

In the days after her return the twins spend a whole night at the temple by the river watching Kathakali, the classical dance-drama of Kerala. In the early morning, back in the old house, the two thirty-one-year-olds sleep together. The novel does not write it as desire but as grief, held in common: they were cut in half at seven, and now they are fitting themselves back together. They have broken the Love Laws again — this time, one that even forgiving readers flinch at.

Then the novel makes a strange and cruel decision. The last chapter goes back to 1969, to Ammu and Velutha's first night. The river, the mud, a spider. She touches him, he touches her, and at last they touch all of each other. Payoff ④ ↑Remember the little gifts held out on a flat palm? In Act IV that was a stray memory — a boy taught how not to touch a higher-caste girl — and now it is turned inside out: the open hand that existed in order not to touch becomes the hand the whole book ends on. The first thing the Love Laws regulate is who may touch whom, and this book ends with a touch.

As they part, Ammu turns and says one Malayalam word: Naaley — "Tomorrow." It is the last word of the novel. The reader knows they have thirteen tomorrows, and then the History House.

If this story has a fuse, it isn't the capsized boat, and it isn't Baby Kochamma's complaint. The fuse is the free drink in the lobby of a Cochin cinema. A seven-year-old was hurt by a stranger and could tell no one, so he got himself prepared: hence the boat, hence the hideout across the river, hence the running away. Sophie Mol came along; the boat turned over; Velutha happened to be sleeping in that house — and the Love Laws only ever needed a pretext. The boat gave them one. One adult's casual malice, noticed by no one, grew quietly inside a child for two weeks and then pulled three lives down with it.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · The lobby of Abhilash Talkies: a grown-up crime in a child's grammar

Replay: on screen the nuns are singing; Estha stands up and sings along, too loud; people shush him and he is sent out. The lobby is empty except for the man behind the drinks counter. He beckons the boy over and asks what he'd like — orange or lemon. Then a hand, a demand, a cold bottle. Estha goes back into the auditorium and says nothing.

Its power comes from three places. First, the point of view is locked at a seven-year-old's height. The novel never uses an adult word for it — no "molest", no "abuse" — only what a child can see and feel: the counter, the cold of the bottle, the stickiness on a hand. The reader knows more than the child, so the reader has to supply the word. The horror is completed inside the reader's head, not on the page. Second, naming is the child's defence: the man has no name, only a fairy-tale epithet, and a child turns something he cannot digest into a sign he can say out loud. Third, the blade is the scene that follows: when the film ends the man chats warmly with Ammu, and Ammu thinks he seems nice. The hurt child stands beside his mother and watches her smile at the man who hurt him. There isn't a line of psychology; it's all in where people are standing.

Two · Kathakali in 1993: they recognise the violence

Replay: the twins sit in a temple courtyard watching all-night Kathakali. By day the dancers perform truncated versions for tourists at a "heritage" hotel — built, it turns out, on the History House — and at night they come back to the temple and perform the whole thing, as if in penance. The first half of the night is Karna from the Mahabharata: a child his mother set adrift on a river, whom she seeks out years later only to plead for her other sons. The second half is Bhima tearing open Dushasana and using his blood to make good Draupadi's vow.

The power lies in where the novel puts this scene: before the night of the police. At this point the reader doesn't yet know what happened in the History House and only sees the twins go strangely still at Bhima's violence — they recognise it, the book says; they have seen it before. When the truth unfolds later, the reader looks back and realises that the dance had already staged that night, in mythological costume. Karna isn't filler either: a child cast out by his mother — the myth says for the twins what they cannot say. And the cut-down version is played for tourists in that very house: one location stacks myth, empire, violence and the tourist trade on top of each other.

Three · The last chapter: ending a funeral on "Tomorrow"

Replay: a night after rain, the river. Ammu comes down the steps; Velutha comes up out of the water. Nothing is said. She touches him; he takes her down into the mud of the bank. Afterwards they lie watching a spider and talk about small things. As they part she turns back and says: tomorrow.

The chapter's force lies in where it has been placed. In chronological order it would sit in the middle of the story, and we'd read it with the suspense of "will they be caught?" Roy puts it at the very end, after we have seen the History House and the lodge in Alleppey. So every gesture is itself and its outcome at once: he touches her hand and you see that hand under a boot; she says "tomorrow" and you know what tomorrow is. It is the oldest trick in Greek tragedy — the audience knows the ending and the characters don't. Roy uses it on a love scene and turns the love scene into a eulogy. She chooses not to end on death but on "tomorrow", and it is the only time in the book these two win.

The book's central device

One · Ending first, causes after: a tragedy that runs backwards

The book is built like a jar smashed and then carefully reassembled: two timelines (two weeks in 1969, a few days in 1993) interleaved, all three deaths announced in the first chapter. It deliberately gives up the suspense of "what happened" in exchange for the weight of "how could it". Because we know Sophie Mol will drown, every second of her waving sweetly at the airport hurts; because we know Velutha will die, the boat he mends turns into a weapon in his hands.

The structure is also an argument. If a catastrophe is made of small things that happened to lock together, the only honest way to tell it is to lay out every small thing and let the reader watch them engage. Big History — caste, empire, Communism — never appears head-on in this book; it only works through small things: a little red flag, a mended boat, a sentence shouted through a door. That is what the title means. The Big God handles history; the God of Small Things handles each person's one day — and the Big God only ever kills through the small one.

Two · A child's language: capitals, compounds, reading backwards

Roy's English is the most argued-over and most recognisable part of the book. She writes with the twins' ears: grown-up words are misheard, split, capitalised — "Locusts Stand I", "Orangedrink Lemondrink Man", "Love Laws"; the twins read sentences backwards and drive their teacher mad; words are run together the way a child says them in one breath.

This is more than a style. Capitalisation is how a child turns an incomprehensible adult world into proper nouns — once a thing has a name it can be moved, feared, remembered. The Love Laws are capitalised the same way: a set of rules nobody says out loud, given a name by a child and thereby made visible. The whole language of the book is doing a single job: turning invisible rules into visible things. One detail from the pickle factory can serve as a footnote to the whole: their banana jam is banned by the food authorities because it is neither a jam nor a jelly. In a society that lives by categories, being unclassifiable is itself an offence.

What it makes you think about

One · Should Ammu have loved Velutha — when the cost doesn't fall on her?

The novel is plainly on the lovers' side: their thirteen nights are the tenderest thing in it, and the rules that kill them are the ugliest.

First, the case against, at full strength. Ammu knows the rules and knows what will be done to Velutha if they are found out. And the risks the two of them carry are utterly unequal: if she is caught, the worst that happens is she's thrown out of the house; if he is caught, he dies. Someone with a way out invites someone with none to share her danger — how much of that is love, and how much is revenge on her family? Defying an unjust rule is right; letting the weakest person pay for your defiance is something else.

The other side is just as hard. The language of "unequal risk" is itself the logic of the Love Laws: it prices one life below another and then tells the cheaper one to be careful. Velutha is a grown man and he chose too; deciding on his behalf that, for his own good, he should not be loved is exactly what caste has done to him all his life. As for the children — if a mother must never love anyone, for their sake, the rules have taken the children hostage as well. What kills Velutha is not Ammu's love but the people who hear about it.

Bring it home: a relationship, or an act of defiance, that is courage for you and everything they own for someone else — did you ask, at the time, which of you could least afford it? And the reverse: have you ever told someone, for their own good, not to love an "unsuitable" person?

Two · Estha's "yes" — if you must choose between two betrayals, are you guilty?

Seven-year-old Estha is told: say yes and your mother won't go to prison; refuse, and she goes, and so do you. He says yes, and identifies the adult he loves best. Then he punishes himself with twenty-three years of silence.

First, the case that he is not guilty, at full strength. The trap was set by adults from start to finish: Baby Kochamma lied, the Inspector knew it was a lie, Pillai withdrew protection, Velutha was nearly dead before the boy opened his mouth. A seven-year-old who can neither understand the situation nor refuse it — any answer he gives has been extracted from him. Moral responsibility requires freedom, and this child had none. To make him carry it for life is to load the adults' crime onto him a second time.

But the book doesn't let him off. Estha's silence isn't there because he is "really guilty"; it's there because he knows the word came out of his mouth. It is an old problem: a driver hits a child who ran into the road — legally blameless, and for the rest of his life he will feel he hit a child. That kind of guilt is illogical, and it may also be the evidence that a conscience is still working — the person who can say "not my fault" with perfect calm is the more frightening one. The person in this novel who actually is guilty — Baby Kochamma — lives into her eighties, lipsticked, in front of the television, and never loses a night's sleep.

Bring it home: inside some system, made to pick between two wrong options — whose name goes on the redundancy list, which sentence to say on the stand — do you tell yourself afterwards "I had no choice", or do you keep remembering that it was your hand?

Three · If the Love Laws are all wrong, what about the twins' night?

The whole book indicts the Love Laws — caste, class and gender deciding who may love whom — as arbitrary, cruel and murderous. Then, just before the end, it has the twins sleep together, written in the same tender register.

First, the case that this is a test of the reader's double standard, at full strength. Roy clearly means it. Reading Ammu and Velutha, we are on the lovers' side and the rule looks barbaric; reading the twins, we instinctively step back. And that stepping back feels, from the inside, exactly like what Ayemenem feels on hearing that Ammu has been with a Paravan: disgust, reflex, no reasons needed. The novel forces you to admit that you too have a Love Law you take for granted, and that it operates in you the way caste operates in Mammachi. Your taboo may have better reasons, but the way you feel it is no more refined than hers.

The other side: not every line is the same kind of line. The caste ban protects a hierarchy, and its victims are the ones held down; the taboo between siblings largely protects the most vulnerable people inside a family from being used by those with more power. Equating the two amounts to saying all norms are mere prejudice — and then nothing is left with which to indict Baby Kochamma, who was also just enforcing a rule. A novel that accuses injustice needs to believe that some rules are right. Besides, that night is written not as liberation but as grief: what they look for in each other isn't a lover, it's the seven-year-old that was cut away.

Bring it home: is there something in you that says "that's just not allowed", and you've never asked why? Try to find the reason. If you find one, it's ethics. If you can't, you may be standing closer to Mammachi than you think.

Motifs: what Roy keeps writing about

One: how big things kill through small things. Twenty years later, The Ministry of Utmost Happiness (2017) changes the stage: a Delhi graveyard where Anjum, a hijra, opens a guesthouse among the graves, and from there out to Kashmir. The stage is bigger; the method is the same: the state, religious violence and the army always land on a particular body. The essays of the twenty years between — "The End of Imagination" against India's 1998 nuclear tests, "The Greater Common Good" on the people displaced by the Narmada dams — say the same thing: "the bigger picture" is most often used to hide who is being sacrificed.

Two: caste. She later wrote a long introduction, The Doctor and the Saint (2014), to a new edition of B. R. Ambedkar's Annihilation of Caste — Ambedkar, born an Untouchable, was the principal architect of India's constitution. From Velutha to that essay the question is the same: when a society talks about progress, who is still left out of the picture?

Three: the mother. Ammu carries something of Roy's own mother, Mary Roy: a divorced Syrian Christian woman who came back to Kerala with two children and went on to found a school. In 1986 Mary Roy won a case in India's Supreme Court that gave Syrian Christian women in Kerala inheritance rights equal to their brothers' — in other words, the woman with no locus standi went to court in real life, and won. Roy's 2025 memoir, Mother Mary Comes to Me, is about her, and about a relationship far more complicated than the one in the novel.

Controversy and criticism

The style: genius or decoration? The argument began on publication day. Admirers say she put a child's ear and the rhythms of Malayalam inside English; detractors say the capitals, compounds and refrains harden into a manner with repetition — too cute by half, and sometimes doing the reader's feeling for them. The Booker year itself was divided: the publisher Carmen Callil, a former chair of the Booker judges, called the book execrable on television. The criticism has a point: in the second half some passages are carried by rhetoric rather than by scene.

Is Velutha a person or an icon? This is the sharpest criticism, and it comes from people who care about caste. Velutha is nearly perfect — skilled, gentle, upright, beautiful, crowned the God of Small Things — and his inner life is almost closed; we see him only through Ammu's and the children's eyes. A novel that condemns caste still tells the tragedy of an upper-caste family, and the Untouchable's death serves as the occasion for the suffering of an upper-caste woman and her children. He is the one who is loved and the one who is killed, but seldom a subject with plans of his own. In fairness, the book is aware that this being-looked-at is part of his condition; awareness is not the same as correction.

The politics. Comrade Pillai's opportunism and Chacko's drawing-room Marxism caused offence in Kerala; E. M. S. Namboodiripad, the state's first Communist chief minister, publicly criticised the book's portrayal of the Party.

The obscenity case. After publication a lawyer in Kerala filed a case claiming that the sex in the final chapter corrupted public morals; it dragged on for years. As for the twins' night, it remains for many readers the hardest chapter to accept. As discussed above it is deliberate — but deliberate is not the same as successful: some argue that the chapter aestheticises an injury, and that objection is not unreasonable.

The author. Roy has since become one of India's most contested public intellectuals, a long-standing critic of its nuclear policy, its big-dam projects and its policy in Kashmir. In 2002 she was convicted of contempt of court by the Supreme Court and given a symbolic one-day jail term and a fine. In 2024 Delhi's Lieutenant Governor sanctioned her prosecution under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act over remarks about Kashmir she made in 2010; the same year she received English PEN's PEN Pinter Prize. These are verifiable facts, and they are a separate matter from whether The God of Small Things succeeds.

Ten lines to take away

① The book tells you in chapter one who dies; what it asks you to follow is the small things that made the ending inevitable.

② The Love Laws decide who may be loved, and how, and how much — never written down, and therefore impossible to repeal.

③ The fuse is a free drink in a cinema lobby: one adult's malice grew inside a child for two weeks and then pulled three lives down.

④ "Locusts Stand I": the legal phrase a child mishears is the most exact account of a divorced woman's position.

⑤ The little red flag Baby Kochamma waved in the march ended up on a Paravan's account — public humiliation always goes looking for the lowest person to pay it off.

⑥ The police who beat Velutha are not angry, only diligent; the worst violence is the kind its agents think of as doing their job.

⑦ Hurt people and they will love you a little less — a passing reproach that settled on a child's heart as a moth.

⑧ Estha's silence says: the word came out of my mouth. The one who was actually guilty spent twenty years in lipstick in front of the television.

⑨ The Big God handles history and the God of Small Things handles each person's day — and the Big God only ever kills through the small one.

⑩ The palm held flat in order not to touch becomes the one touch the book allows; its last word is "Tomorrow", and the reader knows what tomorrow is.
