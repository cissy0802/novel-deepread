Novels, Deep-Read · Lanling Xiaoxiaosheng · No.104

The Plum in the Golden Vase

Jin Ping Mei · also known as The Golden Lotus · Lanling Xiaoxiaosheng · late Ming, c. 1590s · 100 chapters

中文 →

Purple Stone Street, Qinghe County, a spring afternoon.
A woman upstairs is taking in the door-blind when the pole slips from her hand and drops straight onto the cap of a man walking past.
He looks up ready to curse, and the curse dies in his mouth. She offers an apologetic smile; he returns a bow, and turns into Dame Wang's teahouse next door.
In The Water Margin that pole costs several lives. In this book it costs a whole household, and everyone in it.

This piece contains complete spoilers: how Wu the Elder dies and why his brother's blade takes years to fall; how Li Ping'er's baby is frightened to death by a cat; in which chapter Ximen Qing dies, and of what; where every woman in his compound ends up afterwards — who becomes a lady and who is sold to a brothel; and why the last child of the house is led away by a monk. Everything this book means lives in its final twenty-one chapters. Stop before them and you have not read it.

In one sentence

A newly rich apothecary in a county town buys women, office and other people's lives, gathers six wives and a crowd of lovers into one compound, and dies of his own appetites at thirty-three; the moment he is gone, a house that looked unbreakable comes apart in twenty-one chapters — this is not the story of a bad man getting his due, but of an entire way of living in which everything has a price, shown from the inside as it runs and as it collapses.

Where it stands

The author signed himself "Lanling Xiaoxiaosheng," roughly "the Scoffing Scholar of Lanling," and nobody has ever established who he was. Wang Shizhen, Li Kaixian, Tu Long — dozens of candidates have been proposed and none proven. The book was written during the Wanli reign of the Ming; manuscripts were circulating among literati by the 1590s, when the writer Yuan Hongdao read the first half and wrote to a friend that its pages were "filled with clouds and mist," a phrase of high praise. The earliest surviving printed edition, the Jin Ping Mei cihua ("with songs and verses"), carries a preface dated 1617. A revised edition followed in the Chongzhen years, and in the early Qing the critic Zhang Zhupo annotated it chapter by chapter and called it "the first marvelous book." This piece follows the cihua text and flags divergences.

Its place in literary history is unusually clear. Chinese long fiction before it told of history (Three Kingdoms), heroes (The Water Margin) or gods and demons (Journey to the West). The Plum in the Golden Vase was the first to hand an entire long novel over to the daily life of one household — what they ate, what they wore, how much silver they borrowed, who quarrelled with whom. Lu Xun classed it as a "novel of manners" and judged that no fiction of its time could match its grasp of how people actually behave. A century and a half later, the first commentator on Dream of the Red Chamber remarked that Cao Xueqin had learned this book's deepest secrets.

The Story

Act I · The road that forks off The Water Margin

Anyone who knows The Water Margin will recognize the opening chapters: Wu the Elder, a dwarfish seller of steamed buns; his beautiful wife Pan Jinlian; his brother Wu Song, the tiger-killer, now a constable in the county. The pole drops, Dame Wang lays her plans, and the two become lovers in her teahouse. When Wu the Elder tries to catch them, Ximen Qing kicks him in the chest; he takes to his bed, and Jinlian poisons him with arsenic.

Up to this point the two books are the same. They part when Wu Song comes home. In The Water Margin he kills his sister-in-law and her lover, and the account is closed in a few pages. Here the author makes the decisive change: Wu Song misses. He learns that Ximen is drinking at the Lion Tower, climbs the stairs with a blade, and finds that Ximen has just stepped away and gone out of the back window. Wu Song mistakes a county runner named Li Waizhuan, sitting at the same table, for an accomplice and throws him from the window to his death. Setup ① Ximen has already paid off the magistrate; it is Wu Song who becomes the killer, and he is branded and exiled to Mengzhou.

At first it reads simply as frustration: the hero failed. But the author is deliberately leaving that blade in the air. He needs Ximen to live, long enough to act out his whole way of life. And so Pan Jinlian walks through his gate as his fifth wife.

Only now do we see the man clearly. Ximen Qing is not the brief villain of The Water Margin. He inherited an apothecary shop from his father; he has little learning, some skill with his fists, a taste for gambling, and above all a gift for dealing with officials. He "settles matters" in county lawsuits, which is to say he takes money to make them go away. Around him hovers a ring of hangers-on — men with no trade who live by keeping rich men company, running errands and supplying ideas — led by Ying Bojue, who can say what Ximen wants to hear before Ximen has opened his mouth. Ten of them swear brotherhood. Ximen is the eldest brother, because he pays.

Act II · One courtyard, six women

When Jinlian arrives there are already four women in the house. The principal wife, Wu Yueniang, a second marriage for him, comes from a military officer's family; she is proper, devout, and keeps the keys. The Second Lady, Li Jiao'er, was bought out of a brothel. The Third, Meng Yulou, is a cloth merchant's wealthy widow who came with two great canopied Nanjing beds and a large dowry — the matchmaker's argument to her was purely practical: this family has money and pull. The Fourth, Sun Xue'e, was once a maid; she runs the kitchen and stands lowest of all. Jinlian is Fifth.

Her history is the key to her. A tailor's daughter, she was sold at nine to a noble household to be trained in music, sold again at fifteen to a rich old man, and, when his wife would not tolerate her, handed for nothing to Wu the Elder, the shortest and ugliest man in town, so the old man could keep visiting. She had never once chosen anything in her life, until she chose the arsenic. Inside Ximen's house she knows exactly what she wants: the man in her room rather than anyone else's. She is beautiful, literate, plays the pipa, and has a tongue like a blade; she knows how one sentence can set two people against each other and how one small incident can drag a rival down. She makes her maid, Pang Chunmei, her confidante — clever, proud, contemptuous of everyone, and loyal to her alone. The title takes one character from each of three women's names: the Jin of Pan Jinlian, the Ping of Li Ping'er, the Mei of Chunmei.

The first blood this courtyard draws is Song Huilian's. She is the wife of a household servant named Laiwang: pretty, deft with her hands, famous for stewing a whole pig's head to tenderness over a single long stick of firewood. Ximen wants her, and she goes along; she gets clothes and trinkets, lords it over the other servants, and even gets the better of Jinlian on the matter of whose feet are smaller. When Laiwang, drunk, talks of killing his master, the remark reaches Jinlian, who repeats it into Ximen's ear, and Ximen sets a trap: Laiwang is framed for a night attack with a knife, beaten at the yamen, and exiled to Xuzhou. Only then does Huilian understand that she thought she was striking a bargain with her master, when she was merely one of his possessions, as was her husband. She hangs herself, is cut down, and hangs herself again. When her old father goes to court, the officials Ximen has paid have him beaten, and before long he too is dead.

It takes only a few chapters, but it lays out the book's whole ledger. In Ximen Qing's world, people, officials and the law all have prices, and he is the one who quotes them. A life buys a favour at the yamen; a servant woman's dignity is worth a few bolts of satin.

Act III · The woman on the other side of the wall

One of his sworn brothers, Hua Zixu, lives next door, with only a wall between the two compounds. He is the nephew of a palace eunuch, has inherited a fortune, and spends his days in the pleasure quarter. His wife, Li Ping'er, has a strange past: she was once a concubine in the household of a high official in Daming Prefecture, and on the night the outlaws of Liangshan stormed the city she escaped with a bundle of jewels, later marrying Hua.

Ximen and Ping'er become lovers across the wall. When Hua is jailed in an inheritance suit brought by his own brothers, Ping'er asks Ximen to intercede, and by night hands chest after chest of silver, incense and valuables over the wall to him — in effect moving the family fortune into his house in advance. Hua comes out of jail to an empty home, falls ill with rage, and soon dies.

The wedding ought to come next. Instead, Ximen's in-law Chen Hong — whose son Chen Jingji has married Ximen's daughter — is caught up in a great scandal in the capital, and Ximen, terrified, bars his gates and drops the marriage. Ping'er, waiting in vain and ill, marries the doctor treating her, Jiang Zhushan. When Ximen recovers his nerve he hires two street thugs to extort, beat and sue the doctor, and within days Jiang is ruined. Ping'er throws him out and has herself carried into Ximen's house as the Sixth Lady.

For three nights he will not enter her room. She tries to hang herself and is saved by a maid. He comes in with a horsewhip, has her undress and kneel, strikes her, and asks how he compares with Jiang Zhushan. Weeping, she gives him the answer he wants, and from then on they are inseparable. This woman, broken in by a whip, becomes the only person in the book Ximen Qing truly loves — and the author explains that to no one. He just sets it down.

Her wealth lifts the household to a new height. She bears a son, Guan'ge. Almost the same day, the men Ximen sent to the capital with birthday gifts for the Grand Preceptor Cai Jing, the most powerful minister in the empire, return carrying an appointment: Ximen is made a deputy battalion commander in the judicial office of Shandong — an apothecary now sits in judgment over a whole region's criminal cases. The chapter title pairs the two events: a son is born and an office is gained. Heir and official cap arrive together, as though heaven were handing out prizes.

Shortly before this, a physiognomist called Immortal Wu visits the house and reads everyone's face in turn. Setup ② He tells Ximen that his fortune is at its height but he should take care of his body; he tells Ping'er she will bear a noble son but carries the root of an illness; and when he comes to the maid Chunmei, he says this girl will marry a great man, bear a son and wear a pearl headdress. Yueniang afterwards is doubtful, roughly: a maid, in a pearl headdress? Everybody laughs and moves on. In a book crowded with fortune-tellers, itinerant quacks and chanting priests, the scene looks like one more bit of noise.

Act IV · A cat called Snow Lion

The moment Guan'ge is born, Jinlian has lost. Ping'er is rich, gentle and has a son, and Ximen goes to her room every night. Jinlian scolds the maids, beats the dog, insults people by indirection, and deliberately sets the dog howling to startle the sickly baby next door, who goes into convulsions at any fright.

Jinlian keeps a pure white cat called Snow Lion. Noticing that Guan'ge often wears a little red tunic, she wraps meat in red silk and trains the cat, again and again, to pounce on it. One day Ping'er sets the baby down to play, and Snow Lion leaps from a table onto him, clawing, and he screams without stopping. From then on the fits never cease; doctors, diviners, incense, vows — nothing helps, and within days he is dead, not yet two. In a rage Ximen dashes the cat to death on the stone steps. Jinlian, in her own rooms, goes on making pointed remarks loud enough to be heard next door, along the lines of "well, that's settled then."

No one accuses her to her face, and the author passes no sentence. He shows Ping'er weeping over the baby's clothes; shows the bleeding she has long suffered from turning unstoppable; shows her dreaming that her dead husband Hua Zixu has come to claim her. Even as she sickens she gives her clothes and jewels away piece by piece — to her maids, to the wet-nurse, to Yueniang — arranging a future for everyone around her, and warns Yueniang to be on her guard against someone. To the end she never says the name.

Ping'er dies. Ximen's response is the least Ximen-like passage in the book: he howls with grief, refuses food for days, has a painter make her portrait to hang before the coffin, and gives her a funeral grander than any principal wife's. Ying Bojue comes in to tell him that if he ruins his health, the whole household will have no one to lean on; only then does he take a mouthful of rice. Payoff ② ↑ Immortal Wu's words begin to come true one by one: the noble son was born, and the illness came, and neither could be escaped. The face-reading chapter was written as a lively bit of business precisely so you would not take it seriously; by the time you remember it, the debts are already being collected.

But Ximen's grief has a term. Before long he has taken up with Ping'er's wet-nurse, on the grounds that something of Ping'er still clings to her.

Act V · Three pills

After Ping'er's death Ximen climbs higher than ever. He travels to the capital himself with loads of gold and silver for Cai Jing's birthday and kneels to adopt the Grand Preceptor as his "godfather"; on his return he is promoted to full commander, toasts inspectors, censors and a top-ranked scholar as equals, and throws banquets for them at home. He trades in salt certificates — government permits to sell salt, a licence to make the richest profits there were — opens a satin shop and a pawnshop, and sends agents to Yangzhou. A servant named Miao Qing murders his own master and pays Ximen a thousand taels of silver, and the case disappears; when a censor submits a memorial against Ximen, one word from Cai Jing makes it go away.

Nor is he simply cruel. When his poor sworn brother Chang Shijie cannot pay his rent, Ximen hands him the silver for a house without a second thought. When the abbot of the Yongfu Temple comes collecting for repairs, he pledges five hundred taels on the spot, and tells Yueniang, in effect, that as long as he gives generously to good causes, he could ravish every goddess in heaven and it would not dent his towering fortune. He sincerely believes that money can buy back any sin — that is his deepest faith about the world.

Earlier, while seeing off a superior at that same Yongfu Temple outside the city, he had met a foreign monk of strange appearance, sitting rapt in meditation. Setup ③ Ximen invited him home for a meal, and on leaving the monk gave him a packet of aphrodisiac pills, warning that he must take only one at a time and never more, and adding some cryptic words of caution. Ximen treated them like treasure. At that point they look like one more marvel, a toy that lets the bedroom scenes grow more unbridled.

His lovers multiply: Wang Liu'er, wife of his business agent Han Daoguo, whose husband knows and keeps watch for them because it pays; and Lady Lin, widow of the same noble household where Jinlian was sold at nine to learn singing. The house that made Jinlian has become a place where her husband goes to cheat on her — the book touches this once, lightly, and adds not a word.

One night in the first month of the year, Ximen comes home dead drunk from Wang Liu'er's, goes into Jinlian's room and falls asleep. Unwilling to let the night go, she finds the packet in his sleeve. Payoff ③ ↑ The monk said one at a time; she feeds him three. He collapses that night, and for days afterwards he bleeds, while doctor after doctor talks more confidently and helps less. What came from the Yongfu Temple was not a gift but a deferred death sentence: the author put the weapon in his hand in chapter 49 and waited thirty chapters to let someone use it. Ximen Qing dies at thirty-three. On the day he breathes his last, Yueniang gives birth to his posthumous son, Xiaoge.

Act VI · When the tree falls, the monkeys scatter

The twenty-one chapters that follow are where the book becomes truly frightening. Nothing large happens. People simply leave, one at a time.

Ximen is barely cold when Li Jiao'er steals what she can in the confusion, goes back to the brothel, and marries another rich man of the county, Zhang Erguan. Ying Bojue is the first to change masters, settling in as Zhang's hanger-on and advising him that the Fifth Lady over at the Ximen house is a beauty worth acquiring. The first useful thing said by the man who flattered Ximen all his life, once Ximen was dead, was how to divide up his women.

Jinlian and her son-in-law Chen Jingji have been trading glances for years; now no one stops them, and Chunmei keeps watch. When the affair is discovered, Yueniang sells Chunmei first. She is forbidden to take a single garment or ornament. The women of her quarters all weep; she sheds not a tear and walks out without looking back, sold as concubine to Commander Zhou of the local garrison. Then it is Jinlian's turn: Yueniang calls in Dame Wang to take her away and sell her.

Just then an amnesty is proclaimed, and Wu Song comes home from exile. Payoff ① ↑ Hearing that Dame Wang has a widow for sale, he offers a hundred taels to marry her — and Jinlian is genuinely happy: all these years she has remembered the tiger-killing brother-in-law. That night, before his dead brother's spirit tablet and in front of his niece, Wu Song kills Jinlian, then Dame Wang, and flees to join the outlaws of Liangshan. The blade raised at the Lion Tower has hung for nearly eighty chapters. Ximen was let go so that he could live his whole life; now the blade falls on Jinlian, and Ximen has long since died at her hands. Wu Song avenges a man whose killer has already been killed by someone else. The blow finally lands — on a ruin.

Jinlian's body lies in the street for days with no one to claim it. It is Chunmei — by now she has borne Commander Zhou a son, been raised to principal wife, and become a lady — who sends men to bury her, behind the Yongfu Temple outside the city. That Qingming festival, Yueniang takes Xiaoge to sweep the graves, wanders by chance into the Yongfu Temple, and comes face to face with Chunmei, there to mourn: the maid she once drove out empty-handed, arriving now in a great sedan chair with attendants front and back, glittering with pearls and kingfisher feathers. It is Chunmei who bows first to her former mistress. Payoff ② ↑ Immortal Wu's prediction, the one Yueniang laughed at — a great husband, a son, a pearl headdress — has come true, and come true before Yueniang's eyes. Later Chunmei makes a point of revisiting the old Ximen house, where the garden is overgrown, the pavilions have fallen, and dust lies thick in the rooms upstairs where Jinlian used to live.

The others' endings fall one after another. Meng Yulou marries Li Yanei, the magistrate's son, one of the very few who come to a good end; she was the one who best knew not to compete, and best knew when to leave. Sun Xue'e elopes, is caught and sold by the authorities — bought, of all people, by Chunmei, who makes her a kitchen drudge and humiliates her, then sells her to a brothel, where at last she hangs herself. Chen Jingji squanders everything, becomes a beggar, then a Taoist novice, is taken into the commander's household by Chunmei as her "cousin" so they can carry on an affair, and is finally killed by one of the household's soldiers. Chunmei herself, insatiable, dies young.

At the end the Jurchen armies sweep south and the Northern Song is falling. Yueniang flees with the grown boy Xiaoge and her household. Setup ④ Years earlier, on a pilgrimage to Mount Tai, she had nearly been taken by force, escaped by night into a snow cave, and been sheltered there by an old monk named Pujing, who asked for her son as his disciple; shaken and barely listening, she had half agreed. Payoff ④ ↑ Now, lodging on the road at the Yongfu Temple, she meets that old monk again. In the night she watches the restless dead of the household come one by one to be released and reborn — Ximen, Jinlian, Ping'er, Chunmei, Chen Jingji — and in a dream she sees Xiaoge turn over and become Ximen Qing, a cangue around his neck. The child is Ximen Qing himself, reborn. The monk tells her that the boy has come to squander the family into ruin, unless he leaves the world to work off his father's karma. Weeping, Yueniang gives up her son. Xiaoge's head is shaved, he takes the Buddhist name Mingwu, and he goes away with the monk.

Yueniang returns to Qinghe, adopts the servant Daian as her son under the name Ximen An, gives him the family estate, and lives peacefully to seventy. The last flicker of the Ximen line falls to the boy who once led his master's horse, ran his errands and carried his love letters.

The fuse

The fuse is not the blind-pole and not the pills, but Ximen Qing's faith in money. He believed everything had a price, so he treated women, offices, lives, even merit in the next world, as goods to buy and sell — and that trade held only for as long as the buyer was alive. While he lived, money was a net that held wives, agents, hangers-on and officials together; when he died it became a heap of loose threads, and each person followed the same logic he had taught them — go with whoever bids highest — and scattered. That is why the last twenty-one chapters are not an epilogue but a proof: this house was never held up by loyalty or love. It was only a flow of silver.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion 1 · Playing the pipa on a snowy night

The winter Ping'er first comes into favour. On a snowy night Jinlian bathes and dresses early, lights the lamp, keeps her clothes on, and waits for Ximen. Again and again she thinks she hears the gate and sends a maid to look; the maid comes back: only the wind. Again she thinks it is his footstep; it is a branch snapping under snow. Past midnight she takes up her pipa and sings a song of grievance under her breath, a verse, a pause to listen, another verse. At last the maid brings word: the master went to bed long ago, in the Sixth Lady's room.

Why it works: Jinlian does nothing wicked in this chapter, and the author gives the camera to her at her softest. For dozens of chapters we watch her scheme, provoke and curse; only on this night are we shut in her room with her, listening to the wind, the snow, the gate that does not open. The instrument here is sound. Each moment she mistakes snow for a footstep, hope is pressed down and springs up again — tighten, release, tighten, release — until the final message presses it flat for good. The scene does not plead for her. But it lets you understand what kind of night that cat was later launched from.

Slow motion 2 · The portrait before the coffin

Ping'er dies in the night. Heedless of the taboo against touching the dead, Ximen holds her body, presses his face to hers and calls to her until his voice is gone. At daybreak he orders the finest coffin and sends for a painter to make a half-length portrait from her dead face, and then a full-length one; when the painter finishes he says the eyes are not right, and has it corrected, and corrected again, until he nods. He keeps vigil before the coffin and has a portion of every meal set out in front of the picture.

Meanwhile the author keeps cutting away: Yueniang thinks he is overdoing it, Jinlian curls her lip behind him, the steward tallies the silver for white cloth, monks and musicians. Why it works: a man's truest grief is set down in a room full of people doing accounts, and so it is both real and utterly alone. This is the moment Ximen is most human. Yet the author will not let his grief change anything — he is as greedy as before, and soon after another woman. Lu Xun observed that the book often "writes both sides at once," and this chapter is the specimen: the weeping and the abacus sound together, and you cannot ignore either.

Slow motion 3 · The old house and garden

After she becomes the commander's lady, Chunmei goes back once to the old Ximen house. The master is dead, the family fallen, the garden long untended. She walks through it to the little upper rooms where Jinlian lived: windows broken, dust on the stairs, the bed and tables sold off, the flowering trees in the yard dead or toppled. Standing there, remembering the days she spent here with her mistress, she weeps.

Why it works: the same space is shown twice, once in the heat and once in the cold. For seventy-nine chapters this garden was the busiest stage in the book — snow-viewing, drinking, sniping, assignations. Now the same garden is shown again, and nothing needs saying; the broken things are the whole narrative. The point of view is the cruelest part. Chunmei, who walked out empty-handed without a tear, comes back a great lady and weeps here. She would not cry then because she refused to bow before Yueniang; she cries now because no one needs her to be hard anymore. Cao Xueqin would later take a great garden from splendour to ruin with exactly this move.

The core device: a novel that keeps accounts

This is not a novel of invented worlds, so there is no "dark forest" to work through. Its signature device is three things locked together.

First, grafting. The author borrows a ready-made episode and a handful of characters from The Water Margin and changes one step at the crucial moment — Wu Song misses — so that a villain who should have died in chapter 26 lives for seventy chapters more. He does not start from scratch; he pries open a side branch of a heroic saga and widens it into a world where the hero is absent. Wu Song's absence is the book's premise: there is no one to dispense justice on heaven's behalf, only people getting on with life. When the hero finally returns, all he can do is add one more stroke to the ruin. The move holds, and it is elegant: read the two books side by side and you see how much the swashbuckling justice of The Water Margin leaves out.

Second, ledger realism. This book counts things: how many dishes at a banquet and which; what colour jacket a woman wears with which skirt; how many taels went into a lawsuit. It records them so precisely that historians use it as a source on late-Ming material life. The story is nominally set in the Northern Song, in the 1110s, but the circulation of silver, merchants buying office, the salt-certificate trade and the street speech are almost entirely late Ming. The inventories are not padding; they are the characters. Ximen's power, Jinlian's jealousy, Ping'er's standing are all written in objects and prices. The author seldom tells you what a character feels; he tells you what she received and what she lost.

Third, hot-and-cold structure inside a karmic frame. The opening verse sets out the four snares — wine, women, wealth and temper — as a warning; the closing chapter has a monk release the wronged dead and has Xiaoge reborn to pay a debt. The whole book sits in a Buddhist frame of karmic retribution: Ximen, greedy for sex, dies of it; Jinlian, who killed her husband, dies by the blade; Chunmei, insatiable, dies of excess. Each account is settled. Here one must be honest: the frame is the author's own, but the body of the book is bigger than its frame. The retribution is too tidy; some characters (Sun Xue'e, Song Huilian) suffer far beyond their offences, and it is hard to say Meng Yulou and Yueniang end well because they were good. The most alive parts are exactly what the frame cannot hold: Ximen's tears, Jinlian's wait in the snow, Chunmei weeping in the old rooms. That is why Zhang Zhupo's "hot and cold" is a truer skeleton than karma. Retribution is a moral conclusion; hot and cold is what the author actually saw — everything that burns will cool, whether or not you did wrong.

What it makes you think about

One · A woman sold at nine later poisons her husband and frightens another woman's baby to death. How much does her history excuse?

Jinlian poisons Wu the Elder, brings about the deaths of Guan'ge and Ping'er, and finally sends Ximen to his own. But she was sold at nine and sold again at fifteen, and never had a single choice. Suppose you are her judge. What is your sentence?

The case that her history cannot excuse her runs like this. The same courtyard holds plenty of women in situations much like hers — Sun Xue'e was a maid, Meng Yulou a remarried widow, Ping'er had been treated as property by men — and none of them killed a baby. If having been harmed explains harming others, every perpetrator can find a childhood, and the people they harm — here, a child not yet two — have no history at all. Understanding why she did it is not forgiving her for doing it; when the two are blurred together, the one who benefits is always whoever has the power.

The case that her history must count runs like this. This courtyard is a place where a single man allocates everything. A wife's food, clothes, dignity and old age depend on whose room he chooses tonight; a woman without a son is sold off when he dies — and Jinlian's own end proves that fear was not paranoia. Under such rules, fighting is not a flaw of character but the only way to survive; Yueniang can afford to be dignified because she is the principal wife and holds the keys. You cannot throw someone into an arena and then blame her for biting. Besides, the one who actually held power, and destroyed Song Huilian's whole family, was Ximen Qing — yet the "wanton woman" everyone remembers is her.

Where does the author stand? He gives Jinlian a gruesome death, which looks like retribution; yet he also wrote the pipa on the snowy night and her flicker of real joy at the thought of marrying Wu Song. He does not defend her, but he refuses to make her a symbol. Bring it home: the person you have watched become sharp and calculating in a bad workplace or a bad family. The question is not whether she deserves pity, but which needs to change — her, or the courtyard?

Two · Ximen Qing is generous, capable of love, a good businessman — and he takes bribes to free a murderer. Is the fault his, or the system's?

He buys a poor friend a house and weeps himself hoarse for a dead woman; he also takes money to let a killer walk and uses the yamen as his private muscle. Every wrong he does is done by the rules: gifts, an adopted godfather, palms greased. Put someone else in his place — would you get another Ximen Qing?

The case for blaming the system runs like this. From top to bottom there is not one clean official in this book. Cai Jing sells offices, inspectors accept gifts, a censor's impeachment is quashed with a word. Ximen simply read the price list sooner than others and used it to the full. When a system rewards bribery and punishes integrity, personal virtue only decides who loses; it cannot change the outcome. Condemn Ximen if you like — the next Ximen is already sitting in Zhang Erguan's house, and Ying Bojue is already advising him.

The case for blaming him runs like this. No rule ever made a man take other men's wives, frame his servants or ruin a doctor. He wanted those things. Within the same system, others chose to take a little less and harm one person fewer; Chang Shijie was poor and hurt no one. "The system is corrupt" is true, but it slides too easily into a pardon for everyone who profits from it. Ximen's deepest wrong is not greed; it is that he sincerely thought all of it was business.

The author's handling is telling. Ximen does not die in a lawsuit or at an enemy's hand but of his own appetite and at the hands of the woman beside him: the system protected him to the end, and his own hunger killed him. Bring it home: the boss or elder who rose by knowing how things work, and is genuinely good to the people around him. Would you call him a good man? If not — how much better would you be in his seat?

Three · A book that renders desire in this much detail: who should read it, and how much of it?

For four hundred years this book has been banned and cut almost without pause. Its earliest preface already sorts its readers: those who feel pity are bodhisattvas, those who feel fear are gentlemen, those who feel delight are petty men — and those who want to imitate it, the preface says flatly, are beasts. From the 1950s the complete text in mainland China was printed only in small, restricted runs; the edition sold to general readers removed close to twenty thousand characters of sexual description, and most copies in mainland bookshops today are still abridged. Is that right?

The case for cutting runs like this. Those passages are extremely explicit and do not always advance the story; some are simply display. Handing the whole thing to everyone assumes that every reader will be the preface's bodhisattva and not its petty man. Cut twenty thousand characters and the remaining nine hundred thousand — the manners, the people, the structure — can reach hundreds of millions of readers; that is not obviously a bad bargain. Without the abridgment, most people might never be able to get hold of the book at all.

The case against cutting runs like this. The book's subject is desire, and those scenes are not ornament but evidence: they grow more reckless chapter by chapter, which is precisely the curve of Ximen's progress toward the three pills. Remove them and the reader meets a much cleaner, much falser Ximen, and his death is left as a sermon. More fundamentally: who decides which readers are the petty men? Abridgment never protects readers; it decides for them — and the people deciding have always read the full text.

The author's view is hidden in the structure: he puts the most explicit chapters in the hottest stretch and then ends everyone in the cold, as though to say, the more eagerly you watch, the colder it gets. Bring it home: choosing books for a child, or choosing what you yourself won't look at. Where do you draw the line between protecting and restricting — and who taught you to draw it there?

Motifs: what a nameless author keeps returning to

Lanling Xiaoxiaosheng left only this book, so there are no other works to set beside it; we can only see where, within it, he keeps coming back.

His first obsession is things: dishes, cloth, jewels, sedan chairs, coffins, medicines. To him, relations between people are the movement of objects. The silver passed over the wall by Ping'er, the clothes Chunmei was forbidden to take, Jinlian's body that no one would claim: objects state each person's position for him.

His second is dispersal. The book puts its greatest effort not into Ximen's rise but into how people leave after his death: one by one, each with reasons, none staying out of affection. He does not believe a place held together by money can end any other way. That dispersal became one of the deepest undertones of the Chinese novel: Dream of the Red Chamber, which ends with everyone scattered and the land swept clean and white, carries the same obsession from one upstart household to a century-old clan.

His third is looking without judging. He installed the karmic frame, but within it he almost never interrupts his characters to preach. That is his great difference from The Water Margin: there, wanton women and local bullies are just things the heroes dispose of; here they are the protagonists, with desires, calculations and pain. Cao Xueqin learned from this book how to write a household, but turned its gaze from desire to tenderness.

Controversy and criticism

A dirty book. That has been its chief charge for four centuries. It was banned repeatedly under the Qing, and in popular usage its name became almost a byword for pornography. From Zhang Zhupo onward, defenders have argued it is "not a lewd book" but depicts lewdness in order to warn against it. In fairness, the amount of sexual description, its explicitness, and the playful spirit of some passages go well beyond what a warning requires; "it is meant as admonition" does not fully account for the relish the author brings to those pages. Both readings still have committed defenders.

The women. Nearly every woman in the book is written in terms of desire and rivalry for favour; the "wanton" women die one more horribly than the next, while Ximen's end — sudden death, then rebirth and redemption — carries a certain gentleness. Feminist critics note that the title is made of three women's names, yet they are the ones watched and punished. Other scholars argue the reverse: that this is the first book to give a crowd of concubines and maids complex inner lives and real agency, and that Jinlian and Chunmei are among the earliest ambitious women in Chinese fiction. Both views have textual support.

Rough construction. The cihua text is studded with opera lyrics and stock phrases, with names and dates that contradict one another, and some passages look borrowed from elsewhere and pasted in; the Chongzhen edition cut and polished a great deal. Some scholars take this as evidence that the book began as a storyteller's script or passed through several hands. As for the author, centuries of argument have produced no consensus, and any single name is conjecture.

The karmic frame. Many modern readers find the final chapter's release of souls, reincarnation and renunciation forced, a Buddhist label slapped onto a realist novel; others argue that this very stiffness gives the whole book a final, ice-cold echo.

Crossovers

  • Anthropology · Mauss's gift: In The Gift (1925), Marcel Mauss argued that a gift creates an obligation to reciprocate and so binds people into networks. Ximen's birthday tributes, his "godfather," his favours have exactly this shape. The difference: here gifts flow only upward and become openly priced purchases.
  • Neuroscience · "wanting" versus "liking": Kent Berridge's research proposes that the brain systems driving wanting and those producing liking can come apart: one can crave something ever more without enjoying it any more. Ximen moving from one woman to the next, from one pill to three, traces that curve. The difference: that work describes the neural mechanics of addiction; no one in the book is treated as ill, and the author calls it sin, and fate.
  • Anthropology · co-wife conflict: William Jankowiak and colleagues, comparing many polygynous societies, found conflict between co-wives widespread, centred mostly on the husband's time and resources for each wife's children. Jinlian hates precisely the share Guan'ge commands. The difference: co-wives elsewhere often form alliances; in Ximen's house the only alliances are between mistress and maid.
  • Philosophy · Schopenhauer's pendulum: Schopenhauer held that unfulfilled desire is pain and fulfilled desire quickly turns to boredom, so life swings between the two. Ximen's life is that pendulum swinging ever faster. The difference: Schopenhauer meant everyone's normal condition; this book writes one household's account, with interest, and a due date.
