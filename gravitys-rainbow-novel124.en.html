Novels, Deep-Read · Thomas Pynchon · No.124

Gravity's Rainbow

Thomas Pynchon · 1973 · four parts, seventy-three episodes · many points of view

中文 →

London, December 1944, not yet light. A British officer wakes from a dream of an evacuation train running without lights, packed with people being carried deeper into the dark.
He opens the window. Far off, a white contrail is climbing straight up the sky, thin as a thread.
He knows what it is: a rocket fired from Holland. It travels faster than sound, so when it lands you hear the explosion first and the scream of its approach afterward.
He turns back to the kitchen and starts making a banana breakfast for the men sleeping it off in his flat. He grows the bananas himself, in a glass hothouse on the roof.

This piece contains full spoilers: the experiment behind the map, how the hero ends up "scattered," who is inside the rocket numbered 00000, and where the rocket on the last page comes down. Most of the book's meaning rests on an ending that refuses to close, so there's no discussing it without going all the way.

In One Line

An American lieutenant discovers that the places where German V-2 rockets hit London match the stars he has pinned to a wall map to record his sexual conquests, and that the stars always go up first. He follows the thread into the ruins of postwar Germany and finds not an answer but a web that ties together the war, the chemical cartels, behavioral psychology and the rocket itself. Then he comes apart inside the web, until there isn't even a "person" left to find.

Where It Sits

Thomas Pynchon (b. 1937) almost never appears in public and has left very few photographs behind. As a young man he spent two years at Boeing as a technical writer, producing documentation for a missile program. Gravity's Rainbow is his third novel, after V. (1963) and the slim The Crying of Lot 49 (1966); Vineland, Mason & Dixon, Inherent Vice and others came later.

Its place in literary history is "the mountain of postwar American fiction": some 760 dense pages, hundreds of named characters, with behavioral psychology, polymer chemistry and calculus all worked into the plot. In 1974 it shared the National Book Award with Isaac Bashevis Singer. The same year the Pulitzer fiction jury unanimously recommended it, the Pulitzer advisory board overruled them, and no fiction prize was given at all. It's a book that prizes raised up and turned away at the same moment, which is a very Pynchonian fate.

The Story

Act One · London: Stars on a Map

The man making breakfast is Captain Geoffrey "Pirate" Prentice, who has a strange talent that intelligence has learned to use: he can dream other people's dreams. He works through the fantasies of important men too busy or too respectable to have them themselves. The book opens with the scream ("A screaming comes across the sky," first line of Part 1) and moves straight into his dream of the evacuation. The train inches through the night, the passengers know it is already too late, Setup ①the evacuation goes on, but "it's all theatre" (same page). On first reading it sounds like nothing more than dream-gloom, a tired man's sneer at wartime propaganda.

The V-2 was real. From September 1944 Germany fired well over a thousand of them at London from the Dutch coast, and they killed thousands of people. What made them terrifying was physics. Coming back into the atmosphere at several times the speed of sound, the death arrived first and the sound came after it. By the time you heard one coming, you were already dead or already safe. The whole city lived inside that reversed causality.

Tyrone Slothrop is a US Army lieutenant from an old Puritan family in Massachusetts, flying a desk at an Allied technical-intelligence outfit in London. On the wall of his cubicle hangs a map of London covered in little colored stars, one for each girl he has slept with (or says he has). Setup ②The trouble is that somebody has secretly photographed it, and laid against a second map, one plotting where the rockets have fallen, the stars and the craters coincide, and the stars always come first: the rockets land on the same spots two to ten days later.

The people who get hold of this sit at a place called The White Visitation, an old asylum now home to a British psychological-warfare unit, stocked with mediums, hypnotists and laboratory dogs. Two men there stand for the book's two worldviews. Edward Pointsman is a Pavlovian. For him everything is stimulus and response, and everything has a cause, so Slothrop's map makes him tremble with excitement: it means there is a cause to be found. Roger Mexico is a statistician. He has shown that the rocket strikes follow a Poisson distribution, the mathematics of rare, independent random events, under which the number of times a patch of ground has already been hit has no bearing on whether it will be hit next. In Roger's world there are no causes, only probabilities. In Pointsman's, probability is just a cause nobody has found yet.

Roger falls in love during the war. Jessica Swanlake has an officer fiancé, and she and Roger hide out in an abandoned house in a restricted coastal zone, living a life that only the war makes possible. It's one of the few warm stretches in the book, and its warmth is borrowed from the war. Once the war ends, Jessica goes back to her dependable fiancé.

Pointsman decides to make Slothrop his experiment. First they inject him with a "truth drug" and let him talk his way back through his past. Slothrop drifts back to his teens in Boston, drops his harmonica down a toilet in a dance hall, goes in after it, and floats through an entire sewer-world built out of other people's excrement and secrets. It's Pynchon's signature move: the lowest thing and the highest thing in the same sentence.

Act Two · The Casino: A Stolen Name

To get Slothrop somewhere they can control him, Pointsman's people set up a sting. In the last months of the war he is sent on leave to a casino on the French Riviera, taken over by the Allies and renamed the Casino Hermann Goering. On the beach an octopus, trained in advance, lunges out of the water at a young woman, and Slothrop draws it off with a crab and saves her. She is Katje Borgesius, a Dutch double agent, and she has been planted to get close to him.

Here Katje's past comes out. She lived for a time at a V-2 launch site outside The Hague, in the household of an SS officer named Weissmann, code name Blicero (an old German nickname for Death). He commanded a rocket battery, and he had made death into a religion. With Katje and a young German soldier named Gottfried he played out a long game in the house at the site: Setup ③"Hansel and Gretel," the fairy tale where two children are caught by a witch in the forest who means to shove them into her oven. Blicero played the witch, the two young people played the captive children, and there really was an oven in the house. It looks at first like nothing more than sadomasochistic domestic theater, a man unhinged by war rehearsing a fairy tale among the ruins. Katje eventually got away and went over to the British.

At the casino Slothrop starts reading the rocket's technical literature, and a name turns up: Laszlo Jamf, a German chemist who invented a new plastic called Imipolex G. He also gets a dim sense of something else: a special rocket, serial number 00000, carrying a device called the S-Gerät that needs this plastic for insulation.

Payoff ② ↑Then comes the truth. When Slothrop was a baby, his father sold him to an experiment. Jamf, then doing conditioning research in America, linked infant Tyrone's erections to a particular stimulus; in exchange the family got money, and somebody would pay his way through Harvard. The reflex was supposedly "extinguished" when the experiment ended, but in Pavlov's theory an extinguished reflex may only be sleeping. The novel hints that Slothrop's body senses the rocket before it arrives, perhaps through the plastic, perhaps through something else. The stars on the wall in Act One looked like an officer's tally of conquests. Now they turn out to be a medical chart: he isn't a ladies' man, he's a trained dog, and he never knew. The elegance of it is that Pynchon leaves the chart as a hint and never confirms it, so you are left like Slothrop with nothing but suspicion.

He wants to talk it over with someone, but his best friend, a British officer, is abruptly transferred, and word of his death follows. Back in his room, Slothrop finds his clothes gone, his papers gone, his uniform swapped for someone else's. While he slept, someone took away the identity called Tyrone Slothrop. He understands that he's a lab animal, and he runs: through Nice, into neutral Zurich, and from there across into defeated Germany.

Act Three · The Zone

The novel calls Germany in the summer of 1945 "the Zone." The state is gone, the old borders and authorities have collapsed, and the four Allied powers haven't yet nailed down a new order. Four parties are scrambling for the rocket's wreckage, parts, blueprints and engineers: the Americans, the British, the Soviets, and a force nobody expected.

That force is the Schwarzkommando, made up of Herero from South-West Africa. Historically, Germany carried out a massacre of the Herero beginning in 1904, one of the first genocides of the twentieth century. The novel invents a band of Herero exiles who were brought to Germany and trained as rocket troops. Their leader is Enzian, who as a young man in South-West Africa was Weissmann's lover. Now, with Germany beaten, he leads his people around the Zone, piecing together a rocket of their own. No one knows exactly what they mean to do with it: fire it, or make themselves its sacrifice.

Slothrop keeps changing clothes and names. In the ruins of Berlin he puts on a cape and helmet and becomes "Rocketman," fetching a stash of hashish buried in a garden at Potsdam during the Potsdam Conference for an American sailor. Later, at a festival in a small town, he climbs into a pig suit to play a local folk hero. The harder he hunts for why the rockets follow him, the less of a fixed outline he has.

He meets Margherita Erdmann, a faded German film actress who starred in the director von Göll's Expressionist horror film Alpdrücken ("Nightmare"). The film has a scene in which she is assaulted by a series of men, and off camera, Setup ④after that scene was shot, she conceived her daughter Bianca. Bianca is still a child. Aboard the Anubis, a pleasure boat drifting down a river toward the sea, she and Slothrop have a relationship that many readers find deeply disturbing. Slothrop is later pitched overboard, and Bianca's fate on the boat is written like a death that he never even gets the chance to learn about.

Another strand belongs to an engineer. Franz Pökler works on rocket propulsion at Peenemünde, an apolitical man who cares about combustion chambers. His wife Leni, a leftist, left him and took their daughter, and was later sent to a camp. Weissmann offers Pökler a deal: keep working, and every summer your daughter Ilse will be sent to spend a few days with you at a children's holiday camp. So every August a girl arrives, calls him Papa, a little taller each time. Pökler begins to wonder whether it's the same child at all, or whether they simply send whatever girl is the right age. He can't bring himself to ask, because asking might cost him even this one.

Pökler remembers where his daughter came from. Payoff ④ ↑Years earlier he and Leni went to see Alpdrücken. The assault scene left him inflamed all the way home, and Ilse was conceived that night. When Margherita talked about Bianca, it sounded like no more than one actress's sordid history. Here the two strands lock together: the same scene produced a girl on each side of the screen, one conceived during the shooting and one during the watching. And the way Pökler now knows his daughter is the way you watch a film, one frame a year, stitched into a "growing child" by persistence of vision. Pynchon never says whether Ilse is real. He hands the question to cinema itself: the image moves continuously, and every frame is a different picture.

Near the end of the war Pökler walks into Dora, the camp beside the underground factory at Nordhausen where prisoners assembled his rockets. For the first time he sees the other end of his work: corpses, starvation, the dying. He sits beside a woman close to death and slips his wedding ring onto her finger. The novel won't tell you whether that is atonement or self-comfort.

And then there's the fourth party: Tchitcherine, a Soviet intelligence officer, who is hunting a man in the Zone rather than a rocket. Setup ⑤Enzian is his half-brother. Their father, a Russian sailor, fathered Enzian with a Herero woman in South-West Africa. Tchitcherine means to find him and kill him, as if wiping out this dark-skinned brother would make his own origins clean.

Act Four · The Counterforce, and How a Man Comes Apart

Part Four is called "The Counterforce." Having lost Jessica, Roger Mexico finally sees what kind of machine he has been working for. He falls in with a few others whom the machine has used and discarded, and they try to resist "They," a word that in this book never means any one country or company but an invisible web of interests that runs across both sides of the war. Roger's most satisfying act of resistance is to walk into a meeting room full of powerful men, climb onto the table, and piss on it. The novel isn't hopeful about this counterforce: it is loose, juvenile, and most likely gets absorbed in the end.

At The White Visitation the elderly Brigadier Pudding has been submitting in secret to Katje, playing a dominatrix, sessions that include eating her excrement. He dies of an E. coli infection. Pointsman's scheme ends in a botched operation. He sends men to castrate Slothrop, so that his lab dog can be fully controlled, and through a chain of mix-ups the man held down on the table is Major Marvy, a loudmouthed American racist.

Something harder to describe is happening to Slothrop. He's becoming less and less of a character. People who know him meet him and fail to recognize him, or recognize only part of him. His memories, his name, his identity peel away piece by piece, and an old friend who sees him later finds him nearly transparent. One day in open country he sees a rainbow, very concrete and very physical, and he stands there and weeps, nothing in his head, feeling at last natural. After that he slips out of the plot. The last few times the novel mentions him, it's all hearsay.

Payoff ⑤ ↑Tchitcherine's hunt gets an ending too. Geli Tripping, a German girl learning witchcraft in the Zone, has fallen for him and cast a small love spell on him. One day on a road Tchitcherine passes Enzian. The man he has spent the entire book chasing is right in front of him, and he doesn't recognize him; Enzian sees only a white officer he doesn't know. From Tchitcherine's first appearance the reader has been waiting for the bloody meeting of the brothers, and Pynchon gives us a meeting that doesn't happen. It overturns the most traditional plot line in the novel, the revenge plot: what makes a man let go of his hatred isn't reconciliation but failing to recognize his enemy, and what saves the brother isn't some grand force but one girl's private wish.

Act Five · 00000

The book's real ending runs backward in time. In the spring of 1945, the last weeks of the war, on the Lüneburg Heath in northern Germany, Blicero fires the rocket numbered 00000. The S-Gerät, it turns out, is a person.

Payoff ③ ↑Gottfried is wrapped in a white shroud of Imipolex G and fitted into the rocket. Before launch Blicero speaks to him about death and transcendence, then fires. In Act Two the game of "Hansel and Gretel" looked like a sick officer's private amusement. Here it is revealed as a rehearsal all along: the witch finally pushes the child into the oven, and the oven is a rocket. Gretel got away; Hansel didn't. Even the plastic shroud reels in another line from Act Two: the material Jamf invented, once used to train an American baby, now wraps the body of a German boy.

On the last page the camera jumps more than twenty years ahead, to Los Angeles. In a movie house called the Orpheus, the film breaks halfway through and the screen goes dark. The audience sits and waits, and the manager's name and patter are a clear dig at the sitting president, Richard Nixon. Overhead a rocket is coming down, hanging above the roof in the last sliver of time not yet measured. Pynchon calls it by the calculus symbol Δt, an interval that approaches zero without ever quite reaching it. Payoff ① ↑"It's all theatre," the line from the dream on the first page, now comes true literally: we are sitting in the theater, the reader included, with the rocket above us and the film broken. The scream across the sky from the opening line still hasn't been heard, because with this kind of rocket, hearing it means it has already landed.

The fuse: the story looks like Slothrop chasing a rocket, but really the rocket is chasing him, and it has been since shortly after he was born. It isn't that a German weapon found an American. It's that an American father, for the price of a college education, sold his infant son to a scientist tied to the German chemical cartels. The men on both sides believed they were fighting a war, and all along they had signed the same purchase order. Every escape Slothrop makes after that, every change of costume and his final scattering, is an aftershock of that deal, and the rocket hanging over the movie house at the end simply forwards the bill from 1945 to whatever afternoon the reader is sitting in.

Three Slow-Motion Scenes

1 · The Banana Breakfast

At the very start Pirate Prentice cooks breakfast for a houseful of hungover officers: banana omelets, banana sandwiches, banana puddings and more. Pynchon spends pages on the smell of bananas spreading through a cold winter flat, overpowering the stale liquor and sweat. The scene's power is that it puts death and the body on the same table. On the same morning, outside the window, a rocket is climbing that will come down somewhere in the city within minutes; inside, a roomful of living, hungry, smelly bodies. And the shape of a banana, in a book whose great motifs are erections and rockets, is no accident.

2 · The Girl Who Comes Every August

The chapters with Pökler and "Ilse" at the children's camp are the quietest and cruelest in the book. The point of view stays pinned to Pökler, so the reader never knows more than he does. Some of what the girl says fits and some doesn't; her face seems to have changed, but children's faces do change. Pynchon withholds the answer and lets doubt grow over several years, while letting a father's love grow alongside it, two things growing in one man that can't cancel each other out. The lesson for a writer: the strongest suspense isn't "what happens next" but "is what I'm seeing real," and a person who can't bear to lose what he loves will choose not to know.

3 · Slothrop Under the Rainbow

The last few episodes where Slothrop is seen are written in the opposite way from everything else in the book. Elsewhere there's a flood of information: chemical formulas, names, companies, conspiracies. Here it suddenly drains away, leaving a field, the end of a rain, a rainbow and a man crying. He has "nothing in his head," which in a book that thinks on nearly every page feels like a power cut. Pynchon's choice is to write the hero's release so briefly and so emptily that you can't be sure it is a release. The rainbow is also the arc in the title, the rocket's trajectory, restored here to what it was in the first place: light in the sky.

The Core Device: Paranoia as a Way of Knowing

This book has no single premise to work out step by step, the way a "dark forest" can be. Its core device is a way of seeing: paranoia, the belief that everything is connected and that behind everything stands a "They."

The rules. Scattered through the novel are a handful of "Proverbs for Paranoids." The best known says, roughly, that if They can get you asking the wrong questions, they needn't worry about the answers. Slothrop's whole journey runs on that rule. The deeper he digs, the more he finds the rocket, Jamf, his father's business, the German chemical giant IG Farben and the agreements it signed with American and British firms before the war all knotted together. In the newspapers the war is two camps fighting; on the purchase orders it is one market expanding.

How the logic runs. Step one: the V-2's inverted causality, blast before scream, makes effect-before-cause an everyday experience for Londoners. Step two: Slothrop's stars come before the rockets, moving that inversion into one man's body. Step three: Pointsman wants a cause (conditioning) and Roger says there isn't one (Poisson), and both explanations "fit" the data, which can't settle the matter. Step four: in a world where explanations can't be settled, paranoia becomes a rational response. You would rather believe someone is pulling the strings than believe that nothing means anything. Someone in the novel names the opposite condition "anti-paranoia," in which nothing connects to anything, and says few people can stand it for long.

What holds up. The historical layer is real. IG Farben did sign technology and market agreements with American oil interests, and its executives were tried at Nuremberg after the war. The V-2 was assembled by concentration-camp prisoners in the underground works at Nordhausen, and more people died building it than were killed by its attacks. After the war the United States did bring Wernher von Braun and other German rocket engineers over in Operation Paperclip, and they went on to build the rocket that put men on the moon. The epigraph to Part One is from von Braun, to the effect that nature knows no extinction, only transformation. The rocket really did fly from Germany to America. That isn't paranoia; it's in the archives.

What's there because the novel needs it. Slothrop's predictive map, Jamf, Imipolex G, the S-Gerät, rocket 00000 and the Schwarzkommando are all invented. Conditioning cannot make anyone sense an explosion days in advance. That's a fictional premise, not physics and not psychology. Pynchon's real skill is that he never lets you confirm any of the conspiracies; by the last page, whether They exist at all is still a judgment the reader has to make. The form works as the same device. The seventy-three episodes are separated by rows of little squares that many readers take for the sprocket holes along the edge of a film strip. Characters watch films, get filmed, and are assembled frame by frame the way films are, and in the end the reader is seated in a movie house. To read the book is to live through "every fragment might connect, and you can never check."

What It Makes You Think About

One · When you find a web that ties a great many things together, should you trust it, or doubt yourself?

The case for trusting it: Slothrop's suspicions are mostly right. He was sold, he was experimented on, his identity was taken. The ties between IG Farben and Allied corporations, and the engineers boxed up and shipped off after the war, are matters of record. The people who tell you "don't overthink it, it's coincidence" are often the people who profit from things as they are. In a world woven by powerful institutions, a person who refuses to see patterns is the easiest person to use. Paranoia isn't a sickness; it's a clear-headedness that refuses to be soothed.

The case for doubting yourself: but it also destroys Slothrop. The further he pursues it, the more the world looks like a web and the more he looks like a knot in it, until nothing of him is left. Paranoia is a way of thinking with no exit: any counterevidence can be explained as Their cleverer cover-up. Roger's Poisson distribution is a different kind of honesty. Some hits really are random, and admitting that is cold, but it frees you from the illness of being the center of the universe. Today's conspiracy theorists look at the world exactly the way Slothrop does, and mostly they're wrong.

Where does Pynchon stand? In the narrow gap between the two. He lets paranoia be half right and lets it wreck a man with the other half. Bring it home: there really is a system behind every post in your feed, working out who you are, and yet each time you feel "this is aimed at me," it may not be. Telling "there really is a web" apart from "all I can see is webs" is probably something everyone now has to learn.

Two · If a system gives you back your daughter for one summer a year, and the price is that you keep building its weapons, do you keep working?

The case for working: if Pökler won't build the rocket, someone else will, and if he refuses, that girl, whoever she is, stops coming every August. One man's effect on a state machine is close to zero; his effect on one child is everything. In a situation where resistance is no longer possible, holding on to one particular person and one particular summer may be the only thing left that still counts as love.

The case for refusing: deals like this are exactly what keep the machine running. The system's great talent is giving each person a private reason: a daughter, a mortgage, a promotion, "I only do the combustion chamber." Every Pökler thinks his own share of guilt is negligible, and added together they make Dora. And he doesn't even dare ask whether the daughter is real. He's no longer compromising for love; he's using the look of love as an anesthetic.

Pynchon doesn't pass sentence. He walks Pökler into Dora, has him leave the ring, and lets him go. The gesture is a scrap of conscience and also a ritual that comes too late. Bring it home: taking a promotion at a company whose product you half-know does harm, or staying in a job for the sake of your children's school fees. This isn't a problem peculiar to the Second World War. It can find anyone who lives on a salary.

Three · If the only way out of a system that treats you as a lab animal is to stop being someone who can be located, is that freedom or defeat?

The case for freedom: Pointsman wants a subject he can observe, record and castrate. They want a person with a file and a name, a person who can be predicted. Once Slothrop scatters, there's nothing for them to grab. The moment he weeps under the rainbow is the one moment in the book when nobody is using him. Eastern traditions have long treated letting go of the self as liberation, not loss.

The case for defeat: but a scattered person can't do anything either. A counterforce needs people. Love needs people. Revenge and justice both need someone who remembers who he is. Slothrop's disappearance takes one nuisance off the system's hands without costing it an ounce of power. Look at the characters who actually do something: Roger pisses on the conference table, Pökler leaves a ring, Geli keeps one brother from killing the other. All of them are still somebody.

Pynchon's sympathy plainly goes to the scattered man; the whole theology of this book leans toward the people who were passed over. But he doesn't write it as a victory. Bring it home: deleting every account, quitting the job, moving somewhere no one knows you. Is that a fresh start, or handing the whole field to the other side? The answer probably changes at different points in a life.

Through-Line: The Passed-Over

Pynchon has spent his whole career writing about one thing: an invisible system, and the people it skips. One of Slothrop's Puritan ancestors wrote a heretical tract in defense of the "preterite." In Calvinist theology God chose a few, the "elect," for salvation in advance and passed over the rest; this ancestor argued that the passed-over were holy too. That is Pynchon's own position, practically unaltered.

In V. a man chases the woman behind a single letter through the colonial history of the twentieth century. That book already reaches the German massacre in South-West Africa in 1904, and it's where the name Weissmann first turns up; Gravity's Rainbow grows that chapter into an entire novel. The Crying of Lot 49 is the paranoid quest in miniature: a California housewife comes to suspect a secret postal system, and the book ends just before the answer is revealed. Later, Mason & Dixon examines the rational violence of two surveyors drawing a straight line across America, and Inherent Vice turns paranoia into the comedy of a stoned private eye. Gravity's Rainbow is the highest and darkest peak on that ridge: here the system doesn't just skip you, it trained you in advance.

Controversies and Criticism

Unreadable. This is the most common complaint, and it doesn't come only from ordinary readers. When the Pulitzer advisory board overruled the jury in 1974, its members reportedly called the book "unreadable," "turgid" and "overwritten," and parts of it "obscene." Its defenders reply that the difficulty is the point: a book about information overload and things that can't be verified ought to get you lost. Both arguments have something to them.

Sex and women. The book is full of explicit sex, including sadomasochism, coprophagia, and Slothrop's relationship with the underage Bianca. Many readers still can't accept the last of these, finding it written in far too much detail however you frame it as indictment. Most of the women serve as vehicles for male desire or male plots: Katje is bait, Jessica is the one who returns to order, Margherita and Bianca are bodies to be filmed and used. Some critics argue that Pynchon is depicting a system that turns everything into an object; others that the novel takes part in the objectifying itself.

How the Herero are portrayed. The novel writes a real genocide into its plot, rare for an American novel in 1973 and often counted to its credit. But the Schwarzkommando has also been criticized as a symbol a white author borrowed. The Herero in the book carry a great deal of myth, symbolism and an impulse toward collective suicide, and very little ordinary, individual life.

The absent author. Pynchon refuses to appear. At the National Book Award ceremony he sent a comedian to accept for him, who delivered a deliberately incoherent speech, and the next year he turned down the American Academy of Arts and Letters' Howells Medal. Some read this as contempt for literary celebrity, others as a carefully managed mystique.

Crossovers

  • Statistics · R. D. Clarke's flying-bomb analysis (1946): the British actuary R. D. Clarke divided part of south London into several hundred small squares, counted the German flying-bomb hits in each, and found a strikingly close fit to the Poisson distribution, with no sign of aimed clustering. What Roger does in the novel, someone really did. The difference is that Clarke was looking at V-1s, and his result reassured people, where Roger's only makes him lonelier.
  • Psychiatry · Klaus Conrad's "apophany": in 1958 the German psychiatrist Klaus Conrad used the term to describe the onset of schizophrenia, when unrelated things suddenly seem connected and charged with meaning aimed at you. Slothrop's journey reads almost like a novel-length version of that process, sinking deeper just as Conrad describes. Where it differs: for Conrad it is illness, while in Pynchon half the web is real.
  • Political history · Hofstadter's "paranoid style": Richard Hofstadter's 1964 essay "The Paranoid Style in American Politics" describes a rhetoric that sees history as one vast conspiracy. It's often read alongside Pynchon, and the shared shape is a "They" imagined as an all-present whole. The difference is that Hofstadter treats the style as something to guard against, while Pynchon makes it a view that is half right and half wrong.
  • Buddhism · non-self and the five aggregates: Buddhism holds that the "self" is just five aggregates (form, sensation, perception, mental formations, consciousness) temporarily bundled together, with no fixed core. Slothrop's scattering looks like a forced unbundling of the aggregates: name, memory and bodily feeling stripped away one by one until only a feeling under a rainbow is left. Where it differs: in Buddhism non-self is an awakening you practice toward, while Slothrop's is worn away from him, and he can't say whether it is release or collapse.
