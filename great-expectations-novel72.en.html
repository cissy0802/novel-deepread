Great Expectations

Charles Dickens · serialised weekly 1860–61 · three-volume edition 1861 · 59 chapters

The Thames marshes at dusk, the tide out. A boy of seven is in a churchyard working out what his parents looked like — he never saw them, so he reads it off the shapes of the letters on their stones. Two things stand up out of the flat country: a beacon, and a gallows.
Then a man rises from behind the graves in coarse grey, a leg-iron dragging. He turns the boy upside down, and the church, from where the boy now hangs, turns a somersault over its own weathercock. The man wants two things: something to eat, and a file.
Before dawn the next day the boy steals a pork pie from his sister's larder and a file from his brother-in-law's forge. That pie will one day turn into a fortune large enough to make him a London gentleman.

This page contains complete spoilers — who the anonymous benefactor actually is, what happened on Miss Havisham's wedding morning, who Estella's real parents are, and the fact that Dickens wrote two different endings. The convictions of this site: everything worth discussing lies after the last page. Until you know where the money came from, there is nothing to say about Pip.

In one sentence

An orphan from the marshes is raised into a London gentleman by money he is forbidden to ask about, and repays the blacksmith who brought him up by being ashamed of him; five years later he learns the money came from a transported convict he once fed as a child — a man who sent home every pound he earned herding sheep so that he too could own a gentleman — and that the cold girl he has loved all his life is that convict's daughter.

Coordinates

Charles Dickens (1812–1870) was the most successful professional novelist the English language has produced: serial instalments, sold-out public readings, his own magazines. Of his fifteen novels, Great Expectations is the thirteenth and the second-to-last he finished. It ran weekly in his own journal All the Year Round from December 1860 to August 1861, and the reason was commercial: the serial then running was failing and circulation was slipping, so the proprietor stepped in to rescue his own magazine. It is a book written against a weekly deadline, and it is the tightest structure he ever built.

Its place in literary history is equally clear: it is the Bildungsroman turned inside out. The standard plot has an orphan suffer, discover he is of good blood, inherit, and be happy. For half its length this novel follows that template exactly — and then, in chapter 39, kicks it over. The secret of the hero's fortune is not noble birth. It is a convict.

The story

Act one · A bargain in a churchyard

The narrator's name is Philip Pirrip, which his own infant tongue could not manage, so he has always been Pip. Orphaned, he is raised by a sister twenty years older — Mrs Joe, quick with her hands and quicker with her mouth, who keeps a cane for him and has named it Tickler. Her husband, Joe Gargery, is the village blacksmith: large, mild, illiterate, and more like a fellow-sufferer of Pip's own age than a father.

On Christmas Eve, in the churchyard, Pip is seized by an escaped convict, Magwitch, and does as he is told: the pie, the file. Seed ①Out on the marshes he also sees a second convict, his face damaged, plainly the first man's mortal enemy. Seed ②When the soldiers close in, Magwitch does not run. He spends his entire chance of escape on dragging the second man out of the mud and handing him over. Asked about the stolen food, he says in front of Pip that he took it himself — nobody at the forge had anything to do with it. The first moral act in the book is performed by the lowest person in it.

He goes back to the prison hulks moored in the estuary, and from there to transportation — in that era England shipped its serious offenders to Australia by the boatload, and returning before your sentence expired was a hanging matter. Pip assumes the episode is closed. What stays with him is a vague, unplaceable sense of being a thief.

Act two · Satis House: a pair of coarse hands and a clock stopped at twenty to nine

The local great lady, Miss Havisham, sends word that she wants a boy to come and play where she can watch. Pip is scrubbed, buttoned and delivered to Satis House — satis is Latin for enough; whoever owned the house, it was said, could want nothing more. A house called Enough, with a woman inside it for whom nothing has ever been enough.

Every shutter is nailed shut; no daylight gets in. The mistress sits in a yellowed wedding dress with one shoe on and one shoe off, and every clock in the building stands at twenty minutes to nine. On the long table lies a wedding cake gone grey-black, webbed over, with mice running through it. She points at the table and tells Pip what will happen when she dies: they are to lay her out on it in this dress and let the relatives come and look — that will be the last stroke against the man. Seed ③(Pip has no idea yet who the man is.)

The girl she is raising is called Estella, Pip's own age, too beautiful, and all blade. She calls him "boy", and in front of Miss Havisham she mocks his coarse hands, his thick boots, and the fact that he calls the knaves jacks. The card game they are playing is called beggar my neighbour. Pip goes out to the yard and cries. That cry is the real turning point of the novel: from that day he is ashamed of his own home. In the same overgrown garden he runs into a pale young gentleman who proposes a fight for no reason at all, goes down in three punches, and gets up extremely polite. Seed ④

Miss Havisham later pays twenty-five pounds to bind Pip apprentice to Joe — a trade that would feed him for life. But Satis House has already poisoned the well: the coal dust, Joe's grammar, the Sunday clothes, everything makes him wince. Then something happens at home. Mrs Joe is struck down from behind and left half-paralysed and speechless for the rest of her life. The weapon is lying on the kitchen floor: a convict's leg-iron that has been filed through — the very iron Magwitch filed off his leg with the file Pip stole for him. Payoff ① ↑What makes this beautiful is how quietly it is done. In chapter one the pie and the file look like nothing more than a frightened child's kindness; Dickens lets that kindness harden into an actual piece of iron and, years later, bring it down on the back of the only relative Pip has. Everything you have ever done is still out there somewhere; you simply don't know what shape it has taken. (The man who swung it was Joe's journeyman, Orlick. That comes later.) An orphan girl, Biddy, arrives to help, and teaches Pip his letters in passing. She learns everything faster than he does, and she loves him, and he cannot see it.

Act three · London: money you are forbidden to trace

In Pip's eighteenth year a London lawyer walks into the village pub and announces to the room that this young man has come into great expectations: an anonymous benefactor will keep him in London and have him educated as a gentleman, with a handsome property to follow. There is one condition — he must never inquire who the benefactor is.

The lawyer is Jaggers. He cross-examines rather than converses, keeps two death-masks of hanged clients in his office, and washes his hands with scented soap the moment a client leaves — he draws the line between himself and the people who pay him in a bar of soap. Pip does not hesitate for a second: the benefactor is Miss Havisham, Estella has been prepared for him, and this is the second half of a fairy tale. No one in the novel ever says so. That reading is entirely his own.

In London his flatmate turns out to be someone he recognises at once, and who recognises him: the pale young gentleman from the garden, Herbert Pocket, a distant relation of Miss Havisham's. Payoff ④ ↑The virtue of this stroke is that it does not show off. The schoolboy fight meant nothing when it happened; now it stitches Pip's two worlds together — every scrap of polish he will ever acquire comes from the boy he once knocked down, and Herbert corrects his table manners so gently that he never once feels the correction. It is Herbert who tells him Miss Havisham's history: a fiancé who took a large sum of her money, and a letter that arrived at twenty minutes to nine on the wedding morning instead of the man. The clocks have stood there since.

Pip's London life is a bill that thickens by the month: a club, a servant, furniture on credit. From his twenty-first birthday Jaggers pays him five hundred a year. The ugliest scene in the book is Joe coming up to town to visit. Joe in his Sunday clothes, his hat perpetually falling off the mantelpiece, unable to settle on "Pip, old chap" or "sir" and using both in one sentence; Pip squirming and wishing him gone. On his way out Joe says something exactly right — that this is not a place for him, that the two of them only fit together at the forge. Pip does not follow him down.

At Jaggers's dinner table Pip notices the lawyer's housekeeper, Molly: pale, deep scars across both wrists, and a strange, powerful way of moving her fingers as she serves. Jaggers pulls one wrist out of its sleeve in front of his guests and asks whether any of them has seen a stronger hand. Seed ⑤

Estella is sent to London too, with Pip as escort. She tells him repeatedly and plainly that she has no heart, that she was made to do damage, and that he should not love her; Miss Havisham, in his other ear, keeps saying love her, love her, love her. He takes that for a promise. In the same stretch he does the one admirable thing of his life: he spends his own money, in secret, buying a partnership for Herbert in a shipping house, on condition Herbert never learns of it.

Act four · Footsteps on the stairs

Pip is twenty-three and living in chambers by the river. On a night of wind and rain there are footsteps coming up, one tread at a time. At the door is a weathered man of about sixty, dressed like a sailor, who puts out both hands at the sight of him. Pip has no idea who he is. The man gives no name; he simply walks him back, step by step — the marshes, the file, the pie. Then he says the thing the whole book pivots on:

"I've made a gentleman on you!"— Magwitch explaining where the money came from (chapter 39)

He made his start herding sheep in New South Wales and in twenty years never spent a pound on himself. In the huts out there, he says, he thought about the child who brought him food in a graveyard, and he set out to make that child a brighter gentleman than the lot of them — and then he had to come and see it with his own eyes, even though coming back is a capital offence.

Pip's reaction is the most honest and least flattering passage in the novel. He is not moved; he is disgusted. The noises the man makes eating, the knife in his pocket, the way he keeps breaking off to admire the thing he has manufactured. Within a few pages his life collapses: Estella was never intended for him; every pound he has spent came off a convict's back; and Joe and Biddy, whom he traded for all this, are genuinely gone. He discovers not only that the class he looked down on has been keeping him, but that his own refinement is one of that class's products.

Magwitch tells his history: begging and stealing turnips in the street as a child, "in jail and out of jail" the whole way up; then a partner who dressed like a gentleman and could read and talk, named Compeyson — the second convict from the marshes. Payoff ② ↑Tried together, Compeyson in a good suit with a lawyer, laying everything off on his accomplice, got seven years. Magwitch got fourteen. Same case; an accent and a coat were worth seven years. This is the politics of the entire book: in chapter one Magwitch throws away his escape to lay hands on Compeyson, which reads at the time as unaccountable hatred, and four hundred pages later you understand it was a man collecting a debt no court would collect for him. And the sharper turn: Compeyson is the fiancé who took Miss Havisham's money and failed to appear on the wedding morning. The marshes and Satis House — the two worlds Pip thought he was travelling between — were always one network.

Pip goes to Satis House and says it: you let me believe it was you, you watched me get it wrong for ten years and never corrected me. To Estella he makes the one declaration in the book that asks for nothing, and she tells him calmly that she is marrying Bentley Drummle — rich, sullen, stupid, chosen precisely because she does not care about him. Then she turns to Miss Havisham and settles the account:

"I am what you have made me."— Estella to Miss Havisham (chapter 38)

It is the first time Miss Havisham sees what she has done. She catches at Pip's hands, asking over and over what she has done, and goes down on her knees to be forgiven. He forgives her. He leaves the room and turns back — she is standing too near the hearth, and the wedding dress is alight. He wraps her in the cloth off the table and holds her down on the floor, burning both his hands. She lives on for a while, still saying the same words. And when she dies they do what she ordered years before: they lay her out on the long table, beside the cake that has been rotting there for decades. Payoff ③ ↑What makes this stroke land is that it closes a curse without comment. She staged her own funeral as revenge on a man, and by the day she is actually laid out on that table the man has nothing to do with her. Twenty-odd years of planning, and the only targets she hit were herself and a girl she ruined.

One knot is left. Seeing Estella one day, Pip is caught by something in the movement of her fingers, and the face of Jaggers's housekeeper rises in his mind. Wemmick, the lawyer's clerk, confirms it: Molly stood trial twenty-odd years ago for strangling another woman, was got off by Jaggers, and has kept his house ever since; she had a three-year-old daughter at the time, said to have died. And Magwitch has mentioned a woman of his own, and a child lost when that woman was in trouble. The answer assembles itself: Payoff ⑤ ↑Estella is the daughter of Magwitch and Molly, handed over by Jaggers to a wealthy lady who happened to want a little girl. The cleverness here is that it works through the body: Pip does not deduce it, he recognises a pair of hands — and hands are exactly what Estella used to humiliate him with.

Act five · On the river: a failed escape and a hand held

Pip now has one thing left to do: get Magwitch out of England. He will not take another pound of the money, but he intends to save the man. They hide him by the river, work out the tides, and wait for a steamer bound for Hamburg, with Pip and Herbert at the oars themselves.

Compeyson informs. The boat is intercepted in mid-river by a police galley with a muffled figure aboard; Magwitch reaches out, pulls the cloak away, recognises him, and goes for him. They go into the water together and the steamer's paddles turn over the place where they went in. One man is pulled out: Magwitch, with his ribs crushed. Compeyson never surfaces. The only fight to the death in the whole novel is never shown directly — once they are in the water the point of view stays above the surface, and the refusal to describe it is what makes that water bottomless.

The trial is dispatched in a paragraph: the court has thirty-two men and women stood up together, Magwitch among them, and sentences them all to death at once. He lasts some days in the prison infirmary, and Pip comes every day and holds his hand. At the end Pip leans down to his ear and tells him his daughter is alive, that she is beautiful, and that he loves her. Magwitch lifts Pip's hand to his lips and goes. By law his property is forfeit to the Crown — Pip gets nothing, and by now that is no longer bad news.

Then Pip collapses: weeks of fever, creditors at the door in the middle of his delirium. When he comes round, the man sitting by the bed is Joe, who never once reproaches him; and when Pip can walk, Joe quietly goes home, leaving a receipt on the pillow — he has paid every one of Pip's debts. Pip walks back to the village to propose to Biddy, settle down, be a blacksmith, start again. He arrives on the morning the forge has flowers over the door: Joe and Biddy are married that day. Not even the cheapest reward — the one you get for repenting — is granted him. He gives them his blessing, borrows money, leaves the country, and works at Herbert's firm in Cairo for eleven years, clerk and then partner, paying people back by instalments.

Eleven years later he comes home. Joe and Biddy have a child sitting by the fire, named Pip. Then he walks up to where Satis House stood — the house has been pulled down; there is a stretch of wall and an overgrown garden. Estella is there. Drummle killed himself mistreating a horse, and the marriage had worked on her for years before that. They talk briefly, take hands, and walk out of the ruined garden together. The last clause of the novel is a deliberately awkward double negative:

"...no shadow of another parting from her."— final sentence, revised ending (chapter 59)

It is awkward because Dickens had originally written a different ending altogether. That comes up in the last section.

If you want a single fuse for this book, it is not the money and not the stopped house. The fuse is a stolen pie and a stolen file in chapter one. A terrified child brings food to a starving man — and no one else in that man's life has ever done such a thing for him. He remembers it for twenty years, sends home every pound he earns to buy the child a position in the world, and the money converts the child precisely into someone ashamed of his own kitchen. What destroys Pip is not malice. It is a kindness he can neither refuse nor repay.

Two scenes in slow motion

One · Chapter one: the church turns a somersault over its own weathercock

Play it again: flat country, black dykes, a leaden line of river beyond that, and beyond that a low-growling sea. A small boy crying among the stones. A man comes up out of nowhere and holds him upside down, and from where the boy hangs, the church goes over its own weathercock.

Three things are doing the work. First, the point of view is locked to a child's eye-level and a child's vocabulary. Pip explains how he "knows" his parents: he reads the shapes of the letters, decides from the square, stout look of his father's inscription that his father was a square, stout, dark-haired man. That is not a metaphor; that is genuinely how a newly literate child reasons. Second, the fear is rendered as geography — the only two objects standing up on that horizon are a beacon and a gallows, one to steer by and one to hang from. Third, the somersaulting church is description at the level of perception. "He held me upside down" is an action; a church flipping over its own weathercock puts the reader inside the inverted skull. And the whole book is the adult Pip remembering, so there is a layer of grown-up wincing wrapped around the child's eye — which is the most expensive thing first-person narration can buy.

Two · Chapter 39: the novel never lied to you

Play it again: a week of wind, the street lamps blown out. Pip hears a footfall below, takes a lamp to the stair-head and holds it out. A man climbs, stops at the light, looks up. Pip does not know him, but the man puts out both hands; Pip is scrupulously polite — come in, sit down, what is your business — and quietly on his guard. Then the man starts showing him something. An old file.

The technical move is releasing the information a gram at a time: the reader understands first, Pip second, and the pages in between are spent watching him walk towards an answer you are already holding. (Dickens wrote to a weekly deadline and put this chapter at the very end of the second volume; subscribers had seven days to sit with it.) But the suspense is not the remarkable part. The remarkable part is that this book has never once lied to the reader. The sentence "Miss Havisham is your benefactor" is spoken by nobody. Jaggers's phrasing is scrupulously legal throughout: the benefactor wishes to remain unknown, inquiry is forbidden. Miss Havisham neither confirms nor denies, because she is amused to watch Pip get it wrong — which is her sin, not the author's deception. Three hundred pages of illusion are something the reader manufactures voluntarily, alongside Pip: both of us would rather the respectable version were true. So the reversal does not feel like a mystery writer scoring a point. It feels like a slap landing on the reader's own snobbery. The way the book deceives you is precisely the thing the book is about.

The machine inside this book

One · "Great expectations" is a legal term, not an inspirational phrase

In the English of the period, great expectations meant an inheritance one stands to receive. To say a young man "has expectations" was to say money is coming to him. The title is not about ambition; it is about a sum of money waiting offstage. (The standard Chinese title shifts the weight onto aspiration and loses the money entirely; an older one goes the other way and keeps only the pathos.) The precision of the original is in its accounting: an expectation is an asset — and this novel exists to show that the asset buys the person holding it.

Which gives the book a question much sharper than "money corrupts": does where money comes from determine what it weighs? The same five hundred a year makes Pip a gentleman while he believes it comes from a lady; what does it make him once he knows it came from a convict? Not a penny has changed. All that changed is a certificate of origin — and his entire identity rests on that certificate. Dickens then pushes the point out to the scale of empire: the money is made in Australia, the place England tipped its criminals into and then drew profits back out of. An English gentleman's polish is earned by the people England threw away, somewhere England does not have to look.

Structurally it is the leanest thing he built: fifty-nine chapters cut by Dickens himself into three stages (nineteen chapters of marsh and apprenticeship, twenty of London and expectations, twenty of settling up), almost exactly equal, each closing with Pip's world turning over. The method can be reduced to one rule: a plant must do work as character before it does work as plot. Molly's wrists are a quirk of Jaggers's long before they are Estella's parentage.

Two · Everyone is issued a dark twin

The cast is arranged in pairs, each pair two routes out of the same fate. Joe and Jaggers: one cannot read and never says a wrong word that matters; the other has mastered the law and never says anything that counts. Biddy and Estella: two adopted poor girls, one taught to read, the other taught to wound. The pair that stings most is Orlick and Pip. In the sluice-house out on the marshes the sullen journeyman ties Pip up and makes the one accusation in the book that cannot be argued down: I struck your sister, but you did it. He was Pip's underling for years; Pip got every advantage and he got nothing. Orlick is Pip's resentment with the manners stripped off. Had Pip's shame not been cultivated into vanity by that money, it could have grown into Orlick.

What it forces you to think about

One · Should you ask where money came from? Is refusing it integrity, or squeamishness?

The condition of the gift was that he never ask, and he accepted without hesitation. Once he knows, he will not take another pound. The novel treats this as his moral turn.

Take the case against that turn as far as it will go. Look at the order in which he recoils: first the eating noises, the knife, the "my boy", and only days later the ethics. How much of the refusal is principle and how much is shame, he cannot tell himself. And the money is extraordinarily clean — not stolen, but earned over twenty years herding, shearing and dealing in stock by a man who spent nothing on himself. Had it been an earl's returns from a West Indian sugar estate — unremarkable in a gentleman of that period — Pip would not have asked a single question. What he is really refusing is not dirty money but an origin that embarrasses him.

The other side is just as hard. This money was never only money; it comes with a title of ownership. Magwitch's own formulation is "I've made a gentleman on you" — what he bought was not an education but a person, and he wants to sit beside him and watch him eat, read and walk, the way one inspects a finished product. Kept on those terms, none of a man's virtues belong to him. And Pip does not refuse empty-handed: he spends eleven years as a clerk in Cairo. He did not reject the gift; he converted it into a debt — and a debt is something he can actually discharge.

Bring it home: your education, your deposit, the job you were handed — how much of it survives tracing? If the answer made you uncomfortable, would you give it back, or would you stop tracing? And harder: would you accept a gift that comes with "I made you" attached — and have you already?

Two · What is the difference between raising a child as a weapon and raising one as a gentleman?

Miss Havisham adopts Estella, teaches her to have no heart, teaches her to be loved and to do damage with it, and spends a girl on revenge against a man who walked away thirty years ago. Magwitch keeps Pip so as to own someone brighter than the lot of them. Both take a child with no say in the matter and use it to dress an old wound of their own.

Put the case that these are nothing alike, because it is a strong one. What Magwitch spends is his own life — labour, thrift, a capital risk to come and look once, and he asks for nothing. What Miss Havisham spends is someone else's: she pays a proxy to do her hating and sits in a dark room. The outcomes differ too: Pip ends as a man who will hold a dying convict's hand; Estella ends married to a brute who worked on her for a decade.

And yet the novel does not let him off. What he wanted was also a product that would show them — the product merely came out wrong. Five hundred a year, no questions about its source, and on no account back to the forge: that arrangement converts Pip, with precision, into someone who despises Joe. Tenderness and cruelty are the same structural move: an adult packing what he never got into a child's body and sending the child to fetch it for him. Estella at least was told what she was for, which is why she has the strength to say "I am what you have made me"; Pip never knows whose life he is living, so he cannot even find the party to protest to. Besides, the one child in the book who comes out whole is Biddy — nobody invested in her.

Bring it home: "I only want you to have what I never had" — is that love, or an investment expecting a return? The test is: if the child spends your gift on exactly the life you most disapprove of, do you keep giving? Magwitch did. He watched Pip flinch from him, watched the money warp the man, and withdrew nothing. That is the real line between him and Miss Havisham — and it is a much narrower line than "who loved more".

Three · Joe never reproaches Pip. Is that the highest virtue in the book, or a failure to act?

Joe is despised for a whole volume and never says a hard word. When Pip falls ill he comes up to town for weeks, pays every debt, and slips home the moment the patient can stand, leaving a receipt. The moral high ground of the novel sits with him.

Make the case against him properly. Joe is also the man who watched his wife beat a child with a cane year after year and never stopped her. He offers an explanation: his own father beat his mother, he swore never to be that man, and so at home he takes it and gives way. The explanation is moving, and it is also convenient — he trades "not being a tyrant" for "protecting the child". A boy was beaten in that kitchen for ten years while the largest, kindest person in the house chose to be beaten alongside him rather than stand in front of him. And because he never reproaches, he never requires Pip to face anything either. Unconditional forgiveness sometimes wipes the slate, and wipes away the other person's chance to grow up with it.

The other side: Joe's silence is not for want of anything to say. He declines to win an argument at the moment his opponent is least able to have one. What he said in London was devastatingly accurate — this is no place for him, the two of them only fit at the forge, and so he will go. He saw all of it; he simply would not use his clarity as a weapon. And his inaction cost him: he paid off a debt he could barely carry. The whole weight of the book rests on one judgement: the people who could write ruined Pip, and the man who could not write took him back.

Bring it home: the last time you were forgiven, were you also made to say what you had done? If not, were you forgiven, or were you excused from growing? And the harder version: the last time you let something go, was it love — or was it that speaking is more work than silence?

The motif: what Dickens kept writing

One: children without fathers, and men who stand in for fathers. When Dickens was twelve his father was imprisoned for debt in the Marshalsea and he was sent to paste labels in a blacking factory; he never spoke about it publicly in his lifetime, and it is distributed through every book he wrote. Oliver in Oliver Twist (1838), David in David Copperfield (1850) — children of unknown provenance, passed between adults who call themselves guardians. Great Expectations is the end of that line, because it is the first time he refuses the child a respectable father: Pip's "second father" is a transported felon, and the best father he has is an illiterate blacksmith. Dickens reread David Copperfield before starting, to avoid repeating himself; what he produced was that book's negative.

Two: institutions as machinery that grinds people, and every location a cell. In Bleak House (1853) a suit in Chancery runs for generations until the legal costs have eaten the estate; Little Dorrit (1857) revolves around a debtors' prison and a government office designed to ensure nothing is ever done; the great fortune in Our Mutual Friend (1865) comes out of London's dust-heaps, literally out of rubbish. In Great Expectations the machinery contracts into one man, Jaggers, who does no evil and is merely always procedurally correct. And the prison becomes structural: the hulks in the estuary, Newgate, Satis House with its shutters nailed down, Joe's kitchen. A man can be sentenced, or he can sentence himself to life — and that house with its clocks stopped for twenty-odd years is the longest term served in the book.

Criticism and controversy

Two endings. The ending Dickens finished first has Estella, after Drummle's death, remarried to a Shropshire doctor; Pip meets her by chance years later in a London street, they shake hands, exchange a few words, and go their separate ways. At proof stage his friend the novelist Bulwer-Lytton read it and urged him to change it, so he wrote another: the two meet in the ruined garden at Satis House and walk out hand in hand. Both texts survive, and in a hundred and sixty years nobody has settled it — the edition Shaw oversaw simply printed the original. The case for the first ending is strong: this is a novel about not getting the thing you wanted and about the wanting itself being the disease, so letting him have her at last is a verdict that the crying in the yard at eight years old was right. But the revision is underrated too, and its final clause is hedged in its own grammar — "no shadow of another parting from her" is not "we never parted again", it is "I saw none". In a first-person novel, "I saw no shadow" is a very light guarantee.

Too much coincidence. The commonest objection, and a fair one: Estella happens to be the daughter of the convict Pip fed, that convict's mortal enemy happens to be the man who destroyed Miss Havisham, and the lawyer who defended the mother happens to be Pip's guardian. For a novel with a realist floor, the net is woven far too tight. Dickens's defence would be that the subject is precisely that the upper and lower ends of a society share the same plumbing — which can be true without making the coincidences less visible. The other acknowledged weak point is Orlick: he carries the darkest function in the book on a motive about three words thick.

Is Estella a person or a device? She is written beautiful, cold and almost without interiority — the novel is Pip's first person, so her inside is permanently closed to us, and critics have said she is a component for making the hero suffer. The wider problem with the women is harder to answer: they come in three kinds — mad (Miss Havisham), violent (Mrs Joe), and good without edges (Biddy). A woman whose entire life is defined by one broken engagement and who is then written as a monster in a wedding dress is the standing charge against the Miss Havisham figure. In fairness, she is also the only character in the book who asks, to someone's face, what she has done.

Australia is an off-stage cash machine. The postcolonial question here is concrete: Magwitch's fortune comes from sheep in New South Wales, and the people already living on that land, the conditions transported convicts worked under, the actual history of the colony, get not one word — it is simply the direction money arrives from. Said opens Culture and Imperialism on exactly this point; the Australian novelist Peter Carey retold the story from Magwitch's side in Jack Maggs (1997).

The author himself. Two years before this book, in 1858, Dickens separated from Catherine, his wife of twenty-two years and the mother of his ten children, and published a public statement in his own magazine defending his conduct; his relationship with the young actress Ellen Ternan was kept out of sight for the rest of his life. These are checkable facts, and separate from the value of the work, but a reader is entitled to them: the man who wrote Joe's forbearance so well that it makes people cry has a poor domestic record.

Ten sentences that stay

① The fuse of the whole book is a stolen pie: one child's kindness, remembered for twenty years by a man nobody had ever been kind to, converted into a fortune large enough to ruin him.

② "Great expectations" is not ambition; it is an inheritance waiting offstage. The question is whether where money comes from determines what it weighs.

③ Pip's fall does not begin with the spending. It begins the day he is eight and cries in a yard about his own hands and boots.

④ Nobody in the novel ever says Miss Havisham is the benefactor; those three hundred pages of illusion are manufactured voluntarily by the reader and the hero together.

⑤ Same case, same crime: Compeyson seven years, Magwitch fourteen. The difference was a coat and an accent.

⑥ Miss Havisham spent twenty years setting a trap, and the only targets it hit were herself and the girl she raised.

⑦ "I am what you have made me" is the shortest invoice in the book, and it is handed from daughter to mother.

⑧ Everything Magwitch wanted in return came down to one hand holding his — and he got it, in prison.

⑨ What destroys Pip is not malice but a kindness he can neither refuse nor repay, which is far harder to handle than a villain.

⑩ On the day he comes back to start again, the forge has flowers over the door: Joe and Biddy marry that morning. Not even repentance is rewarded — which is the most believable stroke in the book.
