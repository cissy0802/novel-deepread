Novel Deep-Read · F. Scott Fitzgerald · No.9

The Great Gatsby

F. Scott Fitzgerald · 1925 · Long Island · summer 1922

中文 →

Late at night on the north shore of Long Island. A young man who has just moved in stands on his lawn and watches a figure come out of the enormous house next door, whose lights have all gone out.
The man doesn't look at him, and doesn't look back at the garden behind him where a party has just broken up. He walks down to the water and stretches both arms toward the dark bay, trembling a little, as if reaching for something. The young man follows his line of sight: across the water there is nothing but a single small green light at the end of a dock.
By the end of this summer, that man will be dead in his own swimming pool, and almost no one will come to the funeral.

This page spoils the entire novel—where Gatsby actually came from, how he got his money, what Daisy says that afternoon at the Plaza, who the yellow car kills, who is driving it. The value of this book was never in "how it ends": Fitzgerald tells you in chapter one that the man is finished. The value is that once you know the ending and look again, you find the answer was in plain sight all along and you filed it as scenery.

In one sentence

A poor farmer's son from North Dakota renames himself at seventeen, makes a large fortune in a bootlegging operation nobody will describe in detail, buys a mansion on Long Island directly across the water from the woman he loved five years earlier, and throws an open party every week on the chance that she will one day walk in; she does walk in—and then, on the hottest afternoon of the year, she drives his car into her husband's mistress and keeps going; he takes the blame for her, and the next day the dead woman's husband shoots him in his own pool. Almost no one comes to the funeral.

Coordinates

F. Scott Fitzgerald (1896–1940), from St. Paul, Minnesota, became famous overnight at twenty-three with his first novel, This Side of Paradise, and immediately married Zelda Sayre, who had broken off their engagement because he had no money. The Great Gatsby is his third novel, published in April 1925, about fifty thousand words—half the length of either of the first two.

It sold badly. Something over twenty thousand copies in his lifetime; the second printing was still not sold out when he died of a heart attack in Hollywood in 1940. What rescued it was the Second World War: around 1945 the Armed Services Editions put well over a hundred thousand paperback copies into soldiers' pockets, and only then did it enter the classroom.

Its slot in literary history is precise: this is the book in which "the American dream" stopped being an advertising slogan and became something you could put on a table and dissect. Before it, self-invention was an inspirational story. After it, a self-invention story has to answer a question first—the thing you want to buy back with the money, can money buy it?

The story

Act One · Early summer 1922: thirty yards of water

The narrator is Nick Carraway—midwesterner, Yale, veteran of the war, who came home and found the Midwest too narrow to breathe in and moved east in the spring of 1922 to learn the bond business. The small house he rents in West Egg is jammed up against a mansion built as a factual imitation of some town hall in Normandy: tower, ivy, marble pool. The owner's name is Gatsby.

The geography is the skeleton of this book. West Egg and East Egg face each other across a narrow strip of water. East Egg is old money: inherited estates, stables, decorous white palaces. West Egg is new money: people who made it themselves, people from show business, people whose fortunes have no explanation, in houses that are bigger and louder. Thirty yards of water is the one wall in this novel that nobody crosses.

Nick drives over to East Egg for dinner at the house of his distant cousin Daisy Buchanan. Her husband Tom was a Yale football star who peaked at twenty-one, is rich in a way that requires no justification, and is stupid with total confidence—he has just read a book about the white race being swamped and is relaying it earnestly. Halfway through dinner the phone rings and Daisy's friend Jordan Baker, a professional golfer, says under her breath: it's the woman in New York. Daisy laughs it off and talks about her two-year-old daughter—how she cried when she heard it was a girl, and how she hopes the child grows up to be a fool, "a beautiful little fool," because that is the best thing a girl can be in this world.

Driving home, Nick sees his neighbor for the first time, standing on the lawn with his arms out toward the bay. Across the water there is nothing but a green light. Seed ①

Act Two · The valley of ashes, and a party every week

The road from West Egg into New York has to cross the valley of ashes—waste ground built out of the city's burnt coal, where ash grows into ridges, into the shapes of houses, into the shapes of men. Above it stands a faded billboard left behind by a long-bankrupt oculist: the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg, blue retinas a yard high, mounted on an enormous pair of yellow spectacles, looking down on all that ash. Seed ②

At the edge of the valley is a failing garage. Its owner, George Wilson, has been bleached by the ash; his wife Myrtle is Tom's mistress. Tom takes her into the city to drink, and when she starts chanting Daisy's name he breaks her nose with the flat of his hand. Then the drinking continues.

Then there are Gatsby's parties. Every weekend all summer, an orchestra from dusk to dawn, and almost nobody is invited—people simply drive over. None of them know the host, and every one of them has a theory about him: he killed a man, he was a German spy, he was at Oxford. At one party Nick wanders into the library and finds a drunk in enormous owl-eyed glasses staring at the shelves. The man grabs him: these books are real! Then he points out the finer detail—the pages haven't been cut. Not one of them has been read. He is full of admiration: the man knew where to stop. Seed ③

The host himself turns out to be in his early thirties, with an English tic of a phrase—"old sport"—and a smile that, while it's on you, makes you feel the entire world has taken your side; when it goes, he is a young man from nowhere choosing his words a shade too carefully.

As the party breaks up, a coupé goes into the ditch and a wheel is sheared clean off. The drunk who climbs out contemplates the wheel, then suggests he might try driving anyway; when someone asks who was at the wheel, a second drunk emerges from the back seat and says it was him. Seed ④ That summer Nick also starts seeing Jordan. She drives so close to some workmen that she nearly takes a button off one of them, and Nick tells her she's a rotten driver. She says it doesn't matter—other people are careful. "It takes two to make an accident."

Act Three · Tea in the rain, and a closet full of shirts

Gatsby begins working on Nick, and volunteers a biography on the drive into the city: only son of wealthy midwestern people, all dead now, educated at Oxford, then some years drifting through the capitals of Europe collecting jewels and hunting, then the war, where he tried very hard to die. Nick nearly laughs; the whole thing sounds torn out of a cheap magazine. Then Gatsby produces a medal from Montenegro and a photograph taken in a quad at Oxford, and one of the young men in it is unmistakably him. Every exhibit is genuine and the entire story is false. His business associate is Meyer Wolfshiem, an elderly gambler whose cufflinks are human molars, and who fixed the 1919 World Series.

The real explanation comes from Jordan. Louisville, 1917: Daisy is eighteen, and one particular lieutenant looks at her in a way that makes her feel she is the center of the world. He ships out; she promises to wait. But the war goes on and on and she is still young. In June 1919 she is to marry Tom Buchanan of Chicago, a man who gives her a string of pearls worth three hundred and fifty thousand dollars. The evening before the wedding Jordan finds her lying on her bed, drunk, a letter crushed in one hand and the pearls in the other, saying: tell them Daisy's changed her mind. They put her in a cold bath; the letter comes apart in the water. The next day she put on the dress, walked downstairs, and got married without another tremor. Seed ⑤

The favor Jordan has come to ask is Gatsby's: would Nick invite Daisy to tea, and not mention that Gatsby will be there. This man bought the house across the bay and threw parties for two years for exactly one thing—a way of meeting her that would look like an accident. He could not knock on the door, so he built a palace opposite it.

It rains that day. He sends a man to cut Nick's grass, fills the house with flowers, arrives two hours early, white in the face. Standing against the mantelpiece in a posture of manufactured ease, he knocks the defunct clock off it with his elbow and catches it in a scramble. Nick says never mind, it was a broken clock anyway. The best joke in the novel is right there: the first thing he reaches for, he knocks time over.

Nick makes himself scarce for half an hour. When he comes back the rain has stopped, Daisy's face is streaked, and Gatsby is glowing. He walks her through his house and ends at a closet of shirts sent over from England, throwing them out one at a time—linen, heavy silk, fine flannel, coral and apple-green and lavender—until the pile on the bed keeps climbing. Then Daisy puts her face down in the shirts and starts to cry, saying, muffled in the cloth, that she has never seen such—such beautiful shirts.

After dark he stands at the window and shows her the light that burns all night at the end of her dock. Payoff ① ↑ And Fitzgerald immediately adds one of the coldest sentences in the book: the colossal significance of that light was gone forever. That first-act gesture toward the black water only looked like lyricism; it was actually a man taking a bearing on something measurable. With Daisy standing beside him, the green light turns back into a dock light, and his count of enchanted objects goes down by one. The tragedy of the novel is finished here; the remaining four chapters are the execution.

Act Four · James Gatz, and the hottest day of the year

Only now will Fitzgerald give you the truth. Gatsby's name is James Gatz, of North Dakota, son of unsuccessful farm people he never for a single day accepted as his parents. At seventeen he rowed out to warn a yacht anchored over a dangerous shoal on Lake Superior that a half-hour of wind would finish it. The owner was Dan Cody, an old rake who had made a mining fortune; the moment he came aboard, the boy renamed himself Jay Gatsby. He spent five years going around the continent on that yacht, learning how rich men dress and speak. Cody left him twenty-five thousand dollars, and Cody's mistress took all of it with a legal document. He acquired the entire manner and not one dollar.

Then the war, and Louisville. He was a lieutenant with no money, no family, no prospects, wearing a uniform—the one form of respectability this country hands out free. He let the girl in the white dress believe he was of her own kind, and then he began to believe it himself. He had five months at Oxford after the armistice; by the time he got back she was married. Where the money came from the book gives only leads: Wolfshiem's connections, a chain of side-street drugstores selling alcohol under Prohibition. His way of getting money and the Buchanan way of inheriting it are both dirty; the difference is that the Buchanan money has been laundered by three generations.

Daisy comes to one of the parties and is appalled by it—by the people who came without being asked, by the loud unregulated pleasure of it. Gatsby's response is not to scale down but to escalate. Nick tells him you can't repeat the past, and Gatsby is almost incredulous:

"Can't repeat the past? … Why of course you can!"Chapter 6 · Gatsby to Nick

What he wants is for Daisy to say, in front of Tom, that she never loved him—and then for the two of them to go back to Louisville, start from her front parlor, and cancel the five years since 1917. He does not want to marry her. He wants to reverse time. To that end he fires his entire staff and replaces them with people Wolfshiem sends, because Daisy has started coming over in the afternoons and he wants no talk. The parties stop; the house that blazed for two summers goes dark.

Then comes the hottest day of the year. Lunch in East Egg, the rooms stifling. While Tom is out of the room getting drinks, Daisy looks at Gatsby in front of everyone and says: "You always look so cool." She has said she loves him, using a remark about the weather. Tom comes back in time to see her face. No evidence required; one sentence and he knows everything. He decides on the spot that they are all going into town. On the way he stops for gas at Wilson's garage, and Wilson is white as chalk—he has just discovered his wife has another man, though he has no idea who. In that one minute Tom understands that his wife and his mistress are both walking out on him on the same day.

New York, the Plaza, a stifling suite; below, a wedding is going on in the ballroom, and Mendelssohn's march keeps drifting up. Tom opens fire—what exactly are you? Gatsby holds, then plays the card he has been holding for five years: your wife has never loved you. He wants Daisy to say it out loud. She says it. Then Tom presses in with their history—the day he carried her so she wouldn't get her shoes wet, the small things Gatsby wasn't there for. And Daisy's voice collapses:

"I did love him once—but I loved you too."Chapter 7 · Daisy at the Plaza

Payoff ⑤ ↑ Jordan's wedding-eve story back in act three sounded like gossip; it was the foundation under that half-sentence. She had already made this exact choice once—the letter dissolving in the bathwater, the pearls picked back up, the walk downstairs the next morning. Nobody held her hand down. The Plaza only makes her make the same choice a second time, this time with Gatsby in the room. What he is asking her to say is a lie; she can't tell it, not because she is too honest but because those five years actually happened to her. The craft here is that Fitzgerald never lets her betray anyone—he lets her tell half a truth, and half a truth is more lethal than any betrayal.

Tom finds the crack and drives the last nail in: this "Mr. Gatsby" is a bootlegger. Daisy pulls further and further back, and the more Gatsby argues the more he sounds like the man Tom is describing. "Mr. Nobody from Nowhere" was never going to walk out of a room with old money's wife while old money watched. Tom wins so completely that he starts to enjoy it—he sends Daisy home in Gatsby's cream-colored car, with Gatsby, which is the cruelest gesture in the novel: you are not a threat any more; drive her home, I don't mind.

Dusk, the drive back, the valley of ashes. Upstairs at the garage, Wilson has locked his wife in. Myrtle sees the yellow car that passed that afternoon, is certain Tom is in it, runs out and plants herself in the road. The car doesn't stop. It doesn't even slow. It hits her, wavers, and accelerates away.

Payoff ④ ↑ This collision did not fall out of the sky. The coupé with its wheel torn off, the drunk who didn't know he'd been driving, Jordan's line about it taking two to make an accident—all of it was played for laughs. Fitzgerald's method is to rehearse carelessness as comedy and then let the same gesture kill somebody. Jordan's joke turns over here: when the accident finally comes, the careless one drives away and the careful one dies.

Outside the Buchanans' house Nick finds Gatsby hiding in the shrubbery. His first concern isn't himself: he wants to know whether Tom will do something to Daisy over the afternoon, and he intends to stand there all night. As for who was driving—he pauses—it was Daisy, but he'll say it was him. Nick goes around the back and looks through a gap in the kitchen blind: Tom and Daisy sitting across from each other, a plate of cold fried chicken untouched between them, their hands together. They aren't happy and they aren't fighting. They are conferring. Nick goes back out to the road, where the man is still standing in the moonlight—watching over nothing at all.

Act Five · The pool, and a funeral nobody attends

The next day Gatsby hasn't slept and is still waiting for Daisy's call. He tells the servants to drain the pool—unused all summer—then changes his mind and says he'll swim today.

Meanwhile Wilson has sat up all night in the valley of ashes, has forced out of his wife the fact of the other man, and stands at the window telling the enormous eyes behind the yellow spectacles that God sees everything. His neighbor tells him it's an advertisement. Payoff ② ↑ Doctor Eckleburg was a joke the first time he appeared—a ruined oculist's sign over a wasteland he could not treat. The beauty of the move is that the symbol isn't applied by the author; it is recognized by a character: exactly one deranged husband in this novel mistakes a billboard for God, and Fitzgerald adds no comment at all.

Wilson finds Tom, and Tom tells him whose car it was. That afternoon Gatsby is floating on an air mattress. One shot, then a second. When Nick gets there the mattress is still turning slowly and there is a thin red thread on the water; Wilson's body is in the grass a little way off.

The next three days are the hardest part of the book, because nothing happens. Daisy and Tom left the same day with their luggage—no address, no flowers, no telegram. Wolfshiem writes to say a man in business can't get mixed up in this. Out of the hundreds who came all summer, one drunk telephones to ask about a pair of shoes he left behind.

Those who come are: a few servants, the postman, the minister, Nick, and an old man who has traveled from Minnesota—Henry C. Gatz, Gatsby's father, shaking in the rain in a cheap long coat and unable to stop being proud. He shows Nick a battered western his boy owned as a teenager. On the flyleaf, in the hand of a sixteen-year-old James Gatz, is a schedule: rise at six, dumbbell exercise, study electricity, work, practice elocution and poise. Under it, a column headed "General Resolves": no more wasting time, read one improving book a week, save five dollars a week, be better to parents. It is the only page in the novel with no lie on it.

It rains hard at the grave. One car arrives late and a man gets out in enormous owl-eyed glasses. Payoff ③ ↑ He has not appeared once in three months and nobody knows how he heard. He stands in the mud, takes off his glasses and wipes them, and delivers the last verdict anyone passes on Gatsby: "The poor son-of-a-bitch." His admiration in the library was never mockery—he saw at a glance that the house was a set, and saw at the same glance how seriously it had been built. The one man who understood it was an illusion is the only one who grieves for the man who made it; the hundreds who genuinely believed there was drink and music and friendship here did not come.

Before leaving the East, Nick runs into Tom on the street. Tom is unrepentant: I told Wilson whose car it was—what was I supposed to do? Nick does not tell him Daisy was driving. His verdict on the two of them is the most famous conviction in American fiction: they were careless people, who smashed up things and people and then retreated back into their money and let other people clean up.

On his last night Nick lies on Gatsby's emptied beach and thinks about Dutch sailors three hundred years ago seeing this continent for the first time: a fresh green breast of new world, a thing exactly commensurate with the human capacity for wonder. Gatsby felt that when he first picked out the green light.

"So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past."Chapter 9 · the last line of the novel

Put the five acts together and the fuse is not on Long Island and not in the valley of ashes. The fuse is an afternoon in Louisville in 1917, when a lieutenant with no money let a girl with money believe he was one of her own kind. He knew it was false, and then he started believing it; everything after—the new name, the liquor, the house, the parties, the shirts, the light—is paperwork filed retroactively on that afternoon. Which means the thing he wanted was never ahead of him: he spent everything he had running forward in order to get back.

Three slow-motion replays

1 · The shirts: handing an entire breakdown to a pile of cloth

What makes this scene work is that Fitzgerald refuses to explain it. The novel supplies almost nothing of Daisy's interior—she is the one being looked at, and all we get is her voice, her movements, the water on her face. So the interpretation is handed wholesale to the reader. Is she crying because the shirts really are beautiful? Because five years went nowhere? Or the more uncomfortable possibility—because all this could be hers now? The less information you are given, the more your reading of it is a test of you rather than of her. And the emotional peak of the chapter is placed on a purely material object: not an embrace, not a line of dialogue, a heap of silk and flannel.

2 · The Plaza: a wedding playing downstairs

Chapter seven is the only time every principal is locked in one room, and the first thing Fitzgerald reaches for is weather: the hottest day of the year, hot enough that nobody can tell whether they're furious or merely overheated. Heat is a pressure gauge and also an alibi—everyone gets to blame the temperature for losing control. Then the almost vicious detail: a wedding in the ballroom below, the march floating up in gusts. A marriage being made, scoring a marriage being taken apart.

The hardest stroke is structural. You expect the fight to be decided by who is right, or who is richer, or who can expose whom. It is decided by whether one person can say a sentence she doesn't believe. Tom doesn't win on money. He wins because he owns five years of actual life—the puddle, the shoes, the man who carried her. Those five years are precisely what Gatsby needs erased. He isn't beaten by a rival. He's beaten by the calendar.

3 · The last page: rewriting one man's failure as the grammar of a continent

For eight and a half chapters the camera is locked on Nick. On the last page it pulls back three hundred years to Dutch sailors sighting Long Island when it was still fresh and green. The same color: first a private lamp one man watches across the water, then an entire continent's promise to everyone. And the syntax of the final sentence performs the thing it describes: the sentence moves forward (we beat on) while the motion is backward (borne back ceaselessly). A forward verb and a backward outcome in one breath—that is the shape of the whole book.

The engine of this book: an honest man worth doubting

Where a novel like The Three-Body Problem runs on its premise, this one runs on its narrator. Everything passes through Nick Carraway, and that layer is tinted.

One: Gatsby is assembled, not delivered. Everything we get about him comes from party rumor, his own fake biography, Jordan's secondhand account, Wolfshiem's reminiscence, and his father's battered book—not one line of it from an omniscient narrator. The reader's route to Gatsby is identical to Nick's: rumor first, then self-report, and only at the very end, evidence.

Two: Nick makes his own reliability a question on page one. He tells you his father taught him not to judge people, and that he is one of the few honest people he has ever known. Then this honest man brokers an affair, knows Daisy was driving and says nothing to the police, to Wilson, or to Tom, and finally writes a bootlegger up as a martyr—the word "great" in the title is his. The book never tells you whether to trust him. How a story is told and what the story is are the same question.

Three: the order was engineered. In his correspondence with his editor, Maxwell Perkins, Perkins told him after reading the draft that Gatsby was too vague as a person and that his business was left too murky; Fitzgerald broke the biography apart and redistributed it, and put the solid piece—James Gatz and the yacht—after the reunion. The effect is decisive: the reader falls for the man before learning who he is. Told in order, the same facts make a swindler's exposure. Told this way, they make a monument.

What it forces you to think about

QUESTION ONE
Betting a life on one afternoon five years gone: is that purity, or a very well-dressed stupidity?

Take the "stupidity" side first, at full strength, because the beauty of the prose keeps burying it. What Gatsby loves is not Daisy—their total time alone together in this novel might not add up to a few dozen hours. He loves the version of himself he invented in 1917, and Daisy is the screen that version has to be projected on. The proof is the demand: he doesn't ask her to leave with him, he asks her to declare that five years did not occur. That isn't loving a person; that's requiring a person to cooperate in the doctoring of a record. He can't even process the fact that she has a daughter—the child appears once and is carried out, because there's no slot for her in the plan. Using a real human being as a prop in your own story is total self-absorption, however beautifully it is dressed.

Now the other side at full strength. All love contains projection; only the concentration varies, and if projection disqualifies you then no romance in history passes. And Gatsby has one property nobody else in the book has: he actually paid for what he said. He said he'd be rich and got rich; he said he'd wait and waited five years; at the end he took the charge, entirely voluntarily. Set that against the others. Tom has money, a name, an education, and won't pay for a single true sentence. Daisy cries with complete sincerity and then packs. Nick calls himself honest and never says the thing out loud. In a house full of people who won't settle any bill, the con man is the only one who settles his. The "great" in the title is not purely ironic—which is exactly what makes it interesting.

Bring it home: nearly everyone is holding one "if only, back then." The question this book asks isn't the self-help one about letting go. It's harder: is the thing you never let go of an actual possibility, or a fixed point you need to keep existing so that your present self still makes sense? Gatsby's tragedy isn't that he failed to get Daisy. It's that he had her for three months and found the real woman couldn't carry the weight he had loaded onto her. A realized dream devalues on contact: while the light burned it was the only meaning he had; with her standing next to him it was a bulb.

QUESTION TWO
Does Daisy deserve the contempt—or: how many choices did she have in 1919?

A century of readers has taken her as the villain: shallow, cowardly, kills a woman and lets someone else take it. That verdict comes too easily, so take the opposite at full strength first. She was eighteen in 1917 and twenty in 1919. For a woman of that class in that decade, the only career decision available in a lifetime is whom to marry—and her line about the beautiful little fool is the sharpest diagnosis of that condition anyone in the book produces, which is not how fools talk. The lieutenant she was waiting for was overseas, and his letters were promises, and promises don't convert into a life; Tom was an entire answer, available immediately. The night before the wedding she wept, threw the pearls away and tried to back out—which means she knew exactly what she was doing, and also that she was talked around. Calling "a twenty-year-old choosing the only road on offer" a moral stain is demanding of her a freedom her position could not contain. And at the Plaza, what Gatsby wants is a public denial of five years of her own life and of the child she bore. She cannot say it. That's honesty, not cowardice.

Now the prosecution at full strength, without emotion. The marriage can be charged to the era. But not stopping the car is not an era's decision; it is something a person does in two seconds—and letting another person take the charge, then packing and moving away without so much as flowers, is a whole sequence of decisions with plenty of time to reverse any of them. Nor is she powerless: she has money, a name, lawyers, and a man willing to do anything for her. Structural constraint explains a person's situation; it does not automatically absolve every act performed inside that situation—otherwise the phrase "the powerful" never finds a referent at all.

In ordinary life the question is this: when someone's bad behavior really is partly explained by their circumstances, where on the dial does responsibility stop? Stop too early and you're demanding sainthood from everyone. Stop too late and you have abolished responsibility as a concept. The book supplies no dial. It just stacks the evidence high on both sides—and both stacks were written by the same man.

QUESTION THREE
What actually stops Gatsby: class, or time?

The standard reading is the death of the American dream: a poor boy earns all the money there is and still can't get through the door, because old money never admits new money. The evidence is hard—"Mr. Nobody from Nowhere," Daisy's embarrassment at his party, and not one person from East Egg who treats him as a peer. If a society tells everybody "you can become anyone" while keeping one line drawn by birth that money cannot move, that society has told an enormous lie, and Gatsby is the man it kills with precision.

But push that reading all the way and it hits something awkward: even if Tom had exposed nothing, Daisy still could not have said it. Gatsby didn't want to marry her—marriage is purchasable with money and patience. He wanted the years 1917 to 1922 removed from her. No arrangement of class can supply that; the richest man alive cannot buy it. The thing he needed demolished was not thirty yards of water but a calendar. The colder layer of this novel: what people actually want is usually located in time, and every institutional explanation—class, birth, breeding—is standing in as the defendant for a problem that has no solution.

Both readings hold at once, and each one strengthens the other: precisely because that class line is real, a man can mistake it for the whole obstacle and believe that clearing it will also reverse the clock. He misdiagnosed a problem about time as a problem about money, then executed a flawless ten-year plan against a misprinted question.

This is not far from any of us. "I just needed better luck." "Once I've saved enough, then…" Translating a loss located in time into a goal denominated in resources is the most common consolation there is, because resources respond to effort and time doesn't. The cruelty of this book is that it lets its hero actually save the money, and then leaves him standing exactly where he was.

The recurring obsession

Line his books up and the same fixation keeps surfacing: the object of desire devalues the instant it is obtained, and people have to live on it anyway. In the story "Winter Dreams" (1922), a young man who started out as a caddie makes his money and years later hears that the girl who once made him frantic has faded into an ordinary housewife—what he has actually lost is not her, but his own capacity to ache for her. In Tender Is the Night (1934) a promising doctor marries his rich patient and is hollowed out inch by inch by her money: effectively the sequel to Gatsby—what happens if he does get through the door.

The other half is his own ledger. Zelda broke the engagement in 1919 because he had no money; he went home to Minnesota, shut himself in, finished This Side of Paradise, and only after it made him famous did she marry him. "Prove you're rich and you may have the girl" is a road he walked himself—and completed. He also knew what lies past the finish: the twenty years that followed were a slow, public, mutually consuming disaster. This is not an outsider's moral fable. It's a report filed by someone who came out from the inside.

Criticism, honestly

  • Wolfshiem is an unmistakable antisemitic caricature. The accent, the greed, the human molars for cufflinks—every stroke lands on the most fashionable Jewish cartoon of the period. Defenders point out that the model was a real gambler; but binding an ethnic marker to a bundle of vices in one character is itself the problem, and a real-life model does not dissolve it.
  • Race, and the narrator's own eye. Tom's white-supremacist panic in chapter one clearly glances at a bestselling racist tract of the day, and the novel stages it as Tom's stupidity—that much is plain satire. But the description of a carload of Black passengers on the bridge in chapter four is written in a jeering register, and that is not Tom's point of view, it's Nick's. The eye being used to criticize prejudice carries the prejudice of its decade.
  • The women. Daisy is given almost no interior, Jordan is written as an habitual liar, and Myrtle's body is consumed repeatedly and then turned into a corpse with real brutality. One line of criticism holds that the novel treats women as terrain for male desire; another holds that Daisy's silencing is the subject. Both find support in the text.
  • The plot leans on coincidence. Myrtle runs out at exactly that moment, into exactly the car Daisy is driving; Wilson gets the owner's name from exactly the right man. Reviewers said as much at the time—H. L. Mencken called it, in form, no more than a glorified anecdote, while praising the prose extravagantly. The defense is that the coincidences are the theme; the machinery still shows.
  • Part of its canonical status was manufactured by history. What carried it into the canon was the postwar rediscovery and then the school system. "The great American novel" is a verdict handed down jointly by literary history and an education system—not a status the book was born with; one consequence is that it is taught too narrowly, with nearly every classroom running the same "critique of the American dream" reading.

Ten sentences to take away

1 · This is not a love story. It's a story about treating time as a purchasable commodity—what he wants to buy is not Daisy, it's 1917.

2 · The tragedy is over by chapter five: the moment she stands beside him, the green light turns back into a bulb.

3 · The clock he knocks off the mantel and catches is the book's own footnote to itself.

4 · Doctor Eckleburg's eyes are frightening not because they are God, but because a man losing his mind needs them to be.

5 · "It takes two to make an accident" turns over later: when it happens, the careless one drives away and the careful one dies.

6 · The owl-eyed man finds the books real and the pages uncut—the only person who sees through the set, and the only guest who stands in the rain at the grave.

7 · Daisy can't say "I never loved you" out of honesty, not cowardice; those five years happened. Gatsby loses to the calendar, not to Tom.

8 · That schedule written at sixteen on the flyleaf of a cheap western is the only page in the novel without a lie, and the hardest one to read.

9 · Nick calling himself one of the few honest people he knows is the first test the book hands the reader, not an introduction.

10 · The last sentence moves forward while the motion goes backward: a forward verb, a backward result, in one breath. That's the shape of the book.
