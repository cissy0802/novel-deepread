Novels, Deep-Read · Mario Vargas Llosa · No.79

The Green House

La casa verde · Mario Vargas Llosa · novel · 1966 · English translation by Gregory Rabassa, 1968

中文 →

The launch noses upriver against brown water, and the sun has turned its tin cabin into a griddle. Aboard are a sergeant, a handful of soldiers, and two nuns in white habits, sweating under their veils.
They are heading for an Aguaruna village. Not to fight, not to preach — to bring back two little girls.
When they tie up, the men of the village are gone. A few women stand in the clearing and watch the boat empty onto the bank.

Full spoilers ahead — who the orphan raised by the nuns turns out to be, how the sergeant in the jungle relates to Lituma of Piura, how the first Green House burned and who was hidden in its tower, where the smuggler Fushía ends his days, and how all of it collides forty years on at a funeral. Nearly all of the book's suspense lies in who is who; there is no discussing it without saying so.

In one sentence

Two ends of Peru — Piura, a sand-blown city on the northern coastal desert, and a jungle outpost on the upper Amazon that is little more than a mission, a garrison and a few trading posts — are stitched together across forty years by five lives, and at the center of the seam is an Indian girl whom the nuns "brought in" to be civilized and who ends up working in a desert brothel called the Green House; at every step that led her there, somebody believed they were doing what was best for her.

Where it stands

Mario Vargas Llosa (1936–2025) was Peruvian, one of the four headline names of the Latin American Boom, and the 2010 Nobel laureate in literature. The Green House was his second novel, published in 1966; the following year it won the first Rómulo Gallegos Prize, and his acceptance speech in Caracas — "Literature Is Fire" — became a manifesto of its own.

The book has two real-world sources. As a boy he lived in Piura, where an actual green brothel stood out on the sand beyond the town. In 1958 he joined an expedition to the upper Marañón, where he saw the mission girls taken from their tribes and heard about a tribal headman whom the authorities had flogged. In the history of the Latin American novel, it occupies the "structure" slot: while his contemporaries were still writing linear family chronicles, he took five stories apart and fed them through one another.

The story

One fact about reading it first: the novel is not told in the order below. Its five strands are cut into dozens of pieces, time jumps back and forth, and sometimes a single stretch of dialogue braids together two conversations held years apart. What follows is the puzzle reassembled. Readers of the original have to do the assembling themselves — which is both the difficulty and the point.

Act I · A smear of green in the desert

The earliest piece takes place in Piura in the first half of the twentieth century, a city ringed by sand where every wind fills the streets with it. One day a stranger arrives called Anselmo. Nobody knows where he comes from, and he won't say. He spends freely, plays the harp, and soon has the town's men on his side.

Out on the sand across the river he builds a house, and the whole town watches it go up. When it's done he paints it entirely green. Seed ①In a landscape of dun and ochre, it is a loud color that belongs to some other place. Only when it opens does Piura learn what it is: a brothel, with drink, music and women brought in from elsewhere. The men wade across the river at night, their wives seethe at home, and the parish priest, Father García, thunders from the pulpit that it is the devil's house.

Meanwhile a washerwoman in town, Juana Baura, is raising a foundling named Antonia — Toñita. As an infant she was the sole survivor when bandits murdered her parents on a desert road; left on the sand, she had her eyes and tongue torn out by vultures. She lived, blind and mute, and the poor woman brought her up into a quiet adolescent.

Then Toñita vanishes. The whole town searches. No one imagines that Anselmo has taken her and hidden her in the tower at the top of the Green House — music and drunks all night below, and above them a girl who can neither see nor speak. Anselmo will insist, later, that he loved her.

Toñita becomes pregnant and dies giving birth. The baby, a girl, survives. At Toñita's burial the anger the town has been swallowing for years finally breaks: Father García leads a crowd across the river and the Green House is burned to the ground. Seed ②It is the priest's victory and the dividing line of Anselmo's life; as far as anyone can tell, the account between the two men is closed.

Anselmo, left with nothing, stays on in Piura as a drunken itinerant musician, playing his harp in the bars of the Mangachería, the slum on the north side of town, for decades. His daughter grows up; people call her la Chunga.

Act II · The orphan at the mission

Cut to the other end of the map. On a tributary of the upper Amazon lies Santa María de Nieva: a mission, a police post, a governor, a few traders, and all around them the forest and its tribes.

The nuns at the mission have a job: bring in girls from the tribes and teach them Spanish, clothes, prayers and work — make them "civilized." The girls never come of their own accord, so soldiers go along on every trip. That is the opening scene. The nuns sincerely believe they are rescuing these children: to them, a girl left in the forest has nothing ahead but nakedness, an early marriage and a lifetime in the mud.

One of the mission girls is Bonifacia. She was brought there very young and grew up there; she is no longer quite of the tribe, and she will never be taken for white. The nuns treat her as a success story, make her a helper, and put her in charge of the new arrivals.

One night the two newly captured girls are locked in the pantry. Bonifacia goes in, speaks to them in their own language, feeds them — and then opens the door. In the confusion, other girls at the mission run off too.

The nuns interrogate her. Why? She can't say, or rather what she says makes no sense to them: she looked at those two girls and something came back to her. To the Mothers it is pure ingratitude — after all these years of care, she has gone over to the savages. Bonifacia is thrown out.

She is taken in by a couple in town: the river pilot Adrián Nieves and his woman, Lalita. At their house she meets the sergeant in charge of the local police post. Seed ③Throughout the jungle chapters he is almost never called anything but "the sergeant." He comes from the desert coast, and he has taken a liking to this silent girl. They marry.

Act III · Jum and Fushía: the price of rubber

The forest has soldiers, a governor and traders for one reason: rubber. The tribes tap it in the forest, the traders buy it, the traders set the price and keep the books — and the Indians are always in debt.

The headman of the Aguaruna village of Urakusa is Jum. Two young outsiders teach the villagers to keep accounts and tell them what rubber fetches downriver, and Urakusa decides to sell its own rubber collectively, cutting out the middlemen. That threatens one man in particular: Julio Reátegui, the governor, who is at once the state's official and a big boss in the very trade he is supposed to regulate.

Reátegui takes troops to Urakusa. Jum is captured, beaten and tortured in public, his head shaved — a punishment staged for every tribe on the river to see. On his way out Reátegui also takes a little girl from the village and, almost as an afterthought, hands her over to the nuns at Santa María de Nieva. Seed ④In that scene it is an incidental gesture, tucked between the whip and the razor, and it is easy to read straight past.

In the same river network someone is doing darker business. Fushía, a Japanese-Brazilian, broke out of a Brazilian jail and fled to Iquitos in Peru, where he smuggled for Reátegui; eventually he ran off with Reátegui's money and goods and with Lalita, then a teenager, and set himself up as king of an island deep in the forest. He raids the tribes for their rubber and their women and gathers a crew of desperadoes — among them the pilot Nieves.

Island life hardens Lalita. Fushía treats her brutally, and then he falls ill with something that makes his body start to rot away piece by piece. Lalita finally escapes with Nieves and they settle in Santa María de Nieva — the same couple who will later take in Bonifacia. So the door Bonifacia walks through after she is expelled from the mission belongs to people who had themselves escaped from Fushía's island.

Fushía's disease is leprosy. His men scatter. His old friend Aquilino, a boatman who has spent his life on these rivers, loads him into a small boat and floats him downstream for weeks to the leper colony at San Pablo. Lying in the bottom of the boat, Fushía tells Aquilino his life: the jailbreak, the smuggling, the island, the women, the money. What is left of the money goes on getting him quietly into the colony.

Nieves does not escape for good either. Fushía's old business catches up with him and he ends up in prison, and Lalita, with her children, starts over once again.

Act IV · The Unconquerables

Back in Piura, a generation has passed. In the Mangachería there is a gang of four layabouts who call themselves the Unconquerables (los Inconquistables): the León brothers, José and "the Monkey," plus Josefino and Lituma. They don't work; they drink, gamble, sing their own anthem, and at night they go to a brothel — a house also called the Green House, run by the grown-up Chunga, with a white-haired Anselmo in the band.

Then Lituma joins the police and leaves Piura.

When he comes back, he brings a wife he married in the jungle. Payoff ③ ↑Only now can the reader be sure: the man the jungle chapters kept calling "the sergeant" is the Lituma of the Piura chapters. The two strands have been running in parallel with nobody telling you they are two halves of one life; that passing detail — "he comes from the desert coast" — finally clicks. Vargas Llosa isn't hiding a solution so much as making the reader live through the fact that one man carries two names in two worlds: in the jungle he is uniformed authority, back in the Mangachería he is one of four bums.

Home again, Lituma has a reputation to keep up. One night at Chunga's Green House, a rich landowner named Seminario insults him in front of everyone. Two drunk men pull out a revolver and play Russian roulette — each in turn putting the gun to his own head and pulling the trigger. Seminario dies. Lituma is arrested and sent to prison in Lima.

Bonifacia, the girl from the forest, is left alone in Piura. She doesn't know the city or its rules; the only people she knows are her husband's friends. Josefino begins by "looking after" her and ends by taking her. She is carrying Lituma's child; Josefino forces her to get rid of it. After that, she is led into Chunga's Green House.

There she gets a new name: la Selvática — "the jungle girl." Payoff ① ↑The swath of green Anselmo painted in the middle of the sand in Act I now carries its weight. It is a jungle color, carried into the desert by a man with no past; forty years later a woman who really did come out of the jungle is working under that same green. Piura has called the place the Green House for decades without anyone wondering why it was green.

Act V · The funeral

Lituma comes out of prison to find his wife has become la Selvática of the Green House. He doesn't take her away, and he doesn't leave her. The Unconquerables go on as before — living off what la Selvática earns. On that score, not one of the four is clean.

Old Anselmo is dying. Someone goes for a priest, and the one who comes is García — the man who led the crowd that burned the first Green House forty years earlier, now a cantankerous old man. Payoff ② ↑He gives his old enemy the last rites, and at the funeral he turns his temper on the layabouts gathered there, calling them idlers and parasites who live off a woman. The man who burned the house and the man who built it end up square at a deathbed. And in the scraps of information that surface around Anselmo's death, the reader catches a hint that this man who never spoke of his origins may himself have come out of the forest — though the novel never quite says so.

At the other end of the river, Aquilino goes back to San Pablo years later to visit Fushía. The man who once ruled an island has been eaten down by the disease until there is almost nothing left of him that looks human.

As for Bonifacia — Payoff ④ ↑by the time the puzzle is complete, the reader goes back and picks up that incidental gesture: the infant Reátegui carried off from Urakusa and handed to the nuns was Bonifacia. She is Jum's daughter. The novel never lets any character say this aloud; it simply becomes true in the gaps between the fragments. And so the night she opened the pantry door acquires an answer she herself could never have put into words: what was locked in there was the person she had originally been.

The fuse

Lay the fragments out by cause and effect and the fuse is that day in Urakusa. A column of troops sent out over the price of rubber produced two things on a single day: a headman shaved bald in public and a baby girl carried off on the side. The first was called punishment; the second was called charity. Every step that girl took afterward — the mission, the Nieves household, Lituma's marriage bed, Josefino's hands, Chunga's house — was explained by somebody as being for her own good, or as something she wanted, and not once was she actually asked. The rubber flowed down the rivers to the outside world; she flowed down the same route to the desert, and came to rest in a house painted the color of the jungle.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · The opening: a boatload of people sent to "collect" two girls

The novel opens without a word of explanation and simply drops you into that boat: heat, mosquitoes, sodden veils, the soldiers' idle talk, the sergeant's fatigue. The boat lands, the men are away, the women stand still, the soldiers step forward, two girls are taken. Not one character stops to say "this is wrong," and not one says "this is right"; the nuns talk about their work, the soldiers want to get back, the sergeant is reckoning the distance.

Its force comes from a flat point of view. Vargas Llosa puts the camera on the boat of the people carrying it out and renders an act of plunder through their tedium and small talk, so that the violence looks like routine — which is exactly what it was. Only much later does the reader realize the opening is not an isolated episode: every one of those girls is a future Bonifacia. The flatness of that first scene is the coldest stroke in the book.

Two · The pantry door

The scene where Bonifacia frees the girls is extremely sparing with information. We see her go in, hear her speak the tribal language, see her hand over food; then chaos and pursuit; then the nuns' interrogation. In the interrogation the Mothers say a great deal, quickly, full of reasons and wounded feelings; Bonifacia says little, in fragments, and never quite answers the question.

The technique here is to put the silent party at the moral center. Vargas Llosa won't explain Bonifacia; he gives her no interior monologue and lets her come across as clumsy, stubborn, unable to justify herself. The reader is forced to fill the gap — and only after the whole puzzle is assembled, once we know whose daughter she is, do we see that what she couldn't articulate wasn't a reason but an origin. A person's deepest motive is often the thing she has no words for.

Three · Fushía in the bottom of the boat

Almost all of Fushía's strand takes place in a small boat drifting downstream. In the present there are only two old men, the river, a little food and a worsening disease. But whenever Fushía talks, the past floods in — and not as "he remembered," but as old dialogue spliced directly into the present-tense passage. One line is Aquilino in the boat asking if it hurts; the next is Lalita years ago, fighting with him on the island; the next is back on the river.

The effect is to make a man's whole life and its end present at the same time. We hear how grand and how ruthless he once was while watching him decay in the bottom of the boat; every boast is marked down by the rot happening in the present tense. That is harsher than any moral about getting what you deserve: the novel passes no judgment at all. It simply puts two times on the same page.

The book's central device

One · Communicating vessels: two conversations flowing through one passage

Years later, in Letters to a Young Novelist, Vargas Llosa gave this technique a name: communicating vessels (vasos comunicantes) — like the glass tubes in a physics class that are joined at the base, so that pouring water into one changes the level in all the others. In fiction it means cutting up episodes set in different times and places and alternating them, even inside a single dialogue, so that each changes the meaning of the other.

In The Green House this isn't showing off; it's an argument. When the nuns' interrogation is cut together with some conversation in Piura years later, the reader sees for themselves the same logic operating in two places. The structure delivers a verdict the author refuses to state in any sentence. The cost is just as clear: for the first hundred pages you will be lost, with names popping up one after another and no signposts in time.

Two · One person, two names

The sergeant and Lituma; Bonifacia and la Selvática; Anselmo with no past. The most important people in the book each have a name and a face in two worlds, and the novel deliberately withholds the fact that they are one person. This is not a mystery-novel twist but a statement about identity: in a country carved up by desert, mountains and jungle, a person who moves from one place to another is renamed and repriced. The moment the reader realizes "these two are the same person" is the moment the reader understands how the country works.

Three · The houses

The title names a brothel, but there is a second house in the novel: the mission. One in the desert, one in the jungle; one takes in women to be sold, one takes in girls to be taught; one is burned by a priest, one is run by nuns. Vargas Llosa sets the two houses facing each other like mirrors — both take girls away from where they came from, both give them new names and new rules, both say this is a better life. Bonifacia goes from one house to the other with nothing between them but a few men's hands. The novel never says the mission equals the brothel; it simply moves the same girl through both and leaves the comparison to you.

What it makes you think about

One · Were the nuns rescuing those girls, or taking them?

The nuns ride a troop boat into tribal villages, take girls away, and teach them to read, dress and pray. They have no self-interest, and they truly believe life in the forest is harder. If you are sure someone else's way of life is bad for her, do you have the right to change it for her — even at gunpoint?

Make the nuns' case as strong as it goes. "Respect their culture" is easy to say, and the cost never falls on the one saying it. A girl in the forest may be married off very young, have no medicine when she falls ill, never learn to read, and be at the mercy of any trader's ledger — Urakusa got cheated precisely because it couldn't keep accounts. The nuns are offering literacy, medicine and a language to survive in the wider world. Leaving her alone looks like respect but in practice leaves her in a position built for exploitation. And the nuns aren't profiting; they live austere lives themselves and give everything to a fever-ridden outpost.

The other side: rescue presupposes that the rescued can say no. These girls were dragged onto a boat by soldiers while their mothers stood on the bank. The mission gives them a language and a set of rules but no place: white society won't accept them and the tribe is closed to them now. In the novel, the road out of the mission leads to a white family's kitchen or to the Green House. "Civilizing" swaps one form of domination for another, and it removes their capacity to resist: a girl who speaks Spanish and knows her prayers is more alone in front of Josefino than she ever would have been in the forest.

Where does the author stand? He doesn't turn a single nun into a villain, yet he lets the trajectory of Bonifacia's life answer for him. Bring it home: "I'm doing this for your own good" — how many times have you said it, and how many times has it been said to you? Did anyone ask the person it was supposedly for?

Two · When someone cannot speak, who gets to speak for her?

Toñita is blind and mute. Anselmo hides her in the tower and later says he loved her; the town calls it abduction and sin and burns his house down. In the whole affair, the only person who never says a word is Toñita herself.

Make the angry crowd's case as strong as it goes. A brothel keeper takes a blind, mute, utterly defenseless girl from her foster mother, shuts her away above a brothel, makes her pregnant, and she dies in childbirth. Whatever he felt, the shape of the act is predation. Love cannot exist where one side has no possible way to refuse; precisely because she cannot say no, others are obliged to say it for her. When Father García leads the crowd across the river, a city is expressing in the only way it has: this cannot be allowed.

The other side is no weaker: the crowd speaking for her knows her no better than Anselmo did. Before she was hidden away, the town's concern for her amounted to pity. Once she was dead, it used her name to burn down a house it had long wanted to burn — how much of that fury was for Toñita, and how much for finally having an excuse to go after the place that drew its husbands across the river at night? And Anselmo spends the rest of his life in a solitude that looks very much like penance. The novel gives Toñita not a single word precisely so that we notice both sides are speaking for her, and neither can prove it got her right.

Bring it home: an elderly parent with dementia, a baby who can't talk, a patient in a coma, someone at the center of a media storm who never says anything — when a person cannot speak, is whoever speaks for them protecting them, or borrowing their silence to say their own piece?

Three · Is cutting the story into pieces more honest, or more arrogant?

Vargas Llosa could easily have told this story in order. He chose to cut it up, shuffle it and hide identities. When a novel about the poor, about Indians and prostitutes, is written in a form that daunts most readers, is the form speaking for them — or turning them into a puzzle for the literary elite?

Make the "arrogance" case as strong as it goes. People like Bonifacia, Jum and Toñita have never been properly listened to in their lives, and when a novel finally turns to them it chops them into fragments for the reader to solve. The reader's attention is pulled toward structural games — is this sergeant Lituma? — rather than toward the suffering itself. Jum barely does anything in the book but get beaten and shout; his inner life is never opened. The more intricate the form, the more the characters become components. And the people who can enjoy this kind of puzzle are precisely the people furthest from this kind of suffering.

The other side: a linear story would lie. Told from the beginning, this is the decline of an unlucky girl, and the reader would blame her luck, one bad man, one wrong choice. Only by cutting Urakusa's whip, the mission's door and Piura's roulette game together can you see the net — where no single person is the culprit and everyone at each link feels they did nothing wrong. The fragments aren't a game; they are what the country actually looked like: what happened in the jungle, people in the desert never heard about. Making the reader assemble it means making the reader do, with their own hands, what nobody ever did for these people.

Bring it home: the world on your phone each day is fragments too — a war far away, a sale, a friend's dinner. Do the fragments make causes harder to see, or do they finally teach you that causes never line up neatly to find you?

Motif: what Vargas Llosa kept writing about

What Vargas Llosa returned to all his life is how power presses down, layer by layer, until it lands on the people with the least voice — the military academy, the barracks, the dictator, the colonial company, a different institution in each book. The Time of the Hero (1963) shows a military school training boys into dogs who tear at each other; The Green House swaps the institution for a mission, a police post and the rubber trade; Conversation in the Cathedral (1969) packs the whole Odría dictatorship into a talk in a bar and asks at what moment Peru was ruined.

The other obsession is the Amazon. After The Green House came The Storyteller (1987), in which a young white man walks into the forest and becomes a tribe's teller of tales; late in life, The Dream of the Celt (2010) went back to the rubber atrocities of the early twentieth century through Roger Casement, the Irishman who exposed them. From the punishment over rubber prices in The Green House onward, he kept asking the same thing: when "civilization" enters the forest, what exactly does it bring?

And one man walked out of this book into others: Lituma. He became a recurring figure in Vargas Llosa's fiction — a provincial policeman in Who Killed Palomino Molero? (1986), posted to the highlands in Death in the Andes (1993). The layabout who played Russian roulette in the Green House went on to serve, for decades, as the author's eyes in every corner of Peru.

Controversy and criticism

One: hard to read, and not always necessarily so. The most common complaint since publication is that the opening stretches have too many characters, no time markers, and dialogue whose speakers are unclear. Defenders say the difficulty is the book's argument; critics answer that some of the scrambling is scrambling for its own sake and could go without loss. Both have a point, and readers can judge for themselves.

Two: the Indians have bodies but no minds. Some Peruvian and Latin American critics point out that indigenous characters like Jum appear almost solely as victims and as objects of the gaze: he is beaten, he shouts, but he is never given the full interiority granted to Fushía or Lituma. One scholar went so far as to call Jum the book's "unthinkable Indian." Set that beside Vargas Llosa's later cultural positions — in essays he repeatedly argued for the modernization of indigenous societies — and the controversy grows.

Three: the women. Almost every woman in the book is a nun, a prostitute or a girl who has been carried off; their lives are pushed along by men and institutions, and Toñita is entirely voiceless. You can read that as exposure — the novel exists to show where women sit in this net. You can also say the novel repeats what it exposes: in its pages too, women seldom get to speak. Both readings hold up.

Four: the author himself. Vargas Llosa later moved to classical-liberal economics, ran for president of Peru in 1990 at the head of a center-right coalition and lost to Alberto Fujimori; he then took Spanish citizenship, and in his later years publicly backed right-wing candidates in several Latin American elections. These positions made him a long-running lightning rod in Latin American letters, and some readers reread his early critiques of power through the later man. Those are matters of record; whether they should color a judgment of this book is up to each reader.
