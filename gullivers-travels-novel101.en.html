Travels into Several Remote Nations of the World · Jonathan Swift · 1726 · four parts

He wakes on a beach and tries to sit up, and can't.
His arms, his legs, even his long hair have been pegged to the ground with threads, strand by strand. The sun is in his eyes; all he can do is look up.
Something is walking on his left leg. It crosses his stomach and comes right up to his chin. He forces his eyes down as far as they will go and sees a man less than six inches tall, holding a bow, a quiver on his back.
He roars. The little man falls off, and the forty or so behind him fall off too.

This piece contains complete spoilers: how one act of urination earns a bill of impeachment, what the court means by "lenity," why a king turns down gunpowder, what becomes of people who never die once they reach eighty, what animal's skin Gulliver uses to build his last boat, and why, at home again, he seats his wife at the far end of a long table. Without Part Four there is no way to argue about whether this book hates the human race.

In one sentence

An earnest English ship's surgeon is cast up in turn among the tiny, the gigantic, the inhabitants of a flying island and a nation ruled by horses, and in every one of them he cheerfully explains his own country to his hosts. It is not a fairy tale. It is the story of a man held up to four mirrors in a row who comes home refusing to admit he is human.

Coordinates

Jonathan Swift (1667–1745) was born in Dublin, a clergyman of the established Church of Ireland who ended his life as Dean of St Patrick's Cathedral, and the most ferocious satirist of his age. Gulliver's Travels came out in London in 1726 without Swift's name on it. The title page credited its narrator, "Lemuel Gulliver, first a surgeon, and then a captain of several ships," and it sold at once.

It occupies two places in literary history: the high point of satire in English, and the most heavily abridged "children's book" ever printed. Most people have only met the small people and the giants. The last two parts, the fourth above all, are where Swift says what he came to say.

The story

Act one · Lilliput: a mountain under discussion

Gulliver is a Nottinghamshire man who went to Cambridge, trained as a surgeon in London and studied medicine at Leyden. His practice failed after he married, so he went to sea as a ship's doctor. In 1699 the Antelope is wrecked in the South Seas; he swims ashore, sleeps, and wakes to the opening scene.

This is Lilliput, where people stand under six inches and everything else is scaled to match. Their first response to the giant mixes fear with accounting. For days the court debates killing him, by starvation or by poisoned arrows, Setup ①but the ministers worry that a carcass that size, left to rot, might start a plague in the capital, and so they hold back. On first reading it is simply funny: whether a man lives turns on a sanitation problem.

Gulliver is docile, chained at the door of a disused temple, learning the language. Two officials are sent to search his pockets and draw up a solemn inventory: a handkerchief the size of a carpet, a silver chest full of "dust" (his snuffbox), and an engine on a chain that makes a noise like a water-mill. Since he told them he hardly does anything without consulting it, the inventory's authors conclude it is probably the god he worships. It is his watch.

Candidates for high office dance on a slender rope, and whoever jumps highest gets the job; Flimnap, the treasurer, can caper an inch above any rival. Ministers must also leap over and creep under a stick held by the emperor; the winners wear coloured silk threads round their waists. The court is split into two parties by the height of their heels, and the heir to the throne, wearing one high heel and one low, hobbles to keep everyone happy.

The great matter of state is a long war. Everyone used to crack eggs at the big end, until the present emperor's grandfather cut his finger doing so as a boy, and his father ordered the whole nation to crack the small end. Six rebellions followed; one emperor lost his life and another his crown, and eleven thousand people are said to have died rather than switch ends. The exiled Big-Endians fled to the neighbouring island of Blefuscu, and the two nations went to war. The scripture both sides cite says only that true believers shall break their eggs at the convenient end, and which end is convenient was always left to each person's conscience.

Gulliver wades across the channel, his spectacles shielding his eyes from the arrows, and with hooks and cables hauls Blefuscu's fifty warships home in one go. The delighted emperor makes him a Nardac, the highest title in the land, and proposes the next step: Setup ②fetch the rest of the enemy fleet, reduce Blefuscu to a province, and wipe out the Big-Endians. Gulliver refuses, and in strong terms: he will not be the tool that turns a free and brave people into slaves. The emperor's warmth toward him cools from that day.

Some time later a fire breaks out at night in the empress's apartments; a maid of honour fell asleep reading a romance and left the candle burning. The Lilliputians' buckets are the size of thimbles and the blaze is winning. Gulliver happens to have drunk a good deal of the local wine that evening, Setup ③and he finds a way to put the fire out in three minutes: he pisses on it. The palace is saved, and the empress, mortally offended, vows never to live in those rooms again.

Soon afterwards a great man at court, someone Gulliver once did a favour, visits him secretly at night in a closed sedan chair and shows him the articles of impeachment drawn up against him. Payoff ③ ↑The first charge: making water within the precincts of the royal palace, a capital offence whatever the reason. The second: refusing to destroy Blefuscu. The urine was played as a crude joke when it happened. Here it shows its teeth: the court does not remember that you saved the palace, only what you saved it with.

Some ministers want him killed. His "friend" Reldresal, the secretary for private affairs, speaks up for mercy with a lenient alternative: put out his eyes, which will leave his strength intact for His Majesty's service. The emperor agrees. Privately, the ministers also settle on cutting his rations little by little, so that he weakens and starves within months, after which his flesh will be stripped off Payoff ① ↑and carted away for burial in distant places to prevent infection, the skeleton kept as a monument. The worry that made us laugh in chapter two comes back intact in chapter seven: what they have been calculating all along is how to dispose of a very large body, and this time they have even worked out that a slowly starved corpse is a smaller one. Swift adds one more twist of the knife. Whenever this emperor orders a cruel punishment, he makes a speech praising his own mercy, and the whole country has learned to tremble at the word "lenity."

Gulliver slips away to Blefuscu that night, finds an overturned boat of human size on the beach, repairs it and puts to sea, and is picked up by an English merchantman. He has a few tiny cattle and sheep in his pockets, which he grazes on a green at Greenwich and shows for money. After two months at home he goes to sea again.

Act two · Brobdingnag: the same man as somebody's pocket curiosity

In 1702 the Adventure, blown off course, stops at an unknown coast for water. Gulliver wanders off alone, turns, and sees his shipmates rowing frantically for the ship with a giant wading after them. He has been left behind.

This is Brobdingnag, where everything is twelve times larger than at home and the corn stands forty feet tall. A reaper nearly treads on him; the farmer picks him up and peers at him like a strange insect. The farmer's nine-year-old daughter becomes his nurse. She makes him a bed in her doll's cradle, teaches him the language and names him Grildrig, "little man"; he calls her Glumdalclitch, "little nurse." It is the tenderest relationship in the book.

The farmer realises the little creature can earn money and shows him round the country, a dozen performances a day, until Gulliver is skin and bone. Guessing he will not last long, the farmer sells him to the queen, who has taken a fancy to him, for a thousand pieces of gold. Glumdalclitch comes to court as his keeper.

At court he is everyone's plaything. The queen's dwarf, jealous, drops him into a bowl of cream; wasps come at him the size of partridges; a monkey takes him for one of its young, carries him up onto a roof and stuffs food into his mouth. The maids of honour undress in front of him and set him on their breasts, because they do not think of him as a man at all, while what he sees is skin magnified twelve times: pores like pits, hairs like twine, a mole as broad as a plate, a smell that makes him faint. He remembers a Lilliputian friend telling him that from the ground his face looked smooth and fair, but close up it was all craters and coarse bristles. Beauty turns out to be a question of scale. This part is the first part turned around and held up to the light.

The king is a curious, studious man. Over five or six audiences Gulliver proudly describes England: the two houses of Parliament, the courts, the treasury, the army, a century of history. The king listens carefully, takes notes, and at the next audience works through them question by question. How are members of Parliament chosen? What do men who spend fortunes buying votes expect in return? Is it a skill to argue a case you know to be unjust? Why does a country at peace need a standing army, and against whom? At last he takes Gulliver in his hand, strokes him, and gives his verdict, in substance: from all you have told me, I can only conclude that the greater part of your people are the most harmful breed of loathsome little pests that nature ever let crawl across the earth. (Part II, chapter 6, paraphrased)

Stung, Gulliver tries to win back some credit. He offers the king a treasure: gunpowder. He explains the recipe and how to cast cannon, and says a few brass tubes would bring down the strongest walls; if the king will only say the word, he will supervise the workmen himself. The king is horrified that a creature so small and helpless could talk of such slaughter so coolly. Payoff ② ↑He would rather lose half his kingdom than be party to such a secret, and he forbids Gulliver, on pain of death, ever to mention it again. Look back at Part One. In Lilliput it was the emperor who wanted a weapon for enslaving his neighbours, and Gulliver who chose disgrace over complying. In Brobdingnag, now the small one, he is the one pushing a weapon into someone else's hands, and when he is turned down he privately sneers at the king's narrow views. The same man is principled when he is a giant and contemptible when he is an insect. Swift leaves the reader to decide which of the two is really Gulliver.

Two years on, travelling with the court to the coast, he is asleep in a specially built travelling box when an eagle seizes it by the ring, carries it into the sky, lets go, and drops it into the sea. An English ship hauls the box aboard, and Gulliver calls through the wall that somebody need only hook a finger through the ring and lift it on deck. The sailors decide he is mad.

Setup ④At home he stoops to get through his own door and looks down on his servants and family as pygmies; he tells his wife she must have been too thrifty, for she has starved herself and their daughter to nothing. They think his mind has gone. It takes some while before his eyes get used to human scale again. It is told lightly, like the joke everyone brings home from a long trip.

Act three · A flying island, an academy, and the people who cannot die

On his third voyage pirates set him adrift in a canoe. He looks up and sees an island hanging in the sky; the people on it let down a chain and haul him aboard.

This is Laputa, which floats and steers by means of an enormous lodestone at its core. Its people live for mathematics and music, one eye turned inward and the other fixed on the zenith, and so lost in thought that every person of rank keeps a "flapper," a servant with a stick tipped by a bladder of dried peas, whose job is to tap his master on the mouth when he ought to speak and on the ear when he ought to listen. Food comes cut into geometric figures; a shoulder of mutton is served as an equilateral triangle.

The flying island rules the land below. If a city disobeys, the island hovers over it to cut off its sun and rain; if it still disobeys, the island drops rocks on it.

Down on the ground, in the capital Lagado, Gulliver finds fields gone to waste and everyone in rags. Only the estate of an old lord named Munodi is thriving, and Munodi is mocked as a stick-in-the-mud for farming the old way. The cause is the city's Academy of Projectors. Gulliver tours it. One man has spent eight years trying to extract sunbeams from cucumbers, to be sealed in phials and let out to warm the air in raw summers; another works to turn excrement back into food; an architect builds houses from the roof down. There is a writing engine: forty students turn its handles, words fall into random order, and the professor is confident that in time it will produce books on every subject. The school of languages proposes abolishing words entirely: since words only name things, people should carry the things themselves on their backs and hold them up when they want to say something. In the political school, someone suggests settling party quarrels by taking a hundred leaders from each side, sawing their heads in half and swapping the halves, so that each argument can be settled inside a single skull.

On the island of sorcerers, where the governor can summon the dead, Homer and Aristotle appear, and all their commentators hang back at a distance, too ashamed to come near. Calling up the celebrated men of recent history, Gulliver finds that most of the heroes in the history books were frauds.

The heaviest stroke in this part falls in Luggnagg. Now and then a child is born there with a round red spot on its forehead: a struldbrug, who will never die. Gulliver is beside himself, and spills out his plans. If he were immortal he would spend two hundred years becoming the richest man in the kingdom, master every science, and serve as the nation's living memory, watching empires rise and fall and comets return, passing his wisdom down. His listeners laugh and tell him how it really is. Struldbrugs do not die, but they do age. At eighty they are dead in law; their estates go to their heirs and they are left a bare allowance. They cannot remember, they are bad-tempered, they envy everyone who still gets to die, and after two hundred years the language has drifted so far that they cannot talk to their neighbours. Gulliver later sees five or six of them and calls it the most humiliating sight of his life. His craving for immortality, he admits, has cooled a great deal.

Act four · The country of the Houyhnhnms: horses and Yahoos

By 1710 Gulliver is a captain. Many of his crew die, and the replacements he signs on in the West Indies turn out to be pirates; they mutiny and put him ashore on an unknown coast.

The first creatures he meets are hairy, sharp-clawed brutes that climb into the trees and empty their bowels on him. He is flailing with his sword when a horse walks up and the brutes scatter. The horse studies him closely; a second arrives, and the two whinny back and forth as if conferring. Slowly he understands. The masters of this country are horses, who call themselves Houyhnhnms, a word that means roughly "the perfection of nature." The brutes are Yahoos, kept as livestock.

A grey horse takes him home and becomes his "master." The trouble is his body. Setup ⑤The Houyhnhnms can see at a glance that he is built almost exactly like a Yahoo; the only difference is his outer "skin," his clothes. He guards that secret, sleeping fully dressed, until one morning the household's young sorrel nag comes in and finds him half undressed. He has to confess to his master that the clothes come off, and begs him to keep it from the other Houyhnhnms. The master agrees, though he cannot see why it matters.

The Houyhnhnm language has no word for lying. They can only say "the thing which was not," and his master puzzles for a long time over why anyone would speak in order to give someone false information, since that defeats the purpose of speech. They have no words for power, government, war, lawyers or money either. Once again Gulliver explains his country, but this time he no longer defends it. He describes why wars start (sometimes because two princes disagree over which of them should seize a third one's land), how lawyers are hired to prove that white is black, how money lets a few devour the many. In passing he also explains how the English geld their horses to make them gentle.

His master's conclusion is colder than the Brobdingnagian king's. Ordinary Yahoos at least hurt each other only with teeth and claws. Your kind of Yahoo has a little reason added, and you use that reason only to sharpen your natural vices and to acquire new ones that the Yahoos never had.

Gulliver comes to worship the Houyhnhnms. They treat every member of their kind alike and show no partiality even to their own foals; marriages are arranged by the parents for strength and colour, and one colt and one filly are enough. Death strikes them as perfectly ordinary. One mare arrives late for a visit, apologises calmly, and explains that her husband died that morning and she had to settle where his body should be laid.

One day, as he bathes in a river, a young female Yahoo sees him, is seized with desire, leaps into the water and wraps herself around him. He bellows for help; the sorrel nag gallops up and she lets go, and his master's household gets a long run of amusement out of the story. Payoff ⑤ ↑The secret he kept so long under his clothes is given away by one embrace: even the Yahoos know their own. Gulliver says that after this he can no longer deny that he is a real Yahoo in every limb.

At the general assembly the Houyhnhnms hold every four years, they take up an old question: should the Yahoos be exterminated from the face of the earth? Gulliver's master offers a gentler and more economical idea. Castrate the young Yahoos, and within a generation the species will die out of its own accord. He says he got the idea from his own Yahoo, Gulliver, and what he told him about the English and their horses. The assembly also passes an "exhortation": to keep a Yahoo in one's house as a companion is unnatural and dangerous, so the master must either work him like the others or order him to swim back where he came from.

When Gulliver hears the decision, he faints. He spends six weeks building a canoe. Its hull is sewn from Yahoo skins, and so is the sail, for which he picks the youngest he can find because their skins are thinnest; the seams are stopped with Yahoo tallow. Before he leaves he kneels and kisses his master's hoof. From the shore the sorrel nag calls after him words that mean, roughly: take care of yourself, gentle Yahoo.

He means to find an empty island and live out his days alone, but on the coast of New Holland (Australia) the natives wound him in the knee with an arrow, and a Portuguese ship takes him aboard. Its captain, Pedro de Mendez, is the kindest person in the book. He gives Gulliver clothes, invites him to dine at his table, shuts him in his cabin when he tries to throw himself overboard and so saves his life, and in Lisbon takes him into his own house. Gulliver repays him by holding his nose and keeping as far from this Yahoo as he can.

Payoff ④ ↑At home his wife takes him in her arms and kisses him, and he falls into a swoon for nearly an hour. After Brobdingnag his eyes recovered in a while. This time there is no recovery, because what has changed is not the scale of his eyes but the scale by which he measures people. He buys two young stallions and talks with them four hours a day; the smell of the groom, fresh from the stable, revives his spirits. Five years later, in the book's last pages, he reports that only last week he began to let his wife eat at the same table as him, at the far end of a long one.

The fuse

Put the four parts end to end and the fuse is not the threads in Lilliput or the grey horse in the last part. It is the one thing Gulliver does everywhere he lands: he explains "we English" to his hosts. In Brobdingnag he puts the best gloss on it and is classed as vermin. Among the Houyhnhnms he drops the gloss, and ends up believing the verdict himself. Each explanation polishes the mirror a little more and leaves the narrator less ground to stand on. In the end he finds that the country he has been describing all along is himself. The mirror does not make him better; it only means he can never again sit near his family at the table.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion one · The inventory of his pockets

Two Lilliputian officials climb into Gulliver's pockets and record everything they find as they see it. His pistols are "hollow pillars of iron," his gold coins great discs of yellow metal, and his watch a ticking engine their owner very likely takes for a god. Its force comes from handing the point of view entirely to the little men: we recognise every object, yet we are made to see each one through eyes that don't, and the most familiar things in the world turn strange. Swift lays out the method of the whole book in Part One: no argument, just a different pair of eyes. The king hearing about England and the Houyhnhnm hearing about war are this inventory scaled up.

Slow motion two · Gunpowder

In the gunpowder scene the narrator never suspects anything is wrong. Swift has him describe the most horrifying thing in the book in his warmest and proudest voice, then lets the king's horror stand in for the reader's. The technique is to take judgment away from the narrator: Gulliver doesn't know what he is saying, but we do, and the only moral voice in the scene belongs to the king he dismisses as backward. After the refusal Gulliver is not ashamed; he feels sorry for the king. Here the satire turns a second time, and what it skewers is not gunpowder but every reader who also felt a little sorry for the king.

Slow motion three · The far end of the table

The last pages contain no adventure at all, only a man at home: sickened by his wife's smell, talking to horses, training himself to tolerate human beings. Swift takes what should be a homecoming and writes it as a case history, and its last detail, letting his wife sit at the far end of a long table, is so small it is almost a domestic aside. It cuts both ways. It cuts at humankind, which Gulliver no longer thinks deserves a nearer seat. It cuts at Gulliver too, who has turned admiration for reason into cruelty toward the people closest to him and calls it progress. The ending offers no exit: the narrator has gone mad, and not everything he sees is false.

The idea, worked through: a ruler that changes its markings

The book's core device is to change one variable and hold everything else still. Each part turns a single dial and watches what a human being becomes under it.

Rule one: the proportions are actually worked out. Lilliput runs at 1:12, Brobdingnag at 12:1, and Swift carries the ratio into small details. The Lilliputian mathematicians measure Gulliver, reckon that his body holds as much as 1,728 of theirs (twelve cubed), and allot him 1,728 Lilliputian rations a day. The joke is arithmetically correct. It remains a joke: scale a man up twelve times and his bones gain 144 times the cross-section while his weight grows 1,728 times, so a real giant would collapse under himself. This is what the satire needs, not what biology allows.

Rule two: size sets moral position. Bigger than everyone in Lilliput, Gulliver comes across as generous and principled; smaller than everyone in Brobdingnag, he turns vain and ingratiating, desperate to prove he is useful. Swift never says that virtue depends on power, but he lets us watch the same man behave differently at two sizes and leaves the verdict to us.

Rule three: the narrator is an ordinary, credulous man. Gulliver is honest, diligent and without a trace of humour, and records everything at face value. His name is often linked to "gull," someone easily fooled; that is a common reading, not something Swift explained. Because he is naive, readers follow him trustingly, until Part Four forces the question of whether to follow him into the conclusion he finally reaches.

Rule four: the target keeps moving. Part One aims at the court, with allusions to the party politics of Swift's England (who stands for whom has been argued over at length, and much of it is disputed). Part Two aims at England. Part Three aims at learning cut off from life; the Academy is generally read as a jab at the Royal Society of the day. Part Four aims at humanity itself. By the end, the target also includes Gulliver, the man holding the bow.

What it forces you to think about

One · Should you refuse a technology that would make you powerful?

The king of Brobdingnag turns down gunpowder. Suppose you were him. Your neighbours don't have it; with it you would be safe forever. Turn it down, and what happens the day someone else gets it?

The strongest case for taking it: technology does not vanish because you shut your eyes. If Gulliver can recite the recipe, it will be invented sooner or later, so what you are really refusing is the chance for a decent ruler to hold it first. Restraint only means something if you have the power to begin with; without it, restraint is just helplessness.

The strongest case for refusing: some things change whoever picks them up. The king's point is not "gunpowder is too dangerous" but that a mind able to describe slaughter calmly is itself the warning; what he turns down is becoming someone like Gulliver. And he has read his position correctly. A kingdom walled off by mountains and sea has no need to strike first.

Swift's side is plain: the king is the most admired ruler in the book. But Swift also gives him an easy condition, isolation. Brought up to date, the question is not only about nuclear weapons. It is also whether you build a product you know will hurt people because "someone else will build it anyway."

Two · Should a country bet on the projectors, or on Munodi's old estate?

Lagado's projectors have left the country's fields in ruins without a single project working. Munodi farms the old way, his crops do fine, and he is treated as a relic.

The best defence of the projectors: the scientific societies Swift mocked really did run some absurd experiments, and modern science grew out of exactly that absurdity. Recovering useful things from waste, drawing energy from plants: neither sounds ridiculous today. A society that won't tolerate ridiculous failures won't get its successes either. Munodi's estate is fine as it stands, but it only maintains the present, and it will not feed a future with twice the mouths.

The best defence of Munodi: the problem is not that people run experiments, it is that a whole country was made to treat experiments as policy, so the fields are already barren while everyone waits for the new method promised for ten years from now. And who pays for the trial and error? The farmers in rags, not the professors in the academy.

Swift is clearly with Munodi, though he writes so savagely that his own blind spot shows. Most workplaces, schools and families have had this argument at some point: protect the living you have, or stake it on a new plan that might change everything or leave nothing.

Three · The Houyhnhnms: would you live in a perfect society without favourites?

The Houyhnhnms do not lie, covet or fight, and they treat every member of their kind alike. They also do not favour their own foals; their mates are chosen by their parents for build; a widow arrives calmly a little late. And they are seriously discussing how to make another species extinct.

Those who see an ideal here can say that most human catastrophes come precisely from favouritism, for our children, our tribe, our nation. A society without it has no nepotism and no blood feuds. As for grief: if death is truly understood, calm is not coldness but wisdom.

Those who see a nightmare can say that without favouritism there is no love, because love is partial by nature: to love someone is to be willing to do for them what you would not do for a stranger. And how is a "rational" society that can calmly debate wiping out another species different from the twentieth-century regimes that planned their populations on "rational" grounds?

Where Swift himself stands has been fought over for three centuries. One camp says he holds up the Houyhnhnms as the ideal and humans as Yahoos, so Part Four is a misanthrope's manifesto. The other says the Houyhnhnms are satirised too, and the real target is Gulliver's either-or worship: unable to become a horse, he can only hate himself as a Yahoo, and he is completely blind to the Portuguese captain's goodness. The person who behaves best in this book is, of all things, a human being. Bring it home: when you have been deeply moved by a higher standard, do you use it to hold yourself to account, or to look down on the people around you?

Motifs: what Swift kept writing about

All his life Swift aimed at a single target: the flattering definition human beings give themselves. In a 1725 letter to his friend Alexander Pope, he said the book was meant to show that the definition "rational animal" (animal rationale) is false, and that man is at most an animal "capable of reason" (rationis capax). The same letter contains a much-quoted sentiment: he hated the animal called man, yet heartily loved individual people, John and Peter and Thomas. Hate for the species, love for the individual: that is almost exactly where the Portuguese captain stands in Part Four.

His favourite weapon was the straight face. The early A Tale of a Tub (1704) used three brothers and their coats to mock sectarian quarrels, more than twenty years before the Lilliputian eggs. A Modest Proposal, three years after the Travels (1729), adopted a Gulliver-like calm to suggest that the Irish poor sell their babies to the rich as food, prices and nutrition worked out. It is the same narrator's composure, turned on real famine.

The Latin epitaph he wrote for himself says he has gone where fierce indignation can tear his heart no more. Indignation was the engine of the man, and Gulliver's Travels is the farthest it ever took him.

Controversy and criticism

Misanthropy or satire? This is the biggest fight. In his 1851 lectures the Victorian novelist Thackeray denounced Part Four as filthy and blasphemous and advised his audience not to read it. Twentieth-century criticism split into two camps. The so-called "hard" school holds that the Houyhnhnms are Swift's ideal and that the book's conclusion is that humanity is beyond saving; the "soft" school holds that Gulliver ends as the butt of the joke and that the conclusion is "don't be like him." Both can quote chapter and verse.

Orwell's critique. In his 1946 essay "Politics vs. Literature," George Orwell called Swift a kind of "Tory anarchist" and argued that Houyhnhnm society, where everyone conforms to "reason" with no dissent, carries a whiff of totalitarianism. He also wrote that if he had to name six books to be saved when all others were destroyed, this would certainly be one of them.

Disgust with the body, and with women's bodies. The maids of honour's skin, the female Yahoo's desire, the repeated attention to excrement are often criticised as revulsion at the body, and at the female body in particular, a strain even more visible in some of Swift's poems. Defenders answer that his disgust takes in every body, and the men in the book are just as ugly. Still, it is hard to deny that women's bodies are where the book concentrates its nausea.

Editions and cuts. The first edition's publisher, nervous of prosecution, cut and softened politically risky passages, to Swift's annoyance. The 1735 Dublin edition restored much of the text and added "A Letter from Capt. Gulliver to his Cousin Sympson," in which Gulliver complains that six months after publication the Yahoos have not reformed in the slightest. In China, a late-Qing translation by Lin Shu and Wei Yi appeared early on, and the book has long circulated there, as in much of the world, as a children's story: most young readers' editions keep only the first two parts and drop the excrement and the sting of Part Four. A book that savages the human race, recast as an adventure for children, is itself a joke Swift might have written.

Gulliver explaining England to the king and the Houyhnhnm uses the same trick: seeing yourself through a stranger's eyes. The difference is that Miner was warning colleagues not to make others look like freaks; Swift says we already are.
  • Perception · Stratton's inverting lenses: In 1896 George Stratton wore lenses that turned the world upside down for days until his brain adapted; when he took them off, the normal world briefly looked wrong. Gulliver back from Brobdingnag, seeing everyone as tiny, is exactly this aftereffect. The difference is that after Part Four, he never adapts back.
  • Stoicism · Epictetus on kissing your child: In the Handbook, Epictetus advises that when you kiss your child you remind yourself you are kissing someone mortal, so death will not break you when it comes. The Houyhnhnm widow, calmly late, looks like the finished product of that training. The difference: the Stoics trained to survive pain, and the Houyhnhnms have never felt the pain that would require training.
  • Biology · Galileo's square-cube law: In 1638 Galileo showed that when a body is scaled up, its weight grows with the cube and its bones' cross-section only with the square, so giant animals need disproportionately thick bones. Swift's giants and tiny people are scaled uniformly and could not exist. But the error is his method: change only size, keep everything else fixed, and you see what size alone does.

Ten lines to take with you

① The little people hesitated to kill him because the corpse would be too big and too foul; when they finally decide to, the plan is to starve him thin first.

② He saved the empress's palace with a stream of urine, and that stream is article one of his impeachment.

③ A nation fought for years over which end to crack an egg, and its scripture only says "the convenient end."

④ In Lilliput he refused to be the tool that enslaved a neighbour; in Brobdingnag he begged to hand over gunpowder.

⑤ The king would rather lose half his kingdom than know the secret, and Gulliver pities his narrow views.

⑥ Scholars spent eight years extracting sunbeams from cucumbers, while Munodi's crops went on growing in the sun.

⑦ Living forever would be wonderful, if you didn't grow old. Unfortunately, that is a different wish.

⑧ He spent years keeping what was under his clothes a secret, and in the end a Yahoo's embrace told it for him.

⑨ He left the land of horses in a boat of Yahoo skins, choosing the youngest for the sail.

⑩ Home at last, he lets his wife eat at his table, at the far end of a long one.
