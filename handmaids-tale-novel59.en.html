The Handmaid's Tale

The Handmaid's Tale · Margaret Atwood · 1985

中文 →

A high-school gymnasium. The court lines are still painted on the floor, the hoops are still bolted to the wall, the nets long gone. Rows of army cots stand down the middle, made up with army blankets. The women who keep order carry electric cattle prods and walk the aisles between the beds.
After lights-out, talking is forbidden. So the women put their lips against the ear of whoever is nearest and hand over their names across a few inches of darkness: Alma, Janine, Dolores, Moira, June.
It is the only time in the book anyone says her own name out loud.

This page contains complete spoilers — what the Latin scratched inside the closet actually means, why the middle of the ceiling is empty, who she finds at Jezebel's, whether the black van at the end is a rescue or an arrest, and what happens at an academic conference two hundred years later. This site's conviction is that everything worth discussing lies on the far side of the ending; without the ending you can only discuss atmosphere. If you want the suspense, go read the book first. This page will wait.

In one breath

A coup turns the United States into the theocratic Republic of Gilead; with birth rates collapsed, the few women still known to be fertile are conscripted as Handmaids and assigned by the month to the men of the ruling class to bear their children. The narrator is one of them, and her money, her job, her name, her literacy and her daughter are taken from her, lawfully, in a very short space of time. At the end a black van takes her away and the novel declines to say whether it is a rescue or an arrest — and then, two centuries later, at a scholarly conference, several male academics transcribe the thirty-odd tapes she left behind, make a pun about the title, and the room laughs.

Coordinates

Margaret Atwood (b. 1939) is Canadian, a poet first, and a critic and novelist for the whole of a long career. She began The Handmaid's Tale in West Berlin in 1984 — the Wall was still up, and she had travelled in Eastern Europe — and published it in 1985. It won the Governor General's Award and the very first Arthur C. Clarke Award, and was shortlisted for the Booker without winning it. Thirty-four years later she wrote a sequel, The Testaments (2019), and that one did win the Booker.

Its position in the dystopian line is unusual. We, Brave New World and Nineteen Eighty-Four all have male protagonists, and each is granted some vantage point from which the system can be seen whole — Winston at least gets to read the forbidden book. Atwood does the opposite: she nails the camera to someone who knows nothing. Her narrator does not know how the coup was managed, where the fighting is, what the date is, or whether her husband is alive. That is the largest formal choice in the book: the history of Gilead is narrated by the person least qualified to narrate it.

The story

Act one · The red dress

She wears red: a full-length red habit, plus a white-winged headdress that stops her seeing sideways and stops anyone seeing her clearly. Her name is Offred, which is not a name but a possessive — Of-Fred, belonging to Fred. She is assigned to Commander Fred Waterford; the household also holds his Wife, Serena Joy, two domestic servants called Marthas, and a Guardian-chauffeur named Nick. If she is reassigned, the next woman to move into this room will also be called Offred. setup ①The name travels with the house, not with the person; what her own name used to be, the book never says.

The room is painted white: one chair, one table, one window that opens a hand's width, and nothing in it that could be used to do herself harm — no scissors, no cord, the curtain rod fixed in place. setup ②In the centre of the ceiling there is a plaster wreath with nothing inside it, like a face with the eyes taken out. setup ③In the back corner of the closet, the woman who lived here before scratched a line with her fingernail: Nolite te bastardes carborundorum. She has no Latin, but she says it daily and treats it as a message left for her — it must be something magnificent.

She goes out once a day to shop, always in pairs: protection, officially; mutual surveillance, actually. Her partner is Ofglen, and they exchange fixed phrases — blessed be the fruit; may the Lord open. Get a word wrong and the other woman may be an Eye, which is what the secret police are called. setup ④The shops carry no lettering, only pictures: a pork chop, three eggs, a bunch of grapes — women in Gilead are forbidden to read, and the alphabet is contraband. The route home passes the Wall, where the day's executions hang with white bags over their heads and placards on their chests: doctors who performed abortions, priests, Gender Traitors. A little red seeps through the white bag, at about the place where a mouth would be. Sometimes a party of Japanese tourists comes by, in skirts, with their legs showing, in heels, and the first thought that arrives in her head is how can they go around like that — and then the thought frightens her. It has taken only a few years to re-standardise her eyes.

Act two · Before this

Before this she had a job, a husband called Luke, a daughter, a mother who marched and burned pornography and found her insufficiently committed, and a college friend called Moira — clever, foul-mouthed, gay, the least likely person she knew to give in to anything.

The collapse begins on an entirely ordinary morning. She buys coffee on a corner and her card is refused; the clerk tells her to come back tomorrow. That same morning, men with guns walk into her office, call the female employees into the lobby, and announce that they are no longer employed — not fired, but barred by law from employment. Simultaneously every account flagged as belonging to a woman is emptied, the balance transferred to the nearest male relative. No tanks, no barricades, no moment requiring anyone to stand up. The whole thing has the texture of a systems upgrade.

Luke comes home that evening, holds her, and says he will take care of her. He means it kindly. She notes the second it happens: she has stopped being his equal, and he has not noticed.

Some time earlier, Congress had been machine-gunned and the President shot, with the blame laid on Islamic fanatics; the Constitution was suspended, and never restored. Her marriage to Luke is annulled, because Luke had been divorced and under the new law that makes them adulterers — which makes her daughter, legally, a child who should not exist, and therefore a child who can be given to someone else. They run for Canada on forged papers and are stopped in the woods near the border; she is knocked out, and when she wakes, husband and daughter are both gone. setup ⑤From then on she keeps three versions of Luke alive at once: shot on the spot; held in some cell; escaped and organising a rescue. All three cannot be true, and she believes all three — as long as there is no answer, she gets to pick one to live on today.

Then comes the Red Centre. The instructors are called Aunts, wear brown, carry cattle prods, and are the only women in Gilead permitted to read and write. Aunt Lydia lectures them about freedom: there are two kinds, she says — freedom to do as you like, and freedom from having to be afraid. You were given the first, and what you got out of it was rape and abandonment; now you are being given the second. Remember that this is a gift.

There is a class called Testifying. Janine stands in the middle and describes being gang-raped at fourteen and having an abortion. The Aunt asks whose fault it was and the room answers together that it was hers; asked again, the answer is tidier — she led them on. The first time they are made to chant it. By the third time there is something real in their voices. Having the victims preside over the trial of a victim is enormously cheaper than cattle prods, and far more durable.

Moira tries to escape twice. The first time she is brought back and her feet are ruined — the Aunts explain that hands and feet are not reproductive organs, so damaging them costs nothing. The second time she dismantles a lever inside a lavatory cistern, takes Aunt Elizabeth hostage, puts on her brown habit, takes her pass and walks out of the front gate. setup ⑥She is never seen again, and everyone talks about her; she becomes a fantasy held in common. Somebody out there was not broken in.

Act three · The Ceremony

Serena Joy, the Wife of the house, used to be a gospel singer and a television speaker, and her loudest argument was that a woman's place is the home and a career is a betrayal of what God arranged. She now has the world she campaigned for, and she is shut inside it. She does not read, work or go out; she knits scarves, and cuts flowers with a violence that has to go somewhere, beheading the spent tulips one at a time. She hates the woman in red who lives in her house, and once a month she has to hold that woman's hands.

The Ceremony is scheduled by ovulation, and preceded, as always, by the reading: Genesis 30, where Rachel, who cannot conceive, tells Jacob to give her children or she will die, and hands him her maid Bilhah so that the child may be born upon Rachel's knees. The entire legal basis of Gilead is a piece of domestic improvisation from several thousand years ago. Then three people go upstairs. The Handmaid lies on her back between the Wife's legs, her head on the Wife's stomach, fully dressed; the Wife grips her wrists from above, rings pressing into bone; the Commander does his part below, boots still on. Nobody looks at anybody. Nobody speaks.

There is also a Birth Day. The birthing stool has two tiers, Wife above and Handmaid below, and the moment the child arrives the Wife takes it. Janine's baby is later judged deformed — the Gileadean term is an Unbaby — and disposed of. She never entirely comes back.

Act four · Scrabble in the study

One night Nick brings word that the Commander wants her in his study. The whole way there she works out what he wants and whether she can refuse (she cannot). What he wants is a game of Scrabble. payoff ④ ↑This is one of the finest pieces of design in the book: in a country where shop signs have been replaced by pictures, the alphabet is the narcotic. She handles the little plastic squares, spells out words, and feels obscene — and by the law of Gilead she is, in a way that is harder to explain than adultery. This man holds her entire life in his hands, and the transgression he wants from her is literacy. After that she goes every few nights. He shows her old magazines that should have been burned; he gives her a small bottle of hand lotion; one evening he asks her to kiss him as if she meant it, and she does, and he is not satisfied. He does not want the body. He wants a little willingness, which is the scarcest commodity in the country.

payoff ③ ↑Eventually she asks him about the Latin in the closet. He laughs. It is an old schoolboy joke, he says, mock-Latin with the grammar all wrong, and it means: don't let the bastards grind you down. What goes cold in her is something else entirely: he recognised it instantly, which means the previous Offred sat in this chair too, played this game, took the hand lotion. And that woman hanged herself. payoff ② ↑Cora found her, and afterwards Serena had the light fixture taken out — which is why there is nothing but a plaster wreath in the middle of the ceiling, with a hole in it. The deepest splinter in this book is that the line she had been carrying as a declaration of resistance is a boy's dirty joke, and also a death notice.

Later he does something further out of bounds: he produces a sequined costume from the old days and some makeup, has her crouch under a cloak in the back of the car, and takes her to Jezebel's — a state-run brothel in a converted hotel, for senior men and foreign visitors, which officially does not exist. The women there are the ones who could not be adapted: lawyers, sociologists, army officers, wearing bikinis and bunny costumes salvaged from before, because Gilead no longer manufactures such things. payoff ⑥ ↑She finds Moira there. Moira got north, was caught, and was offered two options: the Colonies, cleaning up toxic waste, or this. She took this — there is drink here, and makeup, and you can sleep with women; three or four years and the body is finished, and then they send you to the Colonies anyway. She says all this lazily. She is not angry, and she is not going to run again. The narrator writes that she would like to give Moira a different ending, would like her to go out and blow something up, but she has no such story to tell, because she never saw her again. The book will not issue you even one hero.

Act five · The bargain, and the van

Gilead has a law stating that there is no such thing as a sterile man; there are only women who do not bear fruit. This is not a medical finding but a criminal statute — to suggest the problem might be his is itself the offence. Serena privately knows this is nonsense. She proposes that the Handmaid sleep with Nick, going around the Commander; if she conceives, the child still belongs to the household. In exchange, she shows her a photograph. It is her daughter: taller now, in a white dress, standing on grass, evidently well looked after. The mother studies the face, and the unbearable part is this — she is doing well, and there is nothing of me in her.

She goes to Nick's room, and afterwards she keeps going back on her own, not for a pregnancy. She writes it down without dignifying it: from this point she stops wanting to leave. She starts looking forward to dark, starts treating the house as somewhere a life can be conducted. Ofglen reveals that she belongs to an underground network called Mayday and asks her to take something out of the Commander's study, and she deflects, because she is afraid of losing the little she now has.

Then a Salvaging: two Handmaids and a Wife hanged in public, the charges unannounced. Immediately after comes a Particicution — a man pushed into the centre, declared a rapist, and handed to the assembled Handmaids for disposal. At the whistle, dozens of women in red go for him. Ofglen gets there first and kicks him unconscious; afterwards she says quietly that he was no rapist, he was a political, he was one of ours, and she was sparing him. The next morning a different woman is waiting at the gate, also calling herself Ofglen, who says in a completely level voice that the last one saw the van coming and hanged herself.

That evening Serena finds the sequined costume with the lipstick on it. What she says is short, and it is the only moment in the book when she sounds like a person: you could have left me something. After dark the van comes and two Eyes climb the stairs to take her. She assumes this is the end. Then Nick appears on the landing and says a few words: Mayday, go with them, trust me. She does not know whether this is a rescue or simply the full procedure of an arrest, does not know whether Nick is a comrade or an informer; she knows nothing at all, and she goes down anyway. The last sentence of the novel has her stepping up into the darkness of the van — or else the light.

Act six · An academic conference, two hundred years on

Turn the page and the typeface is the same and the register has completely changed. The date is 25 June 2195; the place is the University of Denay, Nunavit; the occasion is the Twelfth Symposium on Gileadean Studies; the keynote speaker is Professor Pieixoto, of Cambridge.

payoff ⑤ ↑What you have just heard, he explains, exists in the original as some thirty audio tapes, found in an army footlocker in Bangor, Maine, recorded over old popular music, in no order whatsoever, transcribed and arranged by himself and a colleague in the sequence they judged plausible. Which is to say: the hesitating, self-revising, doubling-back voice the reader has just spent a book inside is partly hers and partly the editorial decisions of two men. All the way through she kept saying that this was a story she was telling, that it hadn't happened like that, that she would tell it again; she addressed a "you" she had to invent, because telling a story at all is a wager that someone will still be alive to hear it. She won the bet. He is the one who heard.

The title is his too: a nod to Chaucer, he says, and also for the pun — tale, and tail. The transcript records laughter at this point. payoff ① ↑He then expends considerable effort identifying which historical figure the Commander was, offering two candidates, while showing essentially no interest in who she was — and indeed he cannot find out. Her name can only have been spoken once in the whole book: in that gymnasium after lights-out, in the string of names handed across the dark. (That is a reader's inference; the novel never confirms it.) Two hundred years later a discipline with every archive and method at its disposal cannot name her. The only people who ever knew what she was called were strangers in the next cot.

He closes by cautioning his colleagues against passing hasty moral judgement on the Gileadeans: every society has its circumstances, and our business is to understand rather than to condemn. Applause. And then the true last line of the book: "Are there any questions?" (closing line of the Historical Notes)

The fuse

Not the coup, not the birth rate, not Genesis 30. The fuse is an ordinary weekday morning on which her card will not go through. The same batch of instructions puts every female employee out of work and moves every account flagged female into the hands of the nearest man. There is no gunfire that day, no moment that calls for courage, and almost everyone eats dinner as usual that evening — including her. Everything after it, the red habit, the wings, the white bags on the Wall, the monthly Ceremony, is the wash from that one electronic transfer. The hardest part of stripping half a population of its citizenship is not the violence. It is that on the afternoon it happens, it does not look like the sort of thing you would go into the street about.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · The three minutes of the Ceremony

Why does it land? Because Atwood does not let the narrator weep and does not let her denounce; she has her count details — boots, rings, the lamp, the time. The point of view sits directly behind the victim's eyes while the register belongs to a clerk. That gap is worse than any scream: it tells you this person is splitting herself in half, one half on the bed and the other withdrawn to the ceiling taking notes, because otherwise she cannot get through three minutes.

Worse still, she refuses the word rape, on the grounds that she signed up, that she came of her own accord, since the alternative was the Colonies. A system is at its most successful not when it makes resistance impossible, but when it leaves you without a word for what is being done to you. And the Wife's presence converts a crime into a liturgy: with a third party, a scripture and a fixed procedure, it is no longer something a man is doing. It is the state, conducting business.

Two · The day the card was refused

Atwood's method for writing a coup is to write it as an administrative process: the machine beeps, the clerk frowns, he says come back tomorrow, and outside the weather is fine. The most frightening line in the novel is Luke's "I'll take care of you" — offered in kindness, with nothing malicious in it, and announcing that from today she lives at the discretion of one man's goodwill.

The pacing is exact: she fills the day with dinner, a bath, whether the child is asleep, because that is how people actually lived through it. There is a summing-up in the book to the effect that nothing changes instantaneously — heat the bath slowly enough and you are boiled before you notice. The technical problem of the chapter is persuading a reader that this could happen to someone like them, and the solution is not to intensify the catastrophe but to intensify the ordinary. The more normal the day, the more alarming it is.

Three · The Particicution

Within a few lines of the whistle the formation is gone. The force of this scene comes from a deeply uncomfortable decision: Atwood hands the pleasure of violence to the oppressed, and lets the reader taste it too. The narrator admits she can smell blood and that her body wants to go forward. Several hundred pages of compression find an exit here, and the reader exhales with everyone else — and only then works out what they have just taken part in.

There is a further layer on top: Ofglen's pre-emptive kick is the cleanest moral act in the book (sparing an innocent man), and from the outside it is indistinguishable from the most savage thing in the field. In Gilead, decency and atrocity have to wear the same clothes to survive. A regime's real measure is not how many people fear it, but how many are willing to do its work with their hands.

How the country was designed

The mechanism

Take Gilead apart and there are startlingly few components, each of them cheap. Step one: cut off the money. Bar women from employment, freeze and transfer their accounts. This is the only real coup in the book, and it requires one electronic instruction and not a single soldier. Every rule that follows is simply added on top of a population that already has nowhere to go.

Step two: cut off the letters. Women may not read or write, signage becomes pictorial, the Bible is kept locked and read aloud by men. The purpose is not ignorance — most of these women were educated — it is the removal of the tools of appeal: a person who cannot read the statute cannot show she is being treated unlawfully, and a person who cannot write leaves no evidence. Hence the most obscene image in the novel being a board game.

Step three: colour-code. Use uniforms to cut women into mutually incompatible classes, each with genuine grievances against the others.

Step four, the coldest piece of design in the book: the Aunts. Women policing women. A character states the logic outright — the best and cheapest way to control women is to use women. The brown-clad Aunts are not conscripted thugs; they hold power, they hold the privilege of literacy, they hold convictions, and they genuinely believe they are saving these girls. Step five: the pressure valve. Salvagings, Particicutions, Jezebel's — every high-pressure system needs a sanctioned exit for what it compresses, and the crucial condition is that the exit be state-operated. You may hate, but only the designated object; you may desire, but only in the brothel the government runs.

What holds, and what is there for literary convenience

What holds is the legal shell. Atwood has stated her governing rule many times: nothing in the book is invented — every regulation, punishment and costume has been carried out by human beings somewhere. The list checks out line by line: the fusion of church and state and the public penalties of Puritan New England (the novel is dedicated to Mary Webster, the seventeenth-century woman hanged overnight as a witch who did not die); Romania under Ceaușescu criminalising contraception and abortion and subjecting women of childbearing age to regular gynaecological inspection in the name of population growth; Nazi Germany selecting "suitable" children in occupied territory to be raised as German; the Argentine junta transferring the babies of the disappeared to military families; the direct control of women's reproduction under American slavery; the removal of Indigenous children from their families in Canada and Australia. Every component has a provenance, which is exactly why the book does not read as science fiction.

What does not hold is the arithmetic. The Handmaid class is tiny and could not conceivably close a national fertility gap; as a programme for raising the birth rate the design is absurdly inefficient. Which exposes what Gilead is actually doing: it is not solving fertility, it is reallocating the question of whose children they are. The medical self-sabotage works the same way: by refusing to admit male infertility, the regime forecloses its own remedies, so that even where the problem plainly lies with the Commander nothing can be said and a Wife must arrange the chauffeur privately. That is not a hole in the world-building but a portrait of one: real regimes routinely abandon their stated goal in order to protect their story about themselves. One honest caveat, too — this is not a forecast. What she built is an assembly, every part authentic, the whole machine never yet run anywhere complete.

What it forces you to think about

Question one
Aunt Lydia says the old world gave you freedom to, and the new one gives you freedom from. Put her side as strongly as it can be put.

It has to be put strongly, because this is the hardest passage in the book to answer. Her argument runs: the freedom you had included the possibility of being dragged out of a parking garage at any hour; it included date rape, the boss's hand, the ex-boyfriend who follows you; it included being traded in at forty-five and raising the children alone on maintenance that never arrives. You called that freedom because you had learned to count the risk as part of the price. Now nobody will touch you in the street, nobody will discard you for getting older, you are fed, housed and given something to do. The first kind of freedom belongs to those who were already strong. The second belongs to everyone else.

What makes it worse is that the narrator corroborates it. Walking down a street, she notices she is no longer listening for footsteps behind her, and the relief is real. Atwood does not let her protagonist refute the argument from high ground; she lets her feel the benefit, and be ashamed of feeling it.

The other side is hard too. First, the exchange is grotesquely unequal: what is surrendered is everything — money, letters, name, child, body — and what is returned is a service ordinary policing was supposed to provide. Second, and more fundamentally: freedom from requires somebody else to decide what you are to be afraid of, and that somebody is never the protected party. Gilead's women have stopped fearing strangers and started fearing Aunts, Eyes, and the Wife downstairs; the quantity of fear is unchanged, only the invoice. Third, it does not reverse. Lose the first kind and you can protest; once the second is installed, the language you would protest in has been removed.

Bring it home: dormitory curfews, the camera at the corner, platform moderation, age and identity gates erected for your protection — all of them are the same trade, and many of them genuinely save people. The question worth asking is not whether to trade, but the two conditions attached: who gets to make the trade, and can it be undone? The kind that cannot be undone is not a trade at all.

Question two
The narrator barely resists, and halfway through she stops even wanting to leave. Is that forgivable?

Take the "not forgivable" side first, since nobody usually argues it seriously. The facts are plain: she had chances. Ofglen invited her into the underground and she deflected, on the grounds that she did not want to lose her nights with Nick. Earlier, on the day the accounts were frozen, there were people in the streets — they were fired on, and she was not among them, because she had a child to collect, dinner to make, and a wish to wait and see whether things improved. She writes it down herself: they lived by not reading the papers. A regime is cheap precisely because the overwhelming majority make the same calculation she did, and each individual calculation is defensible. To call that silence survival is to assign the entire bill to the men with the guns, and there are never very many of those.

Now the other side, equally strongly. Demanding heroism from victims is an extension of the injury: resistance requires somewhere to fall back to, and Gilead's design is to remove the fallbacks — money, literacy, communication, alliance — before it moves. Moira is the one genuine resister in the book, and her price is a bed at Jezebel's and then the Colonies; the novel uses her to post the going rate. More decisively: this book exists because she lived and then spoke. Those thirty tapes are an act of resistance whose form happens to be staying alive, remembering, and recording. The story in your hands was handed to you by the woman who was not brave enough.

Atwood's own position shows in one place: she gives her protagonist weakness without giving her contempt — the people written most savagely are never the ones who surrender, but the ones who work for the machine. Nor does she launder weakness into virtue. Bring it home: staying in a steadily worsening situation — a company, a family, an industry — without leaving and without speaking, where each day's reason is sound, and all the reasons together are precisely the conditions on which the thing continues. The question is not whether you are a coward. It is — where is the line you set for yourself, and can you say it out loud? People who cannot say it discover afterwards that they crossed it.

Question three
Two hundred years later Professor Pieixoto says: do not pass moral judgement on the Gileadeans. Is he wrong?

His case is easier to make than you would expect. The professional ethics of history really do forbid trying the past by today's standards: research conducted in indignation finds only what it went looking for, turns the opponent into a monster, and therefore never learns how the mechanism ran inside ordinary people. Understanding why Gilead appealed to so many — including the women who volunteered to be Aunts — is more useful than denouncing it, because the next version will not have the same shape and only those who know the mechanism will recognise it. And he is no bystander: the tapes were rescued, transcribed and ordered by him. Without him there is no testimony at all. Condemnation is free. Archival work is not.

Now look at what he does. Presented with an oral record of a woman raped on schedule for years, his first move is a joke about the title involving a backside, at which the room laughs; his second is an energetic effort to identify which historical figure the Commander was, with no comparable interest in who she was; his third is to remind the profession not to moralise. The cruelty of Atwood's construction is that she makes "maintaining scholarly distance" and "turning a woman back into material" the same gesture. And the laugh is the sharpest detail: two centuries on, Gilead gone, a room of the best-educated people available hears an account of a woman's suffering and responds with a smutty pun.

So this is not a simple question about whether to judge. It is: which expires first, judgement or understanding? While the thing is happening, refusing to judge is taking a side; two hundred years after it ends, rushing to judge is laziness. That closing "Are there any questions?" drops the whole problem into the reader's lap — you are now the person in the conference room. When you look at what is happening elsewhere, are you looking at testimony, or at content?

Question four
Serena Joy campaigned for this system and is now imprisoned in it. Does she deserve any sympathy?

The case against is well stocked. She spent years on television with a microphone telling other women to go home while she herself flew around the country having a career. When the new world arrived she was not deprived of anything real — house, servants, standing; the only thing missing is a child, and her solution is to requisition another woman's body, hold her by the wrists, and hate her for it. She is not a victim of the system. She is its middle management, disappointed by the dividend.

The case for is not light either. Her entire political position was that women should not speak in public, and the moment that position became law, the first microphone confiscated was hers — she can never take a stage again, and knitting is now the most significant event of her day. The violence with which she cuts flowers is what remains of a capable, ambitious person shut in by her own success. She got everything she asked for and found that it contained no place for her.

The question recurs constantly: people who vote for a rule and are then caught by it; people who push a performance-review system through a company and are eventually culled by it; people who enforce a doctrine about what women ought to be and are pinned by the same doctrine in old age. The issue is not how much sympathy to extend. It is this: if even the people who built the stage end badly, where does the confidence that "people like us will be exempt" actually come from?

The thing Atwood keeps writing

Atwood made her name first as a poet and critic. In 1972 she published a short book on Canadian literature, Survival, which sorts literary victims into positions: denying you are a victim, admitting it but blaming fate, admitting it and refusing to treat it as unalterable. Almost every novel since has been an exercise in assigning characters one of those positions. The narrator of The Handmaid's Tale spends most of the book stuck in the second, and moves up one notch only when she records the tapes.

The second line is the woman's testimony as edited by other people. Alias Grace (1996) takes a servant from a real 1843 Canadian murder case whose story is relayed and rewritten by doctors, lawyers and newspapers, so that the reader never obtains the original; The Blind Assassin (2000) is assembled out of a dead woman's text plus somebody else's arrangement of it. The Historical Notes are the sharpest version of this — only on the last page are you told that what you read is an archive curated by men.

The third is women administering women. Cat's Eye (1988) is about the exclusions and humiliations girls organise among themselves, a cruelty calibrated to the level of tone of voice, and it is the Aunt system in miniature. In 2019 The Testaments simply hands the microphone to Aunt Lydia and lets her narrate from inside how she became that person; it won the Booker and took the motif as far as it goes.

There is also the body-and-ecology strand: The Edible Woman (1969), and the trilogy beginning with Oryx and Crake (2003). Catastrophe in her work never falls out of the sky; it is always something people brought about step by step in pursuit of a goal that seemed reasonable.

Criticism, honestly

  • Race is the weightiest objection. The novel mentions in passing that Gilead has resettled Black people — officially the Children of Ham — in National Homeland One, in North Dakota. In other words the author moves Black people out of the frame. Critics point out that the people actually subjected to systematic forced reproduction and the removal of their children in American history were enslaved Black women; the book transposes that experience onto white middle-class women and then removes its original owners. The 2017 television series went the other way with colour-blind casting, and drew the opposite complaint: making Gilead look racially egalitarian erases racial oppression from the premise.
  • Religion and orientalism. The coup is blamed on Islamic fanatics, while the veiling and seclusion imagery draws heavily on Western stereotypes of Muslim societies. The charge is that the book critiques Christian fundamentalism while outsourcing its visual language to another faith.
  • A passive protagonist and a rescue by a man. The last scene is a man saying trust me, and she goes; across the whole book she overturns nothing. Defenders say that is the point. Detractors say that for a book treated as a feminist classic, the structure deserves scrutiny.
  • The genre argument. Atwood has long insisted the book is speculative fiction rather than science fiction, on the grounds that everything in it has already been done. Science fiction has not accepted the distinction; Le Guin, among others, publicly disagreed with her taxonomy. The dispute itself shows how much Atwood cares which shelf the book sits on.
  • The author's own public controversies. In 2016 Atwood signed an open letter calling on the University of British Columbia to account for the process by which it handled the case of the writer Steven Galloway; in 2018 she published a piece in The Globe and Mail titled "Am I a bad feminist?" in response to the criticism. The argument ran for a long time in Canadian literary circles, with serious people on both sides. The facts are recorded here without a verdict.
  • Criticism from the other direction. Religious conservative readers have argued that the portrait of believers is a cartoon: actual church life is compressed into quoting Genesis and then committing assault. The complaint is usually ignored, and the observation behind it is accurate — the novel contains virtually no sympathetic character who takes faith seriously.

Ten sentences to take away

① A coup does not require tanks. Sometimes it is only that one morning your card is refused, and everyone eats dinner as usual that evening.

② "I'll take care of you" can be a declaration of love or a sentence, depending on whether you still have alternatives.

③ Offred is not a name but a possessive: the name travels with the house, not with the person.

④ In a country where women may not read, the most obscene transgression available is a game of Scrabble.

⑤ The defiant Latin in the closet turns out to be a schoolboy's dirty joke, left by a woman who hanged herself — this book will not issue you even a slogan.

⑥ The cheapest way to control women is to employ women; the Aunts work better than cattle prods because they believe they are saving people.

⑦ Every high-pressure system needs a sanctioned exit, and the condition is that the state runs it: you may hate, but only the designated object.

⑧ Freedom from fear sounds excellent until you notice that someone else decides what you are to fear, and that the trade cannot be reversed.

⑨ She was not brave; she survived and recorded — and the story in your hands was handed to you by the woman who was not brave enough.

⑩ Gilead has been gone two hundred years, and a room of scholars hearing a woman's account of it still reaches first for a joke. Regimes can be overthrown; that laughter cannot.
