Novels, Deep-Read · Joseph Conrad · No.96

Heart of Darkness

Joseph Conrad · 1899 · novella in three parts

中文 →

The Thames estuary, at dusk. A cruising yawl has dropped anchor to wait for the tide. Five men no longer young sit on deck; one is fiddling with dominoes, the rest say nothing, watching the light over London thicken and darken upstream.
One of them sits cross-legged against the mast, cheeks sunken, hands dropped with the palms turned out, like an idol. Out of nowhere he says something that has nothing to do with the busy, respectable river in front of them: "And this also has been one of the dark places of the earth." (opening of Part I)
Then he begins a story. By the time he finishes, they have missed the tide.

This piece contains complete spoilers: what Kurtz became at the end of the river, the words he died on, and the lie Marlow told, back in Europe, to the woman who loved him. The book's force lies almost entirely in what you see once you know the ending and look back at every detail along the way.

In one sentence

An English sailor signs on with a European trading company and takes a battered steamer up a great African river to bring back Kurtz, said to be its most brilliant and most idealistic ivory agent; what he finds at the end of the river is a man who has mounted human heads on stakes, who is worshipped by the people around him, and who is wasted to a skeleton by illness — Kurtz passes judgment on his own life in a few words before he dies, and Marlow goes home and keeps that judgment hidden.

Coordinates

Joseph Conrad (1857–1924) was born to Polish parents in a part of the Russian Empire, went to sea at sixteen, spent nearly twenty years on French and British merchant ships, and became a British subject in 1886 — English was the third language he learned. In 1890 he really did go to the Congo Free State, then the personal possession of King Leopold II of Belgium, to work on a river steamer. Nine years later he turned that journey into this novella, serialized in three installments in Blackwood's Magazine in 1899 and collected in book form in 1902.

Its place on the map is the seam between the nineteenth-century adventure tale and twentieth-century modernism: on the surface a sea story, underneath a narrative nested inside a narrative, told by a man who keeps admitting he can't quite say what he means. T. S. Eliot took a line from it as the epigraph to "The Hollow Men" (1925); Coppola's Apocalypse Now (1979) moved it to the Vietnam War. That is an adaptation; everything below is the novella.

The story

Act I · On the Thames, in the whited sepulchre: a blank space on the map

Marlow is not the narrator. The book's "I" is an unnamed passenger on the yawl who simply passes the story on. Aboard with him are the host, a Director of Companies; a Lawyer; an Accountant; and Marlow, the only one of them who still follows the sea. Marlow's remark about foreshadowing ①"one of the dark places" reaches back nineteen hundred years, to when Roman galleys first came up this river and Britain was the end of the known world: swamps, forest, fever, savages.

He goes on, roughly: conquering the earth mostly means taking it from people with a different complexion or slightly flatter noses than ours, and it isn't a pretty thing when you look into it too much. What redeems it is only the idea at the back of it — something you can set up, bow down before, and offer a sacrifice to.

As a boy Marlow was in love with the blank spaces on maps. By the time he grew up, the biggest one, in the heart of Africa, had been filled in with names and had become "a place of darkness" instead, and the great river drawn across it looked to him like an immense uncoiled snake. It had him.

Through an aunt with connections he lands a post as captain of a river steamer for a company trading on that river. The vacancy exists because foreshadowing ②the last captain, a Dane named Fresleven, reputedly the gentlest, quietest creature alive, had spent two years out there in "the noble cause," then got into a quarrel over two black hens, beat the village chief savagely with a stick, and was speared by the chief's son. Nobody buried him; by the time Marlow went looking, the grass was growing up through his ribs.

The company's head office is in a city that reminds Marlow of a whited sepulchre (it is never named; anyone can tell it is Brussels). In the outer room two women sit knitting black wool, watching people go in and come out, as if guarding the door of Darkness. The company doctor measures Marlow's skull, asks whether there's any madness in his family, and observes that the changes take place inside. His aunt sends him off with talk about weaning ignorant millions from their horrid ways. Marlow reminds her that the company is run for profit.

Act II · The Company Station: a cliff that isn't in the way, a man with a spotless collar

He sails down the coast on a French steamer for more than thirty days. Along the way he sees a French warship firing its guns into the bush where there isn't a single hut in sight; the shells vanish into the endless green and nothing happens. Someone explains that there is a camp of "enemies" hidden in there.

At the company's first station a boiler lies wallowing in the grass and a railway truck lies on its back with its wheels in the air, like the carcass of an animal. Men are blasting a cliff for a railway, though the cliff is not in anybody's way. Six Black men climb the slope in single file, each with an iron collar around his neck, all joined by a clinking chain; the man guarding them, rifle on his shoulder, snaps to attention when a white man passes. The prisoners are called "criminals."

Marlow steps aside into some shade and finds it full of laborers who have been worked until they are sick or starving and have crawled under the trees to die. One young man has a bit of white worsted tied around his neck, and Marlow can't work out whether it is a badge, an ornament, or a charm. He says it was like stepping into a circle of some Inferno; at the time, he simply walked away.

Right next to that grove he meets the company's chief accountant: high starched collar, white cuffs, hair parted and brushed, a miracle of grooming in a place of flies and fever. A sick agent lies groaning in his office; the accountant complains that the noise makes it hard to avoid clerical errors. It is from him that Marlow first hears the name: deep in the interior is a Mr. Kurtz, a first-class agent who sends in as much ivory as all the other stations put together, and who will go far, very far, in the Administration.

Act III · The Central Station: three months waiting for rivets

After a two-hundred-mile march with a caravan of sixty men, Marlow reaches the Central Station to learn that the steamer he came to command is at the bottom of the river: the manager, in a hurry, had set off upstream two days earlier and torn the bottom out of her within three hours. The repair needs rivets. There are cases of them at the coast; there is not one here.

The manager has no learning, no talent, no presence; his one gift is that he is never ill, and he wears a faint, unsettling smile. His white agents, whom Marlow calls "pilgrims," drift around the station with long staves in their hands, and the one word they say over and over is "ivory," as though they were praying to it.

The station also has a brickmaker who has never made a brick and whose real job is spying for the manager. On the wall of his room foreshadowing ③Marlow notices a small oil sketch: a woman, draped and blindfolded, carrying a lighted torch against a background that is almost black; the torchlight on her face is sinister. Kurtz painted it here a year ago.

The brickmaker assumes Marlow, like Kurtz, belongs to some "gang of virtue" in Europe with powerful patrons behind him. Marlow lets him think so. And here he stops to tell his listeners on deck that foreshadowing ④he hates a lie — not because he's straighter than anyone else, but because there is a taint of death in lies, like biting into something rotten. Yet for the sake of a Kurtz he has never met, he has already gone along with one.

Over three months of repairs, Kurtz is a legend in everyone's mouth: an emissary of pity, science, and progress to some, a man to be got rid of to others. One night, lying on the deck of the steamer, Marlow overhears the manager and his uncle on the bank: Kurtz had started downriver with a great load of ivory, come three hundred miles, then abruptly turned back, alone, in a dugout with four paddlers, to his own station. The two men hope the climate will finish him off. Marlow says it was the first time he "saw" Kurtz: a lone white man suddenly turning his back on headquarters, on relief, on home, and paddling back toward the depths of the wilderness.

Act IV · Upriver: little sticks out of the fog

The steamer is finally afloat. Marlow takes it upriver with the manager, a few pilgrims, a Black helmsman, a fireman, and a crew of hired local men he calls "cannibals." The walls of forest on either bank never move; at night there are drums; on the shore people leap and shout as the boat goes by. What unsettles Marlow most is the faint sense that between himself and that uproar there is a remote kinship.

The crew's provisions, some rotting hippo meat, were thrown overboard by the pilgrims because of the smell. The crew are starving; they outnumber the whites, and they have knives; and they never lay a finger on the plump pilgrims. Marlow keeps turning it over: what holds them back? He can't find an answer, so he calls it "restraint."

About fifty miles below Kurtz's station they find an abandoned reed hut with a stack of cut firewood and a board with a message telling them the wood is theirs, to hurry up, and to approach cautiously. Inside is a tattered book, foreshadowing ⑤an old manual on points of seamanship, its margins filled with notes in what looks like cipher. To find, in that wilderness, a book about the honest business of doing a job right moves Marlow almost to tenderness.

Fog closes the river, and out of it comes a tremendous, mournful cry. When the fog thins, the arrows come (more on those seconds below). The pilgrims blaze away into the bush with their Winchesters; the helmsman drops the wheel to grab a rifle; a spear comes through the window and into his side. He falls at Marlow's feet and Marlow's shoes fill with blood. Marlow yanks the steam whistle and the shore goes silent as the crowd scatters.

Dying, the helmsman looks up at Marlow with a look like a claim of distant kinship. Marlow throws his blood-soaked shoes into the river and tips the body overboard himself, afraid the hungry crew will have designs on it. The pilgrims decide that Kurtz must be dead, and Marlow is suddenly, crushingly disappointed: all along he had imagined not meeting Kurtz but hearing him talk. In everyone's account, Kurtz was first of all a voice.

Here Marlow jumps ahead to tell them about a report Kurtz had written for the International Society for the Suppression of Savage Customs: seventeen pages of burning eloquence, opening with the argument that whites must necessarily appear to the natives as something like supernatural beings, and so can do almost boundless good. At the foot of the last page, scrawled in an unsteady hand and clearly added later, like lightning out of a clear sky: "Exterminate all the brutes!" (Part II)

Act V · The Inner Station: round knobs on posts, a woman on the bank

As they near Kurtz's station they see a ruined house on a hillside with a row of posts in front of it, each topped by a round knob that Marlow at first takes for ornament. They are greeted by a young Russian whose clothes are patched all over with blue, red, and yellow, like a harlequin's, and whose face wears a child's grin. He is the son of an arch-priest, ran away to sea, drifted out here to trade, and has nursed Kurtz through two illnesses. payoff ⑤ ↑Marlow hands him the seamanship manual and he nearly weeps with joy: the "cipher" was Russian. In Act IV the book looked like a warm little mystery in the wilderness; the answer turns out to be a muddled, ardent, utterly defenceless young man. The one thing in the book devoted to doing a job right belongs to its most innocent character.

The Russian can't stop talking about Kurtz: you don't talk with that man, you listen to him; he has enlarged my mind. Piece by piece Marlow gets the truth out of him. Kurtz has raided the surrounding country for ivory with the people who follow him; chiefs crawl to see him; he has presided at rites Marlow declines to describe. He once threatened to shoot the Russian over a small lot of ivory. And he ordered the attack on the steamer — he did not want to be taken away.

Marlow raises his glass to the posts again. The knobs are not ornaments. They are heads, dried, eyes shut, turned toward the house. The Russian hastily explains that they were the heads of "rebels." Marlow laughs out loud: enemies, criminals, rebels — every word he had heard along the river turned out to mean the same thing.

Men carry Kurtz out on a stretcher. He is a skeleton, like an animated image of death carved out of old ivory. "Kurtz," Marlow notes, means "short" in German, and the name was as true as everything else in his life: he looks at least seven feet long. When he speaks, his voice is deep and full, nothing like a dying man's.

A woman appears on the bank, draped in fringed cloth, hung with brass, proud, wild, and magnificent. She walks to a point opposite the steamer, looks at it, and suddenly flings her arms straight up toward the sky. From the Russian's evasions, Marlow can guess what she is to Kurtz.

payoff ② ↑Only now does the reader think back to Fresleven and his two hens. Marlow told it like an absurd anecdote: the gentlest man alive, after two years in the noble cause, is beating an old man with a stick. Kurtz is the full-length version of that joke — he set out with the finest ideals in the book, and he has gone further than anyone in it from any way back. Conrad laid out the whole slope before the steamer ever left port, then shrank it to an anecdote so you would laugh and slide past it.

Behind Kurtz's back the manager tells Marlow that Kurtz's "method" is unsound and has ruined the district for trade. Marlow says it isn't a method at all; and he sees that the manager cares nothing about how many people Kurtz has killed, only about the business. Forced to choose between two nightmares, Marlow chooses Kurtz — and from then on the manager counts him among the unsound.

That night Kurtz's bed is empty. Marlow follows him through the grass and finds him crawling on all fours toward the distant fires and drums. Marlow puts himself between Kurtz and the firelight. He can't shout, or the whole forest will be on them; he can't appeal to God or conscience, because Kurtz has kicked all of that away. He can only speak to the man himself, and tells him he will be utterly lost. Marlow says afterward that Kurtz's intelligence was perfectly clear; it was his soul that had gone mad. In the end he gets Kurtz back aboard.

The next day the steamer leaves. The bank is packed, the woman at the front calling out over the water. The pilgrims raise their rifles; Marlow pulls the whistle again and again to scatter the crowd; she alone does not move. The pilgrims fire anyway.

Going downstream, Kurtz talks constantly. He gives Marlow a packet of papers and a photograph to keep out of the manager's hands; he speaks of my Intended, my ivory, my station, my river. One night while the steamer is laid up for repairs, Marlow comes in with a candle and sees cross Kurtz's face a look of sombre pride, ruthless power, craven terror, and intense, hopeless despair. As if at some vision only he can see, Kurtz cries out twice, in a whisper —

"The horror! The horror!"Part III · Kurtz's last words

Marlow blows out the candle and goes to the mess-room. A little later the manager's boy puts his head in the doorway and says, with scathing contempt: "Mistah Kurtz—he dead." (Part III) The next day the pilgrims bury "something" in a muddy hole. Marlow nearly dies himself. He would later call those words a judgment, an affirmation, a moral victory paid for by innumerable defeats; when it was his own turn to wrestle with death, he found he had nothing to say.

Act VI · Back in the sepulchral city: a drawing room a year later

Back in the city, the people hurrying about making money and drinking beer look to Marlow like intruders who know nothing of what he knows. A company man comes asking for Kurtz's papers; Marlow gives him only the report, with the postscript torn off. A cousin says Kurtz was a great musician; a journalist says he should have gone into politics as the leader of some extreme party. No one can say what Kurtz actually was.

What's left is the photograph and a small bundle of letters. More than a year later Marlow calls on the woman in the photograph, Kurtz's Intended. Dusk, a lofty drawing room, a grand piano gleaming in the shadows like a polished sarcophagus. She is still in mourning, her forehead pale, her gaze candid and trusting. She says no one knew him as well as she did, that his example will live on. Marlow agrees with her, and watches the room grow darker.

She begs him to tell her Kurtz's last words; she needs something to live with. Marlow hears those two words rising out of the walls around him. He says:

"The last word he pronounced was—your name."Part III · the ending

She gives a low cry, of triumph and of unspeakable pain: she knew it, she was sure. payoff ④ ↑At the Central Station Marlow said there was a taint of death in lies; it sounded like an honest seaman's self-portrait, and only now does it read as a debt coming due. The one plain lie he speaks aloud in the whole book is spoken to the person who trusts him most. He thought the heavens would fall. They didn't; the heavens do not fall for such a trifle. To tell her the truth would have been too dark, too dark altogether.

payoff ③ ↑The painting on the Central Station wall has found its sitter. A blindfolded woman carrying a torch looked, at the time, like an allegory with an oddly sinister air; this pale, unshakeable woman in the darkening room is exactly someone holding up the light of belief with her eyes covered — and Marlow has just tied the blindfold on her himself. Conrad never points it out. He just lets the two images face each other across the length of the book.

payoff ① ↑Marlow stops, and sits apart in the pose of a meditating Buddha. The Director says they have lost the first of the ebb. The narrator looks downstream, where the tranquil waterway leading to the ends of the earth runs sombre under an overcast sky, seeming to lead into the heart of an immense darkness. At the start, "has been" sounded like the past tense, a remark about Roman antiquity. By the end, "has been" has quietly become "still is": the darkness isn't in Africa; it is here on the Thames, among these respectable listeners, in that drawing room.

If you want the single fuse, it isn't Kurtz's greed and it isn't the "savagery" of the jungle. The fuse is the "idea" Marlow mentions almost in passing at the start: conquest can be forgiven because there is something behind it to bow down before. Kurtz is the only man in the book who believes that idea completely; he goes upriver carrying pity, science, and progress. Then, somewhere with no neighbors and no police, the idea runs to its logical end and becomes the postscript, the row of posts, the two words. Only a man with the highest ideals could fall that far. And Marlow's final lie protects exactly that idea.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · Little sticks in the fog: seeing first, understanding later

Replay it: Marlow is leaning out of the pilot-house watching the channel. The man sounding the depth in the bow suddenly lies flat on the deck; the fireman ducks. Marlow sees little sticks, thin ones, flying about and dropping around him with a soft patter. The river, the bank, the forest are all quiet. He thinks: how odd. Only a moment later does it land — arrows.

Why does it work? Conrad splits sensation from understanding: first what the eye takes in (sticks), then the name the mind supplies (arrows). The critic Ian Watt called this "delayed decoding." It puts the reader inside Marlow's body: in the instant of danger, this is how slow and absurd perception really is — shape first, meaning after. Then the pace snaps: helmsman, spear, blood, one blow after another with no transitions. And what is Marlow thinking? That Kurtz must be dead and he will never hear him talk. A Black man has just died for this boat, and the white narrator's grief goes to a white man he has never met. Does Conrad mean us to see that mismatch, or is he caught inside it too? That question is where the later controversy begins.

Two · Knobs on the posts: one word exposes a whole vocabulary

Replay it: as the boat draws near, Marlow sweeps his glass across the posts in front of the house. He has taken the knobs on top for ornaments. He brings one post closer and jerks back as if struck: it's a head, black, dried, eyes closed, the shrunken lips showing a thin line of white teeth, as if smiling.

Why does it work? First, it is delayed decoding again: the same knob seen twice, its true nature arriving only the second time. Second, the detail is minimal and exact: no blood, just a head that seems to smile, and it faces the house — an offering to the man inside. Third, the sharpest cut is the word that follows. The Russian says these were "rebels." The reader suddenly remembers: the warship was shelling "enemies"; the chained men were "criminals." The most effective tool of empire on this river isn't the rifle; it's the label — put a word on a person and you can do anything to them. Marlow's laugh threads the three words onto a single string.

Three · The lie in the drawing room: the heavens did not fall

Replay it: more than a year later, dusk, tall windows, a piano like a sarcophagus. A woman in black sits across from him, growing more fervent, saying she knew him best, that he was so good. The light drains away until only her forehead still glows. She asks: what were his last words? Marlow hears the two words rising out of the walls. He says: your name.

Why does it work? Because this is where the book's lighting makes its final reversal. At the start everyone assumes the light is in Europe and the darkness in Africa; here the room is in the heart of Europe and it is dark, and the only light is on the face of a blindfolded woman, lit by Marlow's lie. Conrad lets her keep talking and Marlow keep agreeing; you know what the truth is, you wait for it to be said, and it never comes. Marlow neither defends himself nor repents; he says the heavens didn't fall, and that the truth would have been too dark. It is hard not to agree with him, and hard to forgive him; the scene's force lies in making you do both at once.

The book's core devices

Heart of Darkness isn't a world-building novel; its core devices are two: the nested narration, and the "idea" itself.

First: the meaning is not in the kernel but in the haze around it. The narrator warns us early: most seamen's yarns have their meaning inside, like the kernel of a nut; with Marlow, the meaning lies outside, the way moonlight brings out a misty halo (paraphrase). Kurtz's words reach us through Marlow's memory, Marlow's through an anonymous passenger's retelling, and Marlow keeps confessing he can't put it into words. The further in you go, the less you are told: the Company Station is dense with detail, the Central Station runs on rumor, and at the Inner Station the crucial "rites" are blocked off by a refusal to describe them, until the center holds only two words with no object. What holds up: it captures how language fails, layer by layer, in the face of something beyond understanding. The cost: the not-saying sometimes shades into mystification, and the people placed on the unspeakable side of the line are precisely Africans and women.

Second: the "idea" is a machine run to its conclusion. The chain goes: ① conquest in itself is robbery; ② what makes it bearable is a lofty idea (civilization, progress, salvation); ③ the loftier the idea, the less its agents need be restrained — the core of Kurtz's report is "we appear to them as gods"; ④ remove every external restraint and nothing stops a man who sees himself as a god from turning boundless good into boundless power; ⑤ the postscript isn't a betrayal of the ideal but its conclusion. Steps ③ to ⑤ are the strongest. Step ④ needs care: Conrad presents the loss of restraint as the work of "the wilderness," as if the African land itself corrupts — exactly what later critics attacked. The novella allows another reading: what unmoored Kurtz was a company that measured only tonnage of ivory, and people who worshipped him, feared him, and couldn't refuse him.

What it forces you to think about

One · The lie in the drawing room: should he have told her the two words?

You watched a man die, and his last words were a curse on his own life. Now the woman who loved him and has mourned him for over a year begs you for those words, says she needs them to live. Do you tell the truth, or say her name?

Make the strongest case for the truth. She is not a child; she has a right to know whom she loved. Marlow isn't protecting her, he is protecting an illusion, and that illusion is exactly what the book indicts: Europe's drawing rooms enshrining "the great Kurtz" while knowing nothing of the river, which is how the next Kurtz gets sent upstream on the same wave of admiration. And by what right does Marlow decide she can't bear it? "She is too fragile for the truth" is itself the condescension that keeps women in "a world of their own."

The other side is just as strong. What would the truth buy? Kurtz is dead; not one post on the river comes down because she knows. All she gets is a ruined memory and a lifetime of chewing over her own blindness. When the truth can only wound, is insisting on it loyalty to her, or to your own fastidiousness about never lying? Marlow would rather let a lie rot in his own mouth than carry that darkness into her house. That needn't be condescension. It could be mercy.

Where does the author stand? Conrad passes no sentence. He has Marlow announce that he hates lies, then makes this lie the book's last act; "the heavens did not fall" can be read as relief, or as a deeper despair: that even a lie no longer has consequences is the darkest thing of all. Bring it home: a seriously ill relative asks about their prognosis; a friend who has just lost a partner asks whether that person truly loved them. The sentence you say — is it for them, or for you?

Two · A choice of nightmares: the honest monster, or the respectable manager?

Kurtz put heads on posts, but at the end he saw himself clearly. The manager never killed anyone with his own hands; he just runs a company that chains men together to work, with that smile always on his face. Marlow chose Kurtz. Whom would you choose?

Make the strongest case for the manager — the man everyone despises. At least he doesn't mount heads on posts. His evil is average, predictable, and bounded: he wants ivory, not an altar. Ten thousand managers can be bound by regulations, replaced by superiors, checked by auditors; once a Kurtz appears, nothing binds him. "At least he was sincere" is a dangerous aesthetic: it mistakes extremity for depth and a demon for a prophet. The voice that captivated Marlow is the same thing that brought the chiefs crawling.

The other side: in the manager's world no one is responsible for anything. The accountant balances his books to the sound of a dying man's groans; the manager calls Kurtz's method unsound while calculating how to recover his ivory. They aren't ignorant; they don't care. Kurtz at least went to the end of his evil and passed judgment on it; the managers can't judge anything, because there is nothing inside them.

Where does the author stand? Marlow plainly sides with Kurtz, but Conrad makes him pay: he is lumped in with the unsound, and ends up lying for Kurtz. Whoever chooses Kurtz inherits his darkness. Bring it home: a boss with spectacular results, ruthless methods, and the occasional moment of raw candor, versus a boss who follows procedure, never makes a mistake, and never cares about anyone — which do you fear more, and which are you more likely to become?

Three · Where there are no police: where does restraint come from?

Marlow's conclusion, roughly: civilized people behave because they have solid pavement under their feet, step delicately between the butcher and the policeman, and live in fear of the neighbors and of scandal — take all that away and you are left with only yourself. Is human morality mostly held up from outside?

Make the strongest case for "yes, mostly from outside." In Europe Kurtz was an idealist everyone admired, and his ideals weren't fake; the moment he entered a place where nobody was watching, they became a license for unlimited power. Most of us have never been put in that situation, so we don't actually know who we are. Rather than trusting our "nature," we should admit that good people are good largely because they live in other people's sight — which is why societies need institutions, oversight, transparency.

The other side: the crew are the counterexample. Hungry, more numerous, armed, with no police aboard, they hold back — something that grows from the inside, not imposed from outside. And Kurtz did not lack for eyes on him: the Russian, the chiefs, the whole forest watched him, but their gaze was worship and fear, and it lifted him higher. Outside eyes can restrain a person, and they can also make him a god; what finally stops someone may be only whatever is left inside.

Where does the author stand? Conrad lets Marlow propound the policeman theory, then puts its biggest counterexample on the same boat and leaves it unexplained. He offers an answer and leaves a hole in it he can't patch. Bring it home: a city where no one knows you, an anonymous account, a job whose boss is a thousand miles away — how different are you there from who you are among people who know you?

Motifs: what Conrad keeps writing about

First, isolation as a test: put a man far from home with no help at hand and see what is left of him. In Lord Jim (1900) a young officer jumps from a ship he believes is sinking and spends the rest of his life in a remote corner of the Malay world trying to redeem that jump; again, Marlow tells it. Second, the gap between noble words and squalid acts: in Nostromo (1904) a silver mine in an invented South American republic is sold as order and progress and ends up swallowing everyone; in The Secret Agent (1907) a bombing done in the name of an ideal kills only a young man with a child's mind. Third, the unreliability of telling itself: Marlow returns in "Youth," Heart of Darkness, Lord Jim, and Chance, always talking, always admitting he can't quite say it.

Heart of Darkness is the most concentrated link in that chain: isolation, idealism, betrayal, and telling, compressed into one river, one man, and two words.

Controversy and criticism

Achebe's charge: there is no getting around it. In 1975 the Nigerian novelist Chinua Achebe gave a lecture at the University of Massachusetts, later published as "An Image of Africa." His central argument: the book uses Africa as Europe's antithesis and as a backdrop, a place where a European soul goes to pieces; Africans have almost no names and no language, only cries and a few broken phrases; he singled out the fireman, whom Marlow compares to a dog in a parody of breeches walking on its hind legs. He called Conrad a racist — the original lecture's wording was harsher, softened slightly when it was collected. Defenders reply that these are Marlow's words, not Conrad's, that the nested frames are themselves ironic, and that to portray colonialism as robbery in 1899 put Conrad far ahead of his contemporaries. Achebe's answer was that irony can't cancel the fact that the book never grants Africans full personhood. The argument is unresolved, and today the book is almost always taught alongside the voices against it.

The place of women: Marlow says outright that women are out of it, live in a world of their own, and should be kept there (paraphrase). Every woman in the book is either a naive believer or a silent symbol; none has a voice of her own. Feminist critics read the final lie as the completion of that exclusion.

A fog of adjectives: F. R. Leavis admired Conrad in The Great Tradition (1948) but faulted this novella for its insistence on adjectives of inexpressible and incomprehensible mystery, substituting atmosphere for what ought to be shown (paraphrase).

Handling the history: the words "Congo," "Belgium," and "Leopold" never appear. The verifiable facts: from 1885 to 1908 the Congo Free State was Leopold II's personal domain; forced labor and violent extraction caused mass loss of life; under international pressure it was taken over by the Belgian state. In his 1917 author's note Conrad said the story was his own experience pushed only a little beyond the facts (paraphrase). Some argue that leaving out the names lends universality; others that it turns a specific historical crime into an abstract parable about "the darkness in man."

Adaptation confusion: Apocalypse Now was enormously influential, and people often take its scenes and lines for the novella's.

Crossovers

  • Philosophy · Kant on lying: In a 1797 essay Kant insisted you may not lie even to a murderer at the door asking where your friend is hiding. Marlow too "hates a lie," and tells one anyway. Unlike Kant's case, where a lie would save a life, Marlow's saves only an illusion.
  • Political philosophy · Arendt's "banality of evil": Watching the Eichmann trial (1963), Arendt saw great evil come from unthinking functionaries. The accountant balancing his books beside the dying has that shape. Unlike Arendt's case, Conrad also gives us Kurtz's far from banal evil, and has Marlow choose it.
  • Behavioral science · the watching-eyes effect: Bateson and colleagues (2006) put a picture of eyes above an office coffee honesty box, and payments rose markedly. It is Marlow's policeman theory in experimental form. The difference: Kurtz had no shortage of eyes on him; worshipful ones pushed him further.
  • Social psychology · cognitive dissonance: Festinger (1957) showed that when actions clash with self-image, people revise their reasons to ease the discomfort. Marlow's redeeming "idea" works like a painkiller for that dissonance. The difference: Marlow sees through the mechanism, and still lies to keep it intact.

Ten lines to take with you

① The Thames "has been" one of the dark places; by the end you see that "has been" was one word too many.

② Conquest is not a pretty thing; only an idea behind it redeems it, and the whole book takes that idea apart.

③ The gentlest man alive, after two years in the noble cause, beats an old man over two hens: the opening joke, and all of Kurtz in miniature.

④ The accountant's collar is spotless and his books balance; outside, people are dying.

⑤ Enemies, criminals, rebels: the empire's best weapon is the label.

⑥ The helmsman died for the boat; Marlow's grief went to a voice he had never heard.

⑦ The starving crew held back and the idealist did not; where restraint comes from, the book won't say.

⑧ Only a man with the highest ideals could fall that far.

⑨ "The horror!" is a verdict, and a victory paid for by innumerable defeats.

⑩ The heavens did not fall; they don't fall for a lie — and that is the darkest place of all.
