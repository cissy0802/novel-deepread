The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy

Douglas Adams · 1979 · first book of the series

中文 →

On a Thursday morning Arthur Dent is lying in the mud in front of his own house, in his dressing gown, with a yellow bulldozer parked a few feet from his head.
The council wants to put a bypass through here, and his house is in the way. He is hungover, cold and wet, and he has decided he is not getting up.
At the same moment, somewhere above him where no one can see, a fleet of huge yellow ships is quietly moving into position. They are building a road too, and the Earth is in the way.

Full spoilers ahead — how the Earth ends, what 42 is the answer to, what the Earth was actually for, and who those two white mice really are. Without those cards on the table there is no way to argue about whether this book is laughing at an absurd universe or at the way we go looking for meaning in it.

In One Line

An utterly ordinary Englishman is hauled off the planet twelve seconds before it is demolished, by a friend who turns out to be an alien; he is thrown out of an airlock, scooped up by sheer improbability, and shown round the factory where planets are made — and learns that the Ultimate Answer to Life, the Universe and Everything was worked out long ago. It is 42. Nobody knows what the question was, and the computer built to find out was the Earth, which was knocked down five minutes before it finished.

Where It Sits

Douglas Adams (1952–2001) was a Cambridge-educated comedy writer who wrote sketches for the BBC and worked as a writer and script editor on Doctor Who. Hitchhiker began not as a novel but as a BBC Radio 4 serial in 1978; in 1979 he turned the early episodes into this book. Four sequels followed — the famous "trilogy in five parts" — and this is the first.

Its place on the shelf is an odd one: it treats science fiction as an enormous stage for comedy. All the props of space opera are present — battle fleets, a Galactic President, supercomputers, manufactured planets — and every one of them is used to talk about bureaucracy, queuing, and the way an Englishman at the end of the world is still thinking about a cup of tea. After this book, SF had another direction open to it: not predicting the future, but using the scale of the cosmos to show up how ridiculous things are down here.

The Story

Act One · Thursday: Two Sets of Plans Nobody Saw

The book opens by panning out to the unfashionable edge of the galaxy: a small, unregarded yellow sun, a little blue-green planet going round it, and on that planet a race of ape-descended creatures so primitive they still think digital watches are a pretty neat idea. Most of them are unhappy most of the time, and most of their proposed solutions have to do with moving small green pieces of paper around. Setup ①The narrator also mentions, in passing, that one Thursday a girl sitting alone in a café in Rickmansworth suddenly understood how the world could be made a good and happy place — and before she could phone anyone, the Earth was gone. This, the narrator says, is not her story.

It is Arthur Dent's. He is about thirty, works in local radio, and has woken up to find a bulldozer at his front door. Mr Prosser from the council explains, with paperwork, that the plans have been on display for months; if Arthur wanted to object, he should have done it then. Arthur says he did go and look — in a cellar with no lights, in a locked filing cabinet, in a disused lavatory, behind a door with a sign on it reading "Beware of the Leopard." Setup ②It reads like a very English grumble. You laugh and move on.

Then his friend Ford Prefect turns up, looking grave, insisting they go to the pub. Ford is not, as he has always claimed, from Guildford. He is from a small planet somewhere near Betelgeuse, a roving researcher for The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy — an electronic travel book that has outsold the Encyclopedia Galactica — and he meant to spend a week on Earth and has been stuck for fifteen years. He picked the name Ford Prefect because he believed it would be nicely inconspicuous: he had mistaken the cars for the dominant life form.

Ford deals with the bulldozer by an elegant trick: he persuades Mr Prosser to lie in the mud in Arthur's place, on the reasonable grounds that no one is going to flatten a council official. Then he drags Arthur to the pub, orders six pints, and urges him to drink up and eat some peanuts — the body needs the salt, because matter transference is hard on it. The world, he says, is about to end in roughly ten minutes. Arthur stares into his beer and mutters that this must be Thursday; Payoff ① ↑he never could get the hang of Thursdays. The "one Thursday" from the first page is today. The narrator handed you the date of the apocalypse on page one, wrapped in the dullest day of the week, and you didn't notice; in the pub it leaks out of a hungover man's mouth as the least apocalyptic line ever spoken about the apocalypse.

The yellow ships stop overhead. From every radio, every pair of headphones, every loudspeaker on the planet, the same voice: Prostetnic Vogon Jeltz of the Galactic Hyperspace Planning Council announces that the Earth is scheduled for demolition to make way for a hyperspace express route. The plans, he points out, have been on display at the local planning department in Alpha Centauri for fifty Earth years, so it's far too late to start making a fuss — and if people can't be bothered to take an interest in local affairs, that's their own lookout. Payoff ② ↑Arthur's house and the entire planet are knocked down by the same sentence: the plans were on display. That morning's joke about the cellar and the leopard was not filler; it was the book writing its own synopsis. The same logic is run once on a house and once on a world. Mr Prosser and Jeltz never meet, and they say essentially the same thing.

And then the Earth is gone, in a matter of seconds. At the last moment Ford triggers the Electronic Thumb in his pocket — a little device for signalling passing spaceships, the way you'd stick your thumb out on a roadside — and the two of them are beamed aboard a Vogon ship.

Act Two · Vogon Poetry, and Thirty Seconds in the Void

In the hold of the Vogon ship, Ford pops a small yellow fish into Arthur's ear. It is a Babel fish: it feeds on brainwave energy and excretes something that lets its host understand any language at all. The first alien words Arthur ever understands are the captain's announcements. Ford hands him the Guide itself, with the words DON'T PANIC printed on its cover in large, friendly letters. Arthur looks up "Earth." The entry reads, in full: "Harmless." Ford, slightly embarrassed, explains that after fifteen years of research he filed a long piece, and his editors cut it to two words: "Mostly harmless." Setup ③Arthur shakes with fury that a planet can be dismissed in a word.

Only now does Arthur try to take in what has happened. The narrator shows him failing: England gone — he can just about manage that. America gone — he can't make it feel real. Then it occurs to him that there is no longer any such thing as a McDonald's hamburger, and he passes out. Our sense of disaster does not sort itself by size. It's the first moment in the book where you laugh and feel the floor drop away at the same time.

The Vogons find the stowaways. Captain Jeltz's preferred punishment is to read them his poetry — Vogon poetry being the third worst in the universe — while they are strapped into Poetry Appreciation Chairs. Arthur, trying to stay alive, improvises a reading of the poem so full of metaphysical depth he almost convinces himself. It doesn't work. Jeltz decides he is being buttered up and has them thrown out of the airlock.

According to the Guide, a person can survive in the vacuum of space for about thirty seconds if they hold a lungful of air. The two of them drift in the void, and with their thirty seconds almost gone, a ship scoops them up. The odds against that, the ship's computer later reports, were two to the power of two hundred and seventy-six thousand, seven hundred and nine to one.

Act Three · The Heart of Gold: A Stolen Ship and a Sealed-Off Brain

The ship is the Heart of Gold, the first ever fitted with an Infinite Improbability Drive. It does not travel through space; it makes the wildly improbable event "this ship is now on the far side of the universe" simply happen. The side effect is that all sorts of improbable things break out around it, which is why Arthur and Ford spend their first minutes aboard undergoing a series of absurd transformations and hallucinations.

At the controls is Zaphod Beeblebrox — Ford's semi-cousin, President of the Imperial Galactic Government, two heads, three arms. The Guide explains that the President has no real power at all: the job is to draw attention away from power, and Zaphod is superb at it. Not long ago, at the ship's launch ceremony, in front of the cameras of the whole galaxy, he stole it. He can't quite say why. He has discovered that part of his brain has been sealed off, and expertly — and the person who sealed it was Zaphod. There is a plan he himself is not allowed to know.

There is also a woman aboard: Trillian. Arthur recognizes her at once. Six months earlier, at a party in Islington, he was getting on beautifully with a mathematician and astrophysicist called Tricia McMillan when a man who said he was from another planet came over, and she left with him. That man was Zaphod. The last two living humans meet again on a stolen ship, while the man who took her from that party sits in the cockpit with his feet up. Trillian has brought two pet white mice aboard, Setup ④and she watches over them more attentively than over any of the people.

And there is Marvin, the Paranoid Android. His makers, the Sirius Cybernetics Corporation, fitted him with a Genuine People Personality, and what came out was bottomless depression: a brain the size of a planet, and they ask him to open doors. Set against him are the relentlessly cheerful ship's computer, Eddie, and doors that sigh with satisfaction every time they open for someone. Arthur wants a cup of tea; the drinks dispenser studies his taste buds and metabolism and produces something almost, but not quite, entirely unlike tea.

What Zaphod is looking for is a planet that exists only in legend: Magrathea, whose people were once the richest custom-planet builders in the universe, making private worlds for the very wealthy, until a galactic recession sent the whole planet to sleep for five million years. Zaphod has just parked above it.

Act Four · Magrathea: The Factory Floor of a Planet

As they approach, the planet plays them a polite recorded message: the commercial council of Magrathea thanks them for their visit but regrets that the entire planet is closed for business. The second message is even more courteous. The third comes with two nuclear missiles. Zaphod panics; Arthur, in the confusion, switches on the Improbability Drive — and the missiles turn, in mid-air, into a sperm whale and a bowl of petunias.

On the surface the party splits up. Arthur, alone in the dark, meets an old man called Slartibartfast, who takes him by aircar through a vast hollow factory beneath the crust, so huge it swallows the imagination, and shows him Earth Mark Two under construction. The old man designs planets — coastlines are his specialty — and his proudest work is the fjords of Norway, which won him an award. Only now does Arthur understand that the Earth didn't just happen. It was commissioned.

Slartibartfast plays him an ancient recording. Long ago, a race of hyperintelligent pan-dimensional beings grew tired of arguing about the meaning of life and built a supercomputer, Deep Thought, to calculate the Ultimate Answer to Life, the Universe and Everything. At the switch-on, two philosophers burst in on behalf of their union to protest that ultimate truth is philosophers' work, and to demand rigidly defined areas of doubt and uncertainty. Deep Thought says the job will take seven and a half million years — and that in the meantime the philosophers can make a fortune arguing on television about what the answer might be. Everyone goes away happy.

Seven and a half million years later, on the day of the Answer, Deep Thought tells the two descendants chosen to receive it: forty-two. Uproar. Deep Thought calmly explains that the Answer is fine; the trouble is that they have never actually known what the Question is. To find the Question it has designed an even greater computer, so vast that organic life itself forms part of its circuitry, to run for ten million years. The Magratheans built it. It was called the Earth.

Arthur thinks of that entry in the Guide: harmless; mostly harmless. Payoff ③ ↑The little planet the galaxy's best-selling reference book brushed off in two words turns out to be the most expensive computer ever built, and the ape-descendants shuffling green paper and admiring their digital watches were its processors. Adams never points at the match, but the two scenes face each other across half the book: an editor decided this planet wasn't worth the space, and it was carrying the biggest question in the universe. As for why it was knocked down five minutes before finishing — Slartibartfast offers no explanation, and neither does this book.

Act Five · The Mice, and a Question You Can Make Up

Slartibartfast leads Arthur into a dining room where Zaphod, Ford and Trillian are already seated. At the head of the table are Trillian's two white mice. Payoff ④ ↑The mice are what those hyperintelligent pan-dimensional beings look like when they poke through into our dimension, and they have been running experiments on Earth all along — humans thought they were experimenting on mice, and it was the other way round. Trillian didn't choose them; they chose her. The least noticeable prop in the book was hiding its biggest identity. That detail from Act Three — that she seemed to care about the mice more than the people — now means something else entirely.

The mice are pragmatic. With the Earth gone, a replacement would take another ten million years, and they can't wait: the galactic media is onto them, and there are chat shows and lecture fees to collect. Arthur was part of the computer's final generation, so the Question might be stored somewhere in his brain. They would like to take it out and dice it, and fit him with a simple electronic one instead — he'd hardly notice. Arthur declines. So the mice settle for plan B: just make up a question that sounds plausible. They start trying some out — "How many roads must a man walk down?" — looking for the one that will play best on television.

Talks collapse; two galactic cops burst in to arrest Zaphod and the group is pinned down. Marvin saves them: he has a chat with the police ship's computer, explaining his view of the universe, and the computer commits suicide, taking the cops' life-support systems with it. The party escapes to the Heart of Gold, and Zaphod suggests they grab a bite at the Restaurant at the End of the Universe. The novel stops there, like a radio episode going out on its closing theme.

Pull the whole book down to one fuse: it wasn't the Vogons who destroyed the Earth. It was a race of beings who, seven and a half million years earlier, asked a question they hadn't thought through. They wanted the answer without first working out what they were asking; and so there was 42, and a ten-million-year computer, and a planet full of unhappy people, and an Englishman lying in the mud. The Vogons were only the final blow — they didn't even know what they were demolishing. All the book's absurdity rolls downhill from that unexamined question.

Three Slow-Motion Scenes

One · The Man in the Mud, and the Fleet Overhead

The opening is a lesson in technique: tell the same story twice, once small and once huge, and never comment on the rhyme. The camera stays on Arthur first — dressing gown, mud, hangover, a bored bulldozer driver waiting. You are placed in a tiny, familiar scale: a local council, a planning notice, an ordinary man fighting the system. Then the narrator yanks the camera up into the sky and tells you another demolition crew is already in place.

The power is in the order of information. Start with the Vogon fleet and then cut to Arthur, and you have a routine "little man caught up in catastrophe" story. Adams does it the other way round. He lets you spend a whole morning annoyed alongside Arthur, siding with him against an unreasonable council — and when Jeltz says on the global PA that the plans were on display, you find your own indignation being held up as a mirror. Every word you threw at Mr Prosser can be thrown at Jeltz, and it will do exactly as much good. The pub scene is deliberately drawn out — beer, peanuts, Thursday — and then the Earth ends inside a single sentence. The longer the wait, the emptier the drop.

Two · The Life of a Whale

Once the two missiles have become a sperm whale and a bowl of petunias, the narrator doesn't follow the heroes down to the planet. He follows the whale. It has just come into existence several miles up, freshly conscious, and everything is new. It starts naming things: what's this — a tail? And this thing rushing past, let's call it wind. It sees a big round thing coming up fast and wonders whether it might be a friend, and decides to call it ground. Then it hits the ground.

It's the funniest passage in the book and the hardest to sit through. Technically, it hands the point of view to a creature that exists for under a minute, in an interior monologue as close as first person, so that a whole life — birth, curiosity, naming, hope, death — is compressed into a page. All its images are firsts: the first knowledge of a tail, the first feeling of wind. The reader knows it is going to hit; the whale doesn't. That gap is the entire recipe for tragedy, and Adams cooks it as comedy. The petunias manage one thought — "Oh no, not again" — and the narrator remarks that if we knew why they thought it, we'd know a lot more about the universe than we do. The book leaves it there.

Three · The Second After Seven and a Half Million Years

Adams builds the Answer scene out of pure waiting. The two chosen descendants sit before Deep Thought while a vast crowd waits outside; seven and a half million years of expectation press on this moment. Deep Thought warns them first that they won't like it. They say that's fine, just tell them. It stalls once more, checking they really want to know. Then it says: forty-two.

It works because the whole joke lives in the timing of the anticlimax. The build-up is religious — temple, chosen ones, oracle, millions of years — and the landing is a meaningless two-digit number. Adams later said he chose 42 because it was ordinary, a number with no particular associations. That's exactly the point: if the answer were 7, or 0, or infinity, readers would read something into it. 42 defeats every attempt. And then Deep Thought's mild explanation — you never knew what the question was — bends the laugh around a corner, away from the two hapless descendants and toward anyone who has assumed that having the answer would be enough.

The Ideas, Taken Seriously

One · The Improbability Drive: Turning Coincidence into an Engine

The premise runs like this. Everything has some probability of happening; however unlikely, if the probability isn't zero, it can happen. So if you could artificially set how improbable something is, you could make it happen. Scientists couldn't build an Infinite Improbability Drive until a student sweeping up the lab after a party reasoned that, since such a machine was "virtually impossible," he could build a Finite Improbability Generator, have it calculate exactly how improbable the infinite version was, feed that figure in — and the Drive would simply appear. A key component of the generator is a really hot cup of tea, as a source of Brownian motion. The student won a prize for extreme cleverness and was then lynched by physicists who had had enough of smart-alecks.

Which link holds? "Events with tiny but non-zero probability will happen given enough trials" is true; macroscopic quantum tunnelling, say, has odds of astronomical smallness, but they are not mathematically zero. Which link is there for the story? The idea that improbability can be dialled up is pure invention — probability is not a knob, and it isn't a fuel stored in an event; it describes our expectations of what will happen. This is a fictional premise, not physics.

As a piece of storytelling, though, it is very clever. Coincidence is the hardest thing in fiction to get away with: hero thrown into space, rescued by a passing ship within thirty seconds — any writer would be accused of cheating. Adams's solution is to build the coincidence into the story as a machine: the ship that rescues them runs on an engine whose whole job is to make impossible things happen. You can't accuse the author of abusing coincidence when coincidence has been made the universe's mode of transport. It is the old deus ex machina, the god lowered on to the stage to sort out the plot — except that Adams actually built the machine, and put a cup of tea in it.

Two · The Babel Fish and God's Puff of Logic

Attached to the Babel fish entry, the Guide offers an argument. The fish is so bizarrely useful that it can't have evolved by chance, so some take it as proof that God exists; but God's position is that proof would abolish faith, so at the moment of being proved he ceases to exist — and vanishes in a puff of logic. It's a send-up of the argument from design (the inference from the intricacy of living things to a designer), and also of the habit of tackling faith with formal logic at all. Adams isn't after a conclusion; he wants you to see how silly "proof" looks when it's pointed at certain kinds of question.

Three · 42: A Premise About Questions

42 is the book's true core device. Answer and question come as a pair; an answer on its own is useless. Deep Thought can't compute the Question directly, so it has to build a computer whose calculation is the living of organic lives — in other words, the Question can only be lived out, not worked out. That is a literary idea, not a mathematical one: there is no rigorous question that corresponds to "life, the universe and everything." But as a narrative device it welds the book's jokes to its philosophy. You laugh at 42, then realize that every bit of unhappiness on Earth was the computer, running.

What It Makes You Think About

One · If someone simply handed you the answer to your life, would you first need to know what you'd been asking?

On Deep Thought's side: the question matters more than the answer. The beings wanted an Ultimate Answer so they could stop arguing; but an answer without its question is just a number. The step they skipped — working out what they actually wanted to know — was the only part that was ever theirs to do. The shape turns up everywhere: someone grinds through the exams, the promotion, the mortgage, each step with a clear "answer," without ever asking what question all this is answering. When the answer arrives, they're sitting in that crowd outside Deep Thought, hearing a number.

On the mice's side — and this case is stronger than it looks: perhaps there is no such question, and making up a workable one is the most honest thing to do. Most of the meaning that keeps human societies running is made up: weddings, flags, holidays, degrees. None of them corresponds to some truth in the cosmos, yet they keep people alive, cooperating, recognizing one another. "How many roads must a man walk down?" is a fake question, but a comforting one; insist on finding the real one and, as Slartibartfast suggests, you may never find it and miss your life in the meantime. The old man's own solution is to forget about the meaning and keep busy — designing coastlines, say. He'd rather be happy than right, he says. Asked whether he is happy, he admits: no.

Where does Adams stand? He makes the mice greedy and ridiculous, so on the surface he mocks the fake question; but he lets no one find the real one, and even Slartibartfast's escape route doesn't make him happy. He seems to be mocking both ends at once: those who think there is an answer, and those who think they can get around the question.

Two · The plans really were on display for fifty years. Does that make the Earth's demolition procedurally just?

The strongest case for the Vogons: a civilization spanning a galaxy cannot halt every project for every primitive planet. The plans had a consultation period, an office, a deadline — that is the only kind of fairness a system that large can offer. Humans not checking the notices in Alpha Centauri is like residents not reading the noticeboard; the system has no duty to knock on every door. The bypass through Arthur's house might well have cut thirty minutes a day off tens of thousands of commutes. Stopping a road for tens of thousands because of one man's house is its own kind of injustice.

The case against is just as strong: a notice you have no realistic way of seeing is not notice; it is a document that protects whoever acts on it. Procedural justice assumes the people affected have a real chance to take part. Put the notice in a locked cabinet in a disused lavatory, or in an office four light-years away, and you have satisfied the procedure in form and abolished it in substance. This is not a distant situation: terms of service nobody reads, a demolition notice pinned in a window that opens at dawn, a public hearing held for two hours on a weekday morning. The procedure exists; people can't get in.

Adams's position is clear from the satire: he has Prosser and Jeltz say the same thing precisely so that you hear how hollow it is. But he offers no better procedure. He just lets the sentence be said twice.

Three · The world has ended and we're making jokes — is that courage in the face of absurdity, or anaesthetic?

For laughter: faced with a disaster too large for a human mind, laughing may be the only response that doesn't lie. Arthur cannot grieve for the whole Earth; he can only faint over hamburgers — not because he is shallow, but because that is the actual scale of human feeling. Comedy admits that the scale has broken and says so out loud; it gives you, in a universe without meaning, providence or God, a decent posture to adopt anyway: know where your towel is, and don't panic. That may be more honest than any amount of grandeur.

Against: laughter can also be training in keeping everything at a distance. In this book the Earth's billions get a subordinate clause; the girl who worked out how to make the world good is dropped in a sentence. You finish the book smiling, and what's left may be a sense that nothing much matters — the universe doesn't care, we are mostly harmless, so why worry? A way of telling stories that never lets you be sad for long may quietly be teaching you that you needn't be. The structure is familiar: someone turns redundancy, a break-up, a parent's illness into a running gag, and friends admire how well they're taking it, and only they know there's a part of it that nothing has touched.

The book leans toward the first view but keeps a shadow of the second: Arthur is the only person in it who is actually grieving, and the least funny character in it — the one being dragged along by the jokes without ever quite joining in.

Motif: What Adams Kept Writing About

Adams kept returning to roughly three obsessions. The first is the indifferent system: bureaucracies, corporations and rules that aren't malicious, simply unable to see individuals. The Vogons are the purest case, and the queues, forms and procedures of the sequels are variations on it. The second is machines with personalities: depressed Marvin, overeager Eddie, sighing doors; and in Dirk Gently's Holistic Detective Agency (1987), an Electric Monk whose job is to believe things on its owner's behalf, so the owner doesn't have to — a machine taking over the part of mental life people would rather not do. The third is the comedy of looking for meaning at all: 42 is where it starts, and the sequels grow steadily darker. By the last book of the five, Mostly Harmless (1992), the Earth is destroyed in every parallel dimension at once and nobody survives; Adams later admitted he had been in a bad way while writing it.

Beyond the series, he wrote Last Chance to See (1990) with the zoologist Mark Carwardine, travelling to see endangered species. He described himself as an atheist, and in later years liked to tell a parable about a puddle that wakes up, notices how perfectly its hole fits it, and concludes the hole must have been made especially for it — and keeps believing so as the sun comes out and it slowly evaporates. That parable and this book are of a piece: the planet that thought it was the centre of the universe was a computer built to someone else's order — and mostly harmless.

Controversy and Criticism

The loose structure draws the most fire. Born as radio, the book often reads as a string of comic set pieces, with the plot pushed along by the Improbability Drive and random turns; in the second half, on Magrathea, it nearly stops to explain its premise, and it ends abruptly mid-chase, as if unfinished. Fans say that is simply its rhythm; detractors say it's a run of sketches, not a novel.

On character, Trillian comes up again and again: the only major female character, with a background in mathematics and astrophysics, she makes almost no decisions that matter, and her part in the plot is mostly to have been picked up, to be found again, and to worry about her mice. Arthur, too, is widely seen as too passive — he barely drives the plot at all. That is by design, but it leaves the book without a spine.

Then there is the question of versions. Adams told this story as a radio series, novels, a TV series, a computer game and a film script, each different and contradicting the others, and he openly said he didn't care about consistency. The 2005 film adds plenty that isn't in the book, and readers should keep the two apart. In 2009, with the blessing of Adams's widow, the Irish writer Eoin Colfer wrote a sixth book, And Another Thing..., which split opinion. Finally there's the cult of 42: a universal password in geek culture. Some see that as a tribute; others feel it has worn the original joke smooth — the barb in it being that you never even thought about the question.

Crossovers

  • Philosophy · Wittgenstein: the Tractatus says the solution to the problem of life lies in the vanishing of the problem. 42 has the same shape — with the question unclear, any answer is empty. The difference: Wittgenstein asks us to let the question go; the mice want to forge one.
  • Neuroscience · the split-brain "interpreter": in Gazzaniga's split-brain studies, the left hemisphere invents reasons on the spot for actions the right hemisphere initiated, and believes them. Zaphod is doing the same — explaining a plan he can't reach, unable to say why he stole the ship. Where it breaks down: his wall was built deliberately. He operated on himself.
  • Sociology · Arendt's "rule by Nobody": Arendt called bureaucracy the rule of Nobody — no one left to hold responsible. Jeltz and Prosser are only carrying out the plans. The mismatch: the Vogons genuinely enjoy reading poetry at prisoners, so Nobody isn't quite nobody.
  • Psychology · Freud on humour: in his 1927 essay "Humour," a criminal led to the gallows on a Monday remarks that the week is starting well; Freud reads humour as the ego refusing to be crushed. Arthur's Thursday line has the same shape — except Arthur doesn't win; he's simply bewildered.

Ten Lines That Linger

① The world ends on a Thursday. You were told on page one and didn't notice.

② A house and a planet come down to the same sentence: the plans were on display.

③ A best-selling guidebook called the Earth "mostly harmless"; it was the most expensive computer in the universe.

④ Disaster doesn't register in order of size: England gone is bearable; no more hamburgers and you faint.

⑤ Thrown into space and rescued within thirty seconds — the author wasn't cheating; he turned coincidence into an engine.

⑥ A whale lives its whole life in a page, and dies hoping the ground will be its friend.

⑦ The answer is 42. The problem is you never worked out what you were asking.

⑧ The Question can only be lived, not computed — which is why the computer had to be a planet full of people.

⑨ The book's least noticeable props are two white mice, and they own the experiment.

⑩ Don't panic, and know where your towel is — in a universe without meaning, that's about as dignified as it gets.
