Novels, Deep-Read · Ralph Ellison · No.81

Invisible Man

Ralph Ellison · 1952 · Prologue, 25 chapters, Epilogue

中文 →

New York, the edge of Harlem: a building rented strictly to whites, and beneath it a basement everyone has forgotten. One thousand three hundred and sixty-nine light bulbs are screwed into the ceiling, all of them burning, all of them powered by current stolen from the electric company.
In the middle of the room a Black man sits pouring sloe gin over a bowl of vanilla ice cream while Louis Armstrong sings on the phonograph about what he did to be so black and blue. He wants four more phonographs, so five copies of the record can play at once — he wants not just to hear the song but to feel it.
He says he is an invisible man. He lives in a hole, but the hole is brighter than the world above.

This page contains complete spoilers — what the letters of recommendation really say, how Tod Clifton dies, who wanted the riot, and why the narrator ends up under fourteen hundred light bulbs. The book's beginning is its ending: without knowing how he fell into the hole, there's no arguing about whether he should climb out.

In one sentence

A nameless young Black man from the South, desperate to be a good student, a good employee, a good comrade, is handed an identity and a use in turn by the white men of his hometown, the Black president of his college, a paint factory and a radical political movement; each time he believes completely, and each time he discovers he was only a prop in someone else's script — until he drops into a coal cellar, burns every paper anyone ever wrote for him, and asks for the first time: then who am I?

Coordinates

Ralph Ellison (1913–1994) was born in Oklahoma City; his father named him Ralph Waldo, after Emerson. He studied music at Tuskegee Institute in Alabama, came to New York in 1936, and began writing with Richard Wright's encouragement; he served in the merchant marine during the war, started the novel in 1945, and spent seven years on it. It came out in 1952 and won the National Book Award the following year. It was the only novel he published in his lifetime; the second one occupied him for some forty years and appeared only posthumously, assembled by his literary executor as Juneteenth (1999).

Its place in literary history is unusual: a Black novel about Black life that refuses to be only a "protest novel." Behind it stood Wright's Native Son (1940) — rage, naturalism, a man crushed by his environment. Ellison packed Dostoevsky's Notes from Underground, Joyce, blues and jazz, and the Brer Rabbit of Southern folktale into one young man's coming of age, so that what happens to him is racial and, at the same time, the common condition of the modern self. Note the missing article: this isn't H. G. Wells's The Invisible Man. Nobody drinks a potion here; no body ever disappears.

The story

Act One · A Southern town: a grandfather's last words, and a briefcase

It begins at a deathbed. The narrator's grandfather has been meek all his life and never talked back to a white man, but as he dies he calls his son close and says something that terrifies the family: Foreshadow ①all his life he has been at war, a spy in enemy country, and they must keep the war going —

"I want you to overcome 'em with yeses, undermine 'em with grins…"— Chapter 1, the grandfather's dying words

Then he dies. The family treat it as an old man's raving and tell the children to forget it. The narrator can't forget it, and can't understand it either. He decides to be the grandfather's opposite: sincerely humble, sincerely obedient, so that white people will sincerely like him. At his high-school graduation he gives a speech on humility as the secret of progress; the town's leading white citizens are so pleased they invite him to deliver it again at a men's smoker in a hotel.

When he gets there he learns there is entertainment first. He and several Black classmates are herded into the ballroom and made to watch a naked blonde dance — a small American flag tattooed on her belly. Then they are blindfolded and set to fight each other in a ring, a "battle royal," until one is left standing. His mouth fills with blood. Next comes a rug scattered with coins: go on, grab it — and the rug is electrified.

Only then is he allowed to speak. He swallows blood mouthful by mouthful and recites his speech on humility word for word while the men laugh and drink. At the phrase "social responsibility" his tongue slips and he says "social equality." The room goes silent. He quickly says it was a mistake. They let it go, and he gets his prize: Foreshadow ②a brand-new calfskin briefcase containing a scholarship to the state college for Negroes.

That night he dreams of his grandfather at a circus, telling him to open the briefcase. Inside is an envelope, and inside that another envelope, and another, until at last there is a single printed sheet that says, in effect: To whom it may concern — keep this boy running. He wakes with his grandfather's laughter in his ears.

Act Two · The college: one afternoon, and seven sealed letters

The college sits in the Southern countryside, a campus as lovely as a postcard. In his junior year he lands an enviable assignment: chauffeuring a visiting white trustee, Mr. Norton, a Boston banker and the college's great benefactor. In the car Norton talks about his dead daughter and how he supports the school in her memory, and he says Foreshadow ③that the students are his fate — that the narrator is his fate. The narrator is flattered beyond words.

Norton wants to see the "real" countryside. Not knowing any better, the narrator drives him out to a cluster of old slave cabins where a sharecropper named Jim Trueblood lives — a name the college is ashamed of, because Trueblood has made his own daughter pregnant at the same time as his wife. Norton insists on hearing the story. Trueblood tells it like a practiced storyteller, almost with relish — the dream, the waking, the scar his wife left with an axe. Since it happened, he says, the Black church has turned him out and the college wants him gone, but white folks come to hear him tell it, and give him money; he's never done better. Norton, white-faced, hands him a hundred-dollar bill.

Out of the cabin, the old man is on the verge of collapse and needs whiskey. The only place nearby is the Golden Day, a bar and brothel, on the very afternoon when a group of shell-shocked Black veterans from the local asylum have been let out for their visit. Norton is carried into a roomful of madmen. One veteran — he had studied medicine in France, and after he came home masked men whipped him and drove him out of town for saving a life — takes Norton's pulse and then, in front of the narrator, tells him: look at this boy, a walking zombie; he has learned to repress himself; he believes in your great false wisdom more than you do.

Back on campus the president, Dr. Bledsoe, is livid. In front of white trustees he keeps his head down; in front of Black students he is a king. Since when, he sneers, do you show white folks whatever they ask to see? You show them what we want them to see. In chapel a blind preacher from Chicago, the Reverend Homer Barbee, gives a sermon on the college's Founder that leaves the room in tears; as he finishes he stumbles, his dark glasses fall, and only then does the narrator realize the man is blind.

The next day Bledsoe expels him, and tells him the truth: he got where he is by playing the role white people wanted, and to keep his place he would see every Black man in the country hanging from a tree by morning. Then he puts on a kindly face. Go to New York, earn your fees, come back in the fall. He hands over seven sealed letters of recommendation, addressed to trustees and great men in New York, and warns him not to open them.

Act Three · New York: letters, paint, and a machine

On the bus north he meets the veteran-doctor from the Golden Day, who is being transferred to a hospital in Washington. Up there, the doctor tells him, believe nothing — be your own father. He doesn't listen.

In New York the letters go out one by one and nothing comes back. The last one reaches the office of a man named Emerson; the person who comes out to see him is Emerson's nervous son, who hesitates a long time and then lets him read it.

Payoff ② ↑In his politest official prose, Bledsoe writes that the bearer has been permanently expelled and will never return; but in the college's interest, he asks, please let the young man go on hoping — like the horizon, which recedes ever further as the traveller walks toward it. In the dream in Act One there was an envelope inside an envelope and a sheet that said "keep this boy running"; now a real sealed envelope has been opened and says the same thing, dressed in respectable language. At the time the dream looked like a boy's anxiety. It turns out to have been the book's prophecy — and the hand that wrote the sentence wasn't a white town father's but a Black college president's.

Young Emerson pities him and points him to a job at Liberty Paints. The firm's famous product is Optic White — the current batch is for a national monument — sold under a slogan that comes down to: if it's Optic White, it's the right white. His task is to put ten drops of a black liquid into each bucket of white paint and stir; the black makes the white whiter.

Then he's sent down to the boiler room as helper to an old Black man named Lucius Brockway. Brockway mixes the base the white paint is made from, and boasts that without him, not a drop upstairs would come out white. He hates the union and suspects the narrator of being a union plant; they fight. When it's over, the pressure gauges are climbing, Brockway sends him to a valve, and he turns the wrong one — or is pointed to the wrong one. The boiler explodes.

He wakes inside a machine in the factory hospital, electrodes on his body, current pulsing through him. The doctors say the machine gets the results of a prefrontal lobotomy without the knife. Afterward someone holds up a card: WHAT IS YOUR NAME? He can't remember. Another card: WHO WAS BRER RABBIT? — the trickster of the Southern folktales he heard as a child. Something stirs in him that he can't put into words. They give him some money and turn him out.

Act Four · The Brotherhood: a new name

He collapses on a Harlem street, and a Black woman named Mary Rambo takes him home to her rooming house. Mary doesn't ask where he's from; she just feeds him and reminds him now and then that Harlem needs its young people to lead. In his room is a cast-iron coin bank shaped like a grinning Black man with huge red lips: put a coin in his hand and he flips it into his mouth. Disgusted, the narrator smashes it — and then can't get rid of it. He drops it in a garbage can in front of a house and a woman screams at him to take it back; he wraps it up and leaves it on the street, and a man chases him down to return the package. He can't shake that grinning figure.

One winter day he comes upon an old couple being evicted. Their belongings are piled on the sidewalk in the snow: a set of knocking bones, some yellowed photographs, a document dated 1859 — the free papers of the old man, Primus Provo. A lifetime's possessions, spread out in a small patch of snow. The crowd grows, and the narrator climbs the steps and begins to speak — not arguments, just these things, and "we law-abiding people." His words get hotter, the crowd surges forward and carries the furniture back inside, the police arrive, and he escapes over the rooftops.

A red-haired white man is waiting for him: Brother Jack, leader of a political organization called the Brotherhood, a man full of historical laws and scientific method. Readers easily recognize the American Communist Party of the thirties and forties, though the book never names it. The organization needs someone who can talk to Harlem. The pay is good, and there's a new identity. At a party Jack hands him an envelope: Foreshadow ④inside, a slip of paper with his new name in the Brotherhood, the name he is to use from now on. The novel never tells us what that name is — or what his old one was.

He moves out of Mary's. He gives his first speech in a huge arena, so blinded by the spotlight he can see nothing, and tells the darkness that tonight, for the first time, he feels more human. The hall erupts. Afterward the Brotherhood's theorists criticize him: too emotional, not scientific; he needs to study.

He studies, and does well. He meets Tod Clifton, leader of the Harlem youth, barely past twenty, handsome as a sculpture, loved by everyone. He also meets their sworn enemy, the street-corner Black nationalist Ras the Exhorter, an orator with a West Indian accent who calls the Brotherhood's Black members white men's dogs. In a street fight Ras holds a knife to Clifton's throat and can't bring himself to use it; instead, near tears, he asks: you're Black, you're beautiful, why are you with these white men? They'll betray you. Afterward Clifton says something strange to the narrator: Foreshadow ⑤sometimes a man has to plunge outside history, or he'll kill somebody — or go crazy.

An old member, Brother Tarp, gives him a filed-open link of leg chain: as a young man in the South Tarp said "no" to a white man, and wore it on a chain gang for nineteen years.

Then the wind shifts. A Brother named Wrestrum accuses him of building a personal following — a magazine has interviewed him and printed his picture. The committee pulls him out of Harlem and sends him downtown to lecture on "the Woman Question," where a wealthy woman invites him home to "discuss" it, and the discussion moves to the bedroom.

Months later he is suddenly recalled to Harlem: Clifton has vanished, the organization's "new strategy" is to de-emphasize Harlem, and members there are drifting away in droves.

Act Five · Forty-Second Street: paper dolls, and a funeral

He finds Clifton on the sidewalk of Forty-Second Street, kneeling over a piece of cardboard and hawking little paper dolls: a grinning Black figure called Sambo — the shuffling, dancing, eager-to-please caricature of American minstrelsy — that jigs on its own when set on the ground. The narrator, shaking with anger, spits on it. Later, when he picks one up, he sees why it dances: a black thread runs from the doll to Clifton's hand. From a few steps away you can't see it.

Payoff ⑤ ↑The narrator remembers "plunge outside history." Already after that street fight, Clifton had begun to feel that the "history" the Brotherhood handed them was a string, and he was tied to it. At the time it sounded like a tired young man's offhand complaint; here it comes due — he really has plunged outside, to the lowest place, and in the most degrading way he is performing, for everyone to see, the thing on the string.

A policeman comes to move him along and shoves him. Clifton turns and punches the cop in the face; the cop draws his gun and shoots him dead, right there on the sidewalk, a few steps away.

Without waiting for orders, the narrator organizes the funeral. Thousands come; an old man starts a slavery-era song. He offers no theory, only repeats: his name was Tod Clifton, he had no gun, and they shot him down.

Back at headquarters Jack and the committee are waiting. Who gave you the right to make decisions for the organization? We aren't here to ask Harlem what it thinks — we're here to tell it. At the height of the argument Jack strains forward and an eye pops out of his head and drops into a glass of water on the table — a glass eye, lost, he says, in the line of duty. Staring at the false eye looking up at him from the glass, the narrator understands: the man who talks all day about seeing history clearly is half blind.

Act Six · Rinehart, the riot, a hole

Ras's men are hunting him. To hide, he buys a pair of dark green glasses and a wide-brimmed hat — and at once Harlem starts calling him "Rinehart." A woman embraces him as her lover; a hustler wants to settle a numbers bet; a cop expects his payoff; a storefront church takes him for its reverend. Who is Rinehart? Runner, gambler, briber, lover, reverend — all of these, and maybe no one. The narrator never meets him. In a world where no one sees you, identity can be put on and taken off like a hat.

He decides to fight a different way. Payoff ① ↑He'll do what his grandfather said: say yes to the Brotherhood — yes and yes again, with a smile — and let them swallow his yeses until they choke. The family took those dying words for madness; it has taken the whole book for him to act on them for the first time — and the moment he does, he understands the bitterness in them: to wage war by obeying, you first have to turn your own life into a lie. To get information, he sets out to seduce Sybil, the wife of a Brotherhood big shot, and finds she wants him to play the "Black rapist" of her fantasies — one more person who can see only her own projection.

That night Harlem explodes. Stores are smashed; some tenants set fire to their own tenement; and slowly he sees it: the Brotherhood knew this riot was coming, and pulling back from Harlem was a way of waiting for it — a few deaths would make a few martyrs. Through the firelight Ras appears on a black horse with a spear, calling himself the Destroyer now, and orders the narrator hanged. The narrator seizes a spear and hurls it through Ras's jaw.

He runs. Two white men chase him; he steps into an open manhole and drops into a coal cellar. They look down, and put the cover back on.

Total darkness. To make light he opens the briefcase and burns its contents one by one: his high-school diploma; Clifton's paper doll; an anonymous letter he received at the height of his Brotherhood success, from a "friend" warning him not to go too fast and not to forget this is a white man's world; and finally the slip of paper with his Brotherhood name.

Payoff ④ ↑In the flare of the flame, the two papers show the same handwriting. The man who gave him his new name and the man who wrote to remind him of his place were one hand: Jack. The envelope in Act Four had looked like a rebirth; now it lies in a row with the dream envelope and Bledsoe's letters — one more identity written for him by someone else. Three times he has accepted the same kind of paper. This time he burns it.

He doesn't climb back up. He stays underground, steals current, wires up his bulbs, and thinks it all over — and he becomes the man of the Prologue.

In the Epilogue he says he's getting ready to come out. He also tells one more story: one day in a subway station a lost, white-haired old man asks him for directions. It is Mr. Norton. Don't you know me? the narrator says. I'm your destiny — you made me. Payoff ③ ↑Norton stares at him in alarm, doesn't recognize him at all, takes him for a madman, and hurries away. "You are my fate," in the car, was a benefactor's lyrical flourish; the narrator hands it back word for word, and the man doesn't remember ever saying it. On the white benefactor's lips it was a pretty phrase; for the young Black man it was a whole life.

The book's last line:

"Who knows but that, on the lower frequencies, I speak for you?"— Epilogue, the final sentence

If you want the fuse, it isn't the riot and it isn't the Brotherhood. It's the briefcase in Chapter One: a boy with a mouth full of blood accepts it, grateful. Everything anyone puts in it afterward — the scholarship, the letters, the new name, the anonymous warning — he takes as a gift and carries as part of himself. The whole novel is that briefcase getting heavier; and his awakening is burning its contents, sheet by sheet, in a coal cellar, for a little light.

Three slow-motion scenes

I · The electrified rug

Replay: the fight has just ended, and the boys' eyes are glued shut with sweat and blood. The master of ceremonies points to the money on the rug — it's all yours, go get it. They dive. At the first touch of a coin the current shoots from fingers to shoulder and jerks the whole body upright. The white men roar with laughter, and anyone who crawls off the rug is shoved back on.

The scene's power is that it turns an entire social arrangement into a device you can touch. The money really is there, and the rules are public; it's just that every reach brings a shock, while the men who invented the game sit outside it and laugh. Crueller still, the "gold pieces" turn out to be brass tokens advertising a make of automobile: what you take under the current isn't even real. Ellison adds not a word of comment — and right after it, the narrator takes the stage to speak about humility. The irony isn't in any line; it's in two things placed too close together.

II · Belongings in the snow

Replay: a thin snow is falling, and the sidewalk holds a rocking chair, a trunk, a box of odds and ends. The narrator crouches to look: a faded photograph, a set of knocking bones, some old clothes, a paper declaring a man free, dated 1859. As he sorts through them a heat rises in him, as if it were his own life being pulled out onto the street.

The passage is built as an inventory. Ellison doesn't describe the old couple's pain; he just lists things, one by one, and makes the reader crouch and sift with him. The trick is that no single item is worth anything, while together they are one person's entire evidence of the road from slavery to the twentieth century — free papers from 1859 lying in 1930s snow beside a broken chair. The speech that follows only shouts what the reader already feels — and it is the first time in the book he opens his mouth without trying to please anyone.

III · An invisible black thread

Replay: Forty-Second Street, crowds passing, Clifton kneeling on the pavement, jiggling his cardboard and calling out his patter while the doll twitches at his feet. The narrator recognizes him, holds his breath through the whole routine, and spits. A cop comes; the crowd scatters. A gunshot. The narrator stands where he is, a paper doll still in his hand.

The technique is to show you the doll dancing first, and only then why it dances. The black thread is the most literal appearance of invisibility in the book: from a few steps away the doll moves by itself; up close, there's a hand. Yet the narrator hasn't yet tied that thread to himself — he still thinks he's one of the ones holding strings. The reader understands first. He has to catch up step by step: at the funeral, in front of Jack's glass eye, in the light of the cellar fire. One image takes three chapters to turn from a prop into a mirror.

The book's core devices

I · "Invisibility": the rules of a metaphor

It isn't physical invisibility. The rule is: when people look at you, what they see is the person already in their heads. The narrator says so plainly in the Prologue:

"I am invisible, understand, simply because people refuse to see me."— Prologue

Follow that rule and every character becomes a projector. The town fathers see an obliging Black boy; Norton sees his "fate"; Bledsoe sees a pawn; Brockway sees a union man; the Brotherhood sees a specimen of "the Harlem masses"; Ras sees a traitor; Sybil sees a sexual fantasy; the street sees Rinehart. He is someone different in everyone's eyes, and has no face of his own.

The metaphor's deepest layer comes later: invisibility isn't only something others do to him; it's something he does to himself. He has always looked at himself through other people's eyes — through the town fathers' eyes his grandfather is mad, through the college's eyes Trueblood is a disgrace, through the Brotherhood's eyes Mary is a hick. So the turning point isn't that others finally see him; it's that in the cellar he no longer needs their eyes. This is a literary insight, not a sociological measurement — but it gives you a tool: the next time someone treats you as a category, you'll know what that feels like, and that you may be doing the same to someone else.

II · The end is in the beginning: a blues structure

The Prologue takes place after everything else in the book: the whole novel is a man sitting in a hole, looking back at how he got there. From page one the reader knows the ending is "a hole"; the suspense isn't what will happen but when he will finally understand.

The twenty-five chapters in between tell the same story four times: promised → believes completely → betrayed → down a level (see the diagram). That is the skeleton of the picaresque — the old form in which a hero drifts from station to station, meeting a new crowd at each — but the rhythm is closer to music. In an essay on Richard Wright, Ellison defined the blues, roughly, as keeping the painful details of a brutal experience alive in the aching consciousness, fingering its jagged grain, and transcending it — not by consolation, but by singing it. The Armstrong record is the book's instruction manual: the lyric asks what did I do to be so black and blue, and the tune swings, almost cheerfully. Turning pain into form is itself a way of not being crushed by it.

An honest flag: the Brotherhood is a literary organization, not a documentary portrait of the Communist Party. Harlem really did have riots in 1935 and 1943, and the novel's riot clearly draws on that history; but "the organization deliberately waited for the riot in order to manufacture martyrs" is the novel's accusation, not a historian's finding.

What it forces you to think about

One · What exactly is wrong with Bledsoe? — If you wear the mask long enough, is that winning?

Bledsoe says outright that to keep his position he'd see every Black man in the country hanging from a tree. And yet his college is real: under open, legal segregation, hundreds of Black students earn their diplomas there.

First, the strongest case that Bledsoe is right. In the Jim Crow South — the legal regime of racial segregation — the only way a Black man could run a school was to keep white people comfortable. If he stops performing, the college may be handed to someone else tomorrow, or cease to exist. The narrator drove a trustee to an incest scandal and a madhouse; that alone could evaporate decades of donations. Demanding that a man holding up a house in enemy territory "be his authentic self" means not knowing how many people live in that house. And what, exactly, is the difference between his mask and the grandfather's "overcome 'em with yeses"?

The other side is just as hard: the difference is which way the mask faces. The grandfather's yes was aimed at the oppressor, and he knew he was at war. Bledsoe has worn his so long that it has turned around to face his own people — he expels a student to protect his own position, and can no longer tell the face from the mask. When a man explains every bad thing he does with "I'm helping you from the inside," one day "the inside" becomes the goal itself.

Where does Ellison stand? He has the narrator end up "yessing" the Brotherhood too, so he doesn't think the mask is wrong in itself; but he makes Bledsoe the coldest man in the book, so the mask has a tipping point past which it eats the wearer. Bring it home: climbing inside an institution you don't believe in, did you ever draw yourself a line? How do you know you haven't crossed it?

Two · The organization says "sacrifice Harlem for now." Do you obey?

The committee's position: the organization has a national strategy, Harlem must be put on hold for the time being; your personal feelings are not history, and discipline is discipline.

First, the committee at its strongest. An organization trying to change a whole society can't make decisions by the mood of each neighbourhood; if everyone acts on the piece they happen to see, the organization falls apart and helps no one. Large change needs people who can keep the whole picture in view even while it hurts — and "the whole picture" has always meant that some places get set aside for a while. Discipline isn't oppression of the individual; it's the precondition for a group doing more than one person can.

The other side: an organization that tells you the suffering you see doesn't count is already looking at you the way your oppressors do. A committee downtown deciding what Harlem should think has the same structure as Norton in Boston deciding what the college should be. And the novel reveals something worse: "set aside for now" really meant waiting for Harlem to burn. The danger of "the bigger picture" is that it never has to say who the "part" being set aside is — or who will bury the dead afterward.

Bring it home: a company says "this quarter we protect the core business"; a movement says "hold off on your group's demands until we've won." How do you tell a necessary sequence from a "later" that will never come?

Three · Ras's question to Clifton: whom do you stand with?

Ras, in tears, asks Clifton why he goes with the white men — they'll betray you. The rest of the plot proves him right almost line by line. Yet by the end Ras, on horseback with a spear, demanding the narrator be hanged, is the most absurd figure in the riot.

First, Ras at his strongest. Every blow the narrator takes comes from letting a group that isn't his own define his fate: white town fathers, white trustees, a white-led Brotherhood. Why should someone who has never once been treated as an equal believe the next alliance will be different? Stand up on your own first, then talk about standing with others — otherwise "alliance" is just being adopted.

The other side: Ras draws "us" too small — too small to hold anyone who wants to be an individual. To him you are either brother or traitor, which is the same blindness as the Brotherhood treating people as "specimens of the masses." Ellison isn't choosing between white and Black; he's looking for a narrow road between two camps that both demand you surrender yourself. In the Epilogue he says America's essence is a "one" made of "many." The alliance he wants is one where no one first requires you to become invisible.

In honesty: plenty of readers — some Black writers of the sixties among them — think Ellison made a caricature of nationalism far too easily here. Bring it home: when you join a group, are you going there to be represented by it, or to be replaced by it?

Motifs: what Ellison kept writing about

One: a man swallowed by someone else's script. In the 1944 story "King of the Bingo Game," a poor Black man onstage in a movie house presses the button of a bingo wheel and can't make himself let go, until he forgets his own name — a first sketch of "keep him running." In "Flying Home" from the same year, a Black Tuskegee pilot crashes and is rescued by an old farmer, and is ashamed to be seen by this "backward" old man — another young man looking at his own people through white eyes.

Two: America is mixed, and the race question is the American question itself. Throughout his essays in Shadow and Act (1964) he insisted that jazz, the blues and Black vernacular are the very bones of American culture. The unfinished second novel pushes this to the limit: a light-skinned boy raised by a Black preacher grows up to be a race-baiting white senator, is shot, and lies in a hospital bed talking with that preacher. Invisible Man asks "who am I"; the unfinished book asks "who are we" — and in forty years he never found an ending he could accept.

Controversy and criticism

"Not angry enough." The early reception was almost all praise; by the sixties the wind had changed. In 1963 the critic Irving Howe, in "Black Boys and Native Sons," measured Ellison and James Baldwin against Wright and argued that a Black writer could not and should not sidestep the anger of protest; Ellison answered with a long essay, "The World and the Jug," arguing in effect that Black life is not only the part that is oppressed, and a writer is not obliged to write only the wound. Some younger writers of the Black Arts Movement saw him as a man far from the street. The argument has never been settled: is a novel that refuses to be a "protest novel" dodging its own era?

The women are props. This is one of the sturdiest literary criticisms. The women have almost no inner lives: the stripper is a projection of desire, Mary a symbol of motherhood, the wealthy woman and Sybil two satirical sketches of white women's sexual fantasies. You could say this is part of the invisibility theme — the narrator can't see women either — but it is hard to tell how aware the novel itself is of that.

The Brotherhood as caricature. In the thirties and forties Ellison moved in left-wing circles and wrote for left-wing publications. Critics on the left saw the Brotherhood as a cartoon drawn in the anti-communist mood of the early Cold War; others call this the most accurate part of the book. Both readings carry a political stance.

A long second half, and a second novel that never came. Many readers find the Brotherhood chapters too argumentative. For the next four decades Ellison published no other novel; by his account a 1967 fire at his summer house in Massachusetts destroyed a large part of the manuscript, though scholars disagree about how much. The writer who gave us "invisible" became literature's longest-running invisibility himself. The book has also drawn repeated challenges in American schools: in 2013 a county school board in North Carolina voted to pull it from school libraries, and after a public outcry reversed itself a little over a week later.

Crossovers

  • Neuroscience · Inattentional blindness: In Simons and Chabris's 1999 experiment, people counting basketball passes missed — about half of them — a person in a gorilla suit strolling through the middle of the scene. Same shape as invisibility: nothing wrong with the eyes, attention just goes to what's expected. The difference: there it's a capacity limit; in the novel, people choose not to look.
  • Sociology · Cooley's looking-glass self: Charles Horton Cooley proposed in 1902 that our sense of self comes from imagining how others see us. That is the narrator for most of the book, pricing himself through the eyes of town elders, the college, the Brotherhood. Unlike Cooley's ordinary, universal mechanism, every mirror here is a funhouse mirror, and he has to smash them to see himself.
  • Sociology · Goffman's front stage and back stage: In The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (1959), Erving Goffman treats social life as performance: on the front stage you play to the audience's expectations, backstage you take off the makeup. Bledsoe is the virtuoso — one face for the trustees, another behind a closed door. Where it breaks: Goffman's actors always have a backstage to return to; Bledsoe has played so long that nothing is left back there but power.
  • Buddhism · Non-self: Rinehart is gambler, lover, briber and preacher; put on the dark glasses and anyone can be him, take them off and he's no one. That echoes the Buddhist claim that the "self" is a passing bundle of the five aggregates with no fixed owner. But Buddhism treats seeing through the self as release; for the narrator, Rinehart brings vertigo, and what he finally wants is a self he can claim.

Ten lines to take with you

① Invisibility isn't the body vanishing; it's people looking at you and seeing only the person already in their heads.

② The fuse is a briefcase: a boy with a mouth full of blood accepts it, grateful.

③ The gold coins on the electrified rug are advertising tokens — what you grab under the current isn't even real.

④ The dream letter says "keep this boy running"; the real letter of recommendation says the same thing, in respectable prose.

⑤ The whitest paint needs ten drops of black, and all the white upstairs is mixed by an old Black man in the basement.

⑥ Free papers from 1859 and a broken chair in the same patch of snow: that's the whole history lesson.

⑦ The paper doll dances by itself — until you get close enough to see the black thread.

⑧ The man who talks all day about seeing history clearly keeps one of his eyes in a glass of water.

⑨ It takes him the whole book to act on his grandfather's "overcome 'em with yeses," and only then does he taste its bitterness.

⑩ He burns every paper anyone wrote for him for a little light, and then says: perhaps, on the lower frequencies, I speak for you.
