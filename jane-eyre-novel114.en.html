Jane Eyre

Jane Eyre: An Autobiography · Charlotte Brontë (as "Currer Bell") · 1847 · three volumes, 38 chapters

A cold November rain; no walk is possible today.
A ten-year-old girl has drawn the red curtains of a window seat around herself and sits with a book of British birds open on her knees, all arctic cliffs and ships caught in storms.
The curtain is wrenched back. Her fourteen-year-old cousin orders her to come and be hit, then flings the heavy book at her head.
She falls against the door and bleeds. For the first time in her life she hits back — and is carried off and locked in the red room where a man once died.

Full spoilers ahead. This covers whose laugh it is on the third floor; why the wedding stops halfway through the vows; why Jane walks out before dawn on a man she loves and who loves her; how close she comes to marrying a clergyman who doesn't love her and sailing to India; whose voice she hears on the moor one night; and what is left of Thornfield at the end.

In one sentence

A penniless, plain, unwanted orphan makes her way from a locked red room to the foot of an attic in another great house, falls in love with its master, and discovers, at the very altar, that the attic holds his wife. She wants to love and she refuses to be owned, and the book is the story of how she wins both at once.

Where it stands

Charlotte Brontë (1816–1855) was a clergyman's daughter from the Yorkshire moors. She and her sisters Emily (Wuthering Heights) and Anne published almost simultaneously, all under androgynous pen names. Jane Eyre came out in October 1847, "edited by Currer Bell," subtitled "An Autobiography." It was her first published novel; it sold at once, and literary London spent months guessing whether its author was a man or a woman.

Its place in the history of the novel is easy to state. It was one of the first English novels to hand the first person to an ordinary woman and let her tell her own anger, desire and conscience from the inside. It is also a graft: the stock machinery of Gothic fiction — the brooding house, the laugh in the night, the locked-away secret — put to work in a story about growing up.

The Story

Act I · Gateshead: the red room

Jane's father was a poor clergyman; her mother came from the well-off Reed family, who cut her off for marrying him. Both died of typhus. Jane's uncle, Mr Reed, took the baby into Gateshead Hall and on his deathbed made his wife promise to raise her as her own. Mrs Reed promised, and then spent ten years proving that promising and doing are different things.

After the book hits her, Jane screams at her cousin John that he is like a murderer, like a slave-driver — words she has picked up from her reading. She is dragged to the red room: the room where her uncle died, unslept-in for nine years, hung in deep crimson, with a great mirror. In the glass she looks pale and glittering-eyed, like one of the little sprites in the stories her nurse told. A light slides across the wall; she is certain her uncle's ghost has come back, throws herself at the locked door screaming, and faints. The light, she later supposes, was probably someone crossing the lawn with a lantern.

Soon after, Mr Brocklehurst, who runs the charity school at Lowood, comes to inspect her — black-clad, tall as a pillar. What must she do to avoid hell? Jane thinks it over: keep in good health and not die. Mrs Reed tells him, in front of the child, that she is a liar. When he has gone, Jane stands in front of her aunt and says it: I am not a liar; I will never call you aunt again; if anyone asks, I will tell them how you treated me. Mrs Reed is actually frightened. Jane feels as if a stretch of moorland has caught fire, and when it burns out there is only blackness and a bitter taste. It is the book's first pattern: she has to speak, speaking sets her free, and freedom does not taste only sweet.

Act II · Lowood: hunger, humiliation, a friend who endures

Lowood takes in poor girls and feeds them burnt porridge. Brocklehurst preaches plainness and orders one girl's naturally curly hair cut off, while his own wife and daughters sweep into the schoolroom in velvet and plumes.

Jane meets two people here. Miss Temple, the superintendent, sees the porridge is inedible and orders bread and cheese for the whole school on her own authority. Helen Burns, a few years older, always reading, is regularly flogged across the neck with a bundle of twigs in front of the class. She neither cries nor resents it; life is too short, she says, to nurse grudges. It is the first time Jane has heard of another way to answer injustice. She doesn't agree. She remembers.

On the day of an inspection Jane drops her slate and it shatters. Brocklehurst has her stand on a stool and announces to the school that she is a liar. Up there she can hardly breathe; Helen walks past, looks up and smiles at her, and that is enough. Afterwards Miss Temple writes to check the facts and clears her name in front of everyone.

In spring, typhus sweeps through the school. Helen has consumption. Jane creeps into her sickroom at night and gets into her bed; they talk until they fall asleep, and in the morning Helen is dead in her arms. Many years later a stone is put on the grave with a single Latin word: Resurgam — I shall rise again.

Jane stays eight years at Lowood, six as a pupil and two as a teacher. When Miss Temple marries and leaves, Jane looks at the road outside the window and realises she has never been out of the valley. She doesn't dare ask for liberty; she asks only for a new servitude, and advertises for a post as a governess.

Act III · Thornfield: a laugh, a fall, a bed on fire

Thornfield Hall is a grey battlemented house. The housekeeper, Mrs Fairfax, is kind and talkative; Jane's pupil is a little French girl, Adèle, the ward of the master, Mr Rochester, who is never at home.

On her first day Mrs Fairfax shows her the third storey: old furniture, a row of shut doors, a narrow dark passage like a corridor in Bluebeard's castle. Jane hears a laugh — low, distinct, not like anyone laughing. Mrs Fairfax calls "Grace!" and a stolid, heavy-set servant comes out of a door; the laughing stops. That's Grace Poole, says the housekeeper, who does the sewing and is given to larking. Setup ① Jane believes it, and so do we: a gloomy old house is allowed a few odd noises. Later she often walks that corridor alone, restless beyond control: women are supposed to be calm, but women feel just as men do, and need room for their faculties as much as their brothers do. Halfway through the thought, the laugh comes again from behind a door.

One January dusk, walking to the village to post a letter, she sees a horse slip on the icy lane and throw its rider, who sprains his ankle and has to lean on her shoulder to limp back to the saddle. Back at the house, the rider's big dog, Pilot, is lying by the fire: the master has come home. Rochester is perhaps thirty-five, heavy-browed, square-faced, not handsome. He asks her straight off whether she bewitched his horse, and then whether she thinks him handsome. No, she says. He likes that, and begins to send for her in the evenings. He tells her where Adèle came from: her mother was a Paris opera dancer, his mistress, who deceived him with another man and later ran off and left the child. He brought the girl to England without believing she is his.

One night a strange laugh wakes Jane; the corridor is full of smoke. Rochester's bed curtains are burning and he is sound asleep. She empties the basin and the ewer over the flames and over him. Setup ② He goes up to the third floor alone, is gone a long time, and comes back to say: it was Grace Poole; say nothing. Then he holds both her hands and won't let go, calling her his preserver. She doesn't sleep; her mind is a sea with waves rising.

Act IV · Blanche, a gypsy, a night on the third floor

Next day he rides off to stay with neighbours who include the beautiful Blanche Ingram, whom everyone expects him to marry. That night Jane passes sentence on herself. She draws her own face in chalk in front of a mirror, unflattered, then paints an imagined Blanche on ivory with her best colours, and labels the first one, roughly: Portrait of a Governess, disconnected, poor, and plain. Setup ③

He comes back with a houseful of guests, and Blanche mocks governesses within Jane's hearing. From the shadow of a window curtain Jane understands: he doesn't love Blanche, and Blanche wants his money. That hurts more than if he loved her. One evening an old gypsy woman arrives to tell the ladies' fortunes. When Jane's turn comes, the crone by the library fire talks and talks until her voice changes. It is Rochester in disguise, trying to read her heart, and failing.

The same day a stranger named Mason arrives from Jamaica. At the name, Rochester grips Jane's shoulder and says he has had a blow. At midnight a scream from the third floor wakes the house. He calms the guests, then quietly fetches Jane with a sponge and smelling salts. Behind a tapestry Mason lies soaked in blood, knifed and bitten in the arm. Rochester goes for a surgeon and locks Jane in with the wounded man; from the inner room comes a snarling like an animal's. She sponges blood all night while, in the candlelight, the carved Judas on a cabinet seems about to come alive.

In the morning, in the garden, Rochester puts a "suppose" to her. A young man commits a great error abroad — not a crime, an error — and spends years in dissipation. Now he meets a stranger who could make him new. May he defy convention to win her? Jane answers that no one's reformation should depend on another human being; look higher.

Act V · A deathbed, a chestnut tree, a letter

Word comes from Gateshead: cousin John has died in debt, and Mrs Reed has had a stroke. On her deathbed the aunt gives up a secret. Three years ago Jane's uncle, John Eyre, wrote from Madeira wanting to adopt her and leave her his fortune. Mrs Reed, hating the child, wrote back that Jane had died of typhus at Lowood. Setup ④ Jane forgives her. Mrs Reed dies unreconciled.

On a June evening, back at Thornfield, Jane meets Rochester in the orchard. He tells her he will soon be married and has found her a post in Ireland. He says there is a string tied under his left ribs, knotted to the same place in her; put the sea between them and it will snap, and he fears he will bleed inwardly.

Jane weeps, then makes the speech everyone remembers. In substance: do you think that because I am poor, obscure, plain and little, I have no soul and no heart? I have as much soul as you and as much heart. It is not custom or flesh speaking now, but my spirit addressing yours, as if we had both passed through the grave and stood before God as equals. Payoff ③ ↑ Poor; plain — the very words she had written under her chalk self-portrait months earlier as a punishment. Now they come out of her mouth facing the other way: not "and therefore I am nothing," but "and even so we are equal." She doesn't deny being poor or plain. She takes the words back.

He proposes. There never was a Blanche: he had let it be rumoured that his fortune was a third of what people thought, watched the Ingrams cool, and kept up the courtship only to make Jane jealous. She says yes. That night a storm breaks, and the great horse-chestnut at the bottom of the orchard is struck by lightning and half of it split away. Setup ⑤

The month of the engagement is dangerously sweet. He wants to dress her in silk and hang the Thornfield heirlooms on her, and calls her his fairy. She thinks he smiles at her the way a sultan smiles at a slave his gold has decked out. She won't be an angel; she'll be herself. So as not to depend on him entirely, she writes to her uncle in Madeira to tell him she is to be married.

One night before the wedding she wakes to find a tall woman in her room, with thick dark hair and a purplish face, like the vampire of German legend. The woman puts Jane's wedding veil on her own head, looks in the mirror, tears the veil in two and tramples it. Rochester goes pale when he hears, then says: half dream, half real — it was Grace Poole. In a year and a day he will tell her why.

Act VI · Halfway through the vows

The church is nearly empty. When the clergyman asks whether anyone knows an impediment, a voice says yes. A London solicitor stands there with Mason behind him, and reads it out: fifteen years ago, in Jamaica, Mr Rochester married Mason's sister, Bertha Mason. She is alive, and living at Thornfield.

How did the solicitor know? Jane's letter. Her uncle in Madeira did business with the Masons' firm, and Mason, on his way home to Jamaica, happened to be with him when it arrived. Too ill to travel, the uncle sent Mason back to England to stop the marriage. Payoff ④ ↑ When Mrs Reed confessed, this uncle looked like nothing more than a belated might-have-been. The letter Jane wrote to keep a little independence is what brought the truth to the church door. The step she took to protect herself is the step that cost her the marriage — and the uncle hasn't finished with her yet.

Rochester makes no defence. He meant to be a bigamist, he says, and fate has outplayed him; come, all of you. He leads them up to the third floor and lifts the tapestry. Grace is cooking at the fire. At the far end of the room a figure scrambles about on all fours, hair like a mane over its face. It springs at him, grips his throat and bites his cheek, and he ties it to a chair and turns round: this is my wife. Payoff ① ↑ The laugh from Jane's first day was hers; Grace was never the culprit, only the keeper. Look back and the laugh always sounded at Jane's most rebellious moments — she was thinking that women need room for their faculties, and it laughed from behind the door. This is the boldest move in the book: the heroine's restlessness and rage have a double upstairs, locked away and mad.

That afternoon he tells her everything. As a younger son, he was sent by his father to Jamaica to marry a beauty with thirty thousand pounds. Only afterwards did he learn that her mother was shut in an asylum, a fact the family had hidden, and Bertha herself went mad. English law gave him no divorce on those grounds. He locked her on the third floor and spent ten years drifting round Europe through a series of mistresses, each emptier than the last.

He begs her to come with him to a white villa on the Mediterranean and live as his wife. He pleads, he rages, he says he could snap her with one hand, but what he wants is her consent. She loves him, and she knows no one in the world would be hurt by what she does — she has no family; who would care? Then she answers herself: I care for myself. The more friendless and unsustained I am, the more I will respect myself. Principles are for exactly this moment; they are worth nothing if kept only when there is no temptation. That night she dreams the moon turns into a woman who tells her: daughter, flee temptation. Before dawn, with twenty shillings, she slips out by a side door.

Act VII · The moor, Moor House, a man who wants her soul

Twenty shillings carries her only as far as Whitcross, a stone post at a crossroads on the moor. She has left her parcel in the coach and owns nothing. She sleeps in the heather, is turned away when she asks for work, is suspected when she offers her gloves for bread, and at last asks a little girl for the cold porridge she is about to tip into a pig trough, and eats it. On the third night, in cold rain, she follows a light to a house, is sent off by the servant, and collapses on the step: there is nothing left but to die.

A man behind her says that all men must die, but not all are condemned to a slow, premature death. He is the clergyman St John Rivers, and he and his sisters Diana and Mary take her in. She gives a false name. St John is handsome as a Greek statue, cold, severe, and set on missionary work in India. He finds her a post teaching poor girls in the village of Morton. A rich, lovely young woman, Rosamond Oliver, loves him, and he loves her, but he has decided she could never be a missionary's wife; he takes out his watch, allows himself a quarter of an hour of thinking about her, then gets up and leaves.

One day he tears a narrow strip from the margin of the paper Jane rests her hand on while painting, and pockets it: absent-mindedly, she has written her real name there. On a snowy evening he sits by her fire and slowly tells the story of an orphan, then gives her the news. John Eyre of Madeira is dead and has left twenty thousand pounds to his niece Jane Eyre — and St John's own mother was John Eyre's sister. They are cousins. Jane's first thought is not the money but that she has family. She insists on splitting it four ways, five thousand each. Payoff ④ ↑ The uncle Mrs Reed hid away has now intervened twice: once to wreck her wedding, once to give her money and a family. At last she is not "disconnected" — and not through marriage or anyone's charity.

Then St John begins to want her. He has her drop German and learn Hindustani with him, and watches her work and endure and obey the way one tests a tool. On the moor at summer's end he tells her she was formed for labour, not for love; God means her to be a missionary's wife; come to India. She says she will go as his sister and his helper, not as his wife. He says no: marry, or refuse God. After that he turns on her a coldness like ice pressed to the chest. He never loses his temper. He just freezes her by degrees. She knows that if she marries him he will have all her strength while her heart is locked away to starve.

One night, after prayers, he asks again, more gently than ever, and she nearly gives in: if she could only be sure it was God's will, she would marry him now. The candle is guttering; the room is full of moonlight. Her heart jolts and she hears a voice from nowhere calling her name — Jane! Jane! Jane! — Rochester's voice, in pain and urgent. She cries into the dark: I am coming, wait for me! Where are you? There is only the wind on the moor.

Act VIII · Ferndean: "Reader, I married him"

Thirty-six hours later she crosses the Thornfield orchard and looks up at a blackened shell. The innkeeper tells her what happened. After the governess vanished, the master sent away the housekeeper, put Adèle in school and shut himself up. One night Grace got drunk again; the woman upstairs took the keys, set fire to the hangings in the room next to hers, then went down a floor and set fire to the bed the governess used to sleep in. Payoff ② ↑ Jane put out the first fire with a water jug; this time there was no one with a jug. Bertha's first fire was Rochester's bed, her last was Jane's — and with it she burned down the house that had held her for ten years, along with the secret it was built to keep. Rochester got the servants out, then climbed to the roof to save her. She stood on the battlements with her hair streaming in the firelight, shouted, and jumped. He was inside when the staircase fell. When they brought him out, one eye was gone, one hand had to be amputated, and the other eye was blind.

He lives now at Ferndean, a damp old manor deep in a wood thirty miles away. In a fine evening drizzle Jane sees him come to the door, put out his good hand to feel for rain, and grope his way back in. She takes the tray of water and candles from the servant and carries it in herself. Pilot leaps up; he knows her. Who is it, Rochester asks. Pilot knows me, she says; I came only this evening.

She makes him wait a little, mentioning her handsome clergyman cousin until his jealousy flares, before telling him she doesn't love the man. He says he is no better than the old lightning-struck chestnut in the Thornfield orchard; what right has such a ruin to keep a flowering woodbine beside it? She tells him he is no ruin and no lightning-struck tree; he is green and strong, and plants will grow round his roots and lean on him whether he asks them to or not. Payoff ⑤ ↑ On the night of the proposal the split tree read like a curse: this marriage is not allowed. Now Rochester picks up the image himself to describe his maimed body, and Jane rewrites it. The split tree is not dead. It just no longer towers over everything; from now on it grows with what is beside it.

He tells her that a few nights ago, a Monday, near midnight, he sat at his window and called her name three times in despair, and heard a voice answer: I am coming, wait for me — and then, where are you? Those were her words on the moor. Afraid of unsettling him, she says nothing.

The last chapter begins: "Reader, I married him." (ch. 38). By the time she writes the book they have been married ten years; she is his eyes and his right hand. In time one eye partly recovers, enough for him to see their first child, whose eyes are what his once were, large and black. The book's last lines go to St John, dying in India, ready to meet his Master.

The fuse

The fuse isn't at Thornfield. It's in the red room at Gateshead: a ten-year-old locked up because she spoke and hit back. Everything after echoes the same question. If she speaks, will they call her mad? Must what she wants be paid for by being shut away? Lowood tries to grind her into a silent poor girl, Rochester to dress her up as a pampered doll, St John to forge her into a tool for God; the woman on the third floor is what she might become if she lost hold of herself. Each time she speaks, and then she leaves. The ending isn't someone rescuing her from the red room. She opens the door herself, once she has money, family, and someone she can love as an equal.

Three slow-motion scenes

1 · The mirror in the red room

The camera sits very low here. The narrator is the adult Jane, but she tells us only what a ten-year-old knew. What the light was gets a flat, belated "probably a lantern" after the child has fainted, so we are frightened along with her. The mirror is the key prop: for the first time the child sees herself from outside, and what she sees is the strange creature others see. That mirror never really goes away. She sits before one to draw her "poor and plain" self; Rochester calls her elf and sprite; Bertha tries on the veil before the glass, and Jane sees that face reflected. Brontë never points it out. She simply puts a mirror in the room each time the question of who Jane is gets reopened.

2 · The string under the ribs

The proposal is built out of delay. Rochester has already decided to propose, but he takes the long way round: the marriage, Ireland, the string. He is testing her, and we are dragged through the test too. He holds all the information and sets the whole rhythm until Jane breaks and speaks, and only then does control change hands. Everything he says after that is a reply. The sweetness gets one page: the wind rises, cloud swallows the moon, and next morning Adèle runs in to say the tree was split in the night. The finest thing about the scene is that the speech isn't made in triumph. She makes it believing she has already lost him. Equality is not granted to her; she claims it with nothing in her hands.

3 · Snow in June

The walk from the church back to the house gets a sentence or two: Rochester in front, the others following in silence. A narrator who has dissected herself at every turn goes quiet just where we most expect her to speak, and the silence pushes us straight up to the door. Her inner voice comes back only that afternoon, alone in her room: yesterday an expectant bride, today a cold solitary girl again, as if a December storm had blown through June and frozen every flower and fruit (roughly). Weather instead of tears is her habit throughout: the cold rain at the start, the storm on the night of the proposal, the drizzle at Ferndean. Weather is how this book measures the inside of a person.

The core device: an "I" looking back

The subtitle, "An Autobiography," is the device. The teller is Jane ten years married. She knows everything, but she tells it in the order her younger self found it out. That gives two voices at once: the eyes of the child, the girl and the lover, frightened, eager, often wrong; and an adult judgement that surfaces now and then to lead the way.

The second device is "Reader." Jane turns and addresses you directly, more than once. It isn't a mannerism. It's an alliance: she has no family and no friends, and the one person she can trust is you, holding the book. A governess sat between servants and masters, and neither side wanted her. This one buttonholes the reader and makes us hear her whole life out.

The third is Gothic props with realist explanations. The red-room light was a lantern, the gypsy was Rochester, the ghost upstairs is a sick woman. Brontë hooks us with the full Gothic apparatus and then dismantles it piece by piece, to show that what is truly frightening is what people do to each other. Only one thing she leaves standing: the call across the moor. Time and words match, and no explanation is offered. This is a novelist's choice, not a claim about the paranormal. A woman who has come this far on reason and principle is finally called home by something reason can't account for.

What it makes you think about

One · The person you love has been chained for fifteen years to a marriage that exists only on paper, and the law won't let him out. He asks you to come away to a place where no one knows you. Do you go?

The case for going can be made better than Rochester makes it. The marriage was a fraud from day one: her family concealed the madness, his father and brother sold him for a dowry. Bertha no longer recognises anyone and cannot be hurt by his loving someone else. The law that forbids divorce on grounds of insanity is a cruelty of law, not a truth of morals. Jane has no relatives to be shamed. Refusing him preserves an abstract rule and wrecks the rest of two real lives — and in fact, after she leaves, he nearly dies and she nearly starves on the moor.

The case for staying away is strongest not on religious grounds but on Jane's position. Without money, family or standing, she would be a mistress kept in a villa, exactly like the ones he described to her with such weariness and contempt. He loves her today; and tomorrow? Someone wholly dependent on another person cannot treat "he loves me" as insurance. She isn't keeping someone else's rule. She is keeping a line she drew herself: not to hand herself over until she can stand in front of him as an equal.

The author is on the side of leaving, but she doesn't let it come cheap; the flight reads like tearing off a piece of her own flesh. Bring it home: being with someone whose marriage is dead but not yet dissolved; taking a job you love with no contract, on nothing but the boss's word. The real question isn't whether the rule is right. It's whether the choice you make now leaves a way out for the future self who might be let down.

Two · Someone serious, high-minded and impressed by your abilities asks you to marry him and do something great together. He doesn't love you. Do you say yes?

The case for St John. He offers Jane what Rochester never did: an invitation addressed to someone capable rather than someone lovable. Rochester calls her a fairy and wants her in silk; St John sees that she can work hard, learn a language, and keep a school going. He doesn't lie to her, hide a wife, or play testing games. He has given up the woman he really loves, and he measures Jane with the same rule he applies to himself. What he wants to do is, by both their beliefs, the greatest good there is. If a life is asked once to serve something larger than itself, is that worth giving up romantic love?

The case for Jane's refusal. The trouble is precisely that rule. He won't let her come as a sister, because then she would keep part of herself back. He wants not only her work but all of her, including her obedience on the matter of not loving him. And how will a man who can time his own love with a watch treat a wife who doesn't fully submit?

The author has Jane refuse, yet gives St John the last lines of the book without a trace of irony. She respects such men; she just won't become part of one. Bring it home: a hugely meaningful career that asks for you around the clock; a partner who admires your competence and never asks whether you're happy. Being needed and being loved look alike. The difference is whether the other person can let you keep a part of yourself that isn't theirs.

Three · Is locking a violent, insane wife in an attic for ten years cruelty, or the best care her era could offer?

The case for Rochester. Everyone then knew what asylums were like: chains, cold water, paying visitors. He says he could have sent her to damp Ferndean and let her die there, and he didn't. He kept her at home with a fire and a paid attendant, at real risk to himself, and on the night of the fire he climbed onto the roof to save her. In a world that couldn't treat her and a law that gave him no way out, keeping her at home may really have been the least bad option.

The case for Bertha. We have one version of her, from the man who locked her up and meant to remarry behind her back. Why is she mad? Heredity — or is this simply what becomes of someone carried off to a cold, rainy country and shut in a windowless room for a decade? He calls her intemperate and violent, and admits he hardly spoke to her before the wedding. Hiding her was care, but it was also how he kept his own life open for loving someone else, up to leading Jane to the altar. The difference between care and confinement often comes down to whether the confined person ever gets to speak.

The author sides with Rochester; on the page Bertha is barely more than a laugh and a mass of hair. Bring it home: a relative with dementia or serious mental illness — do you move them into care? Do you tell a new partner, a new employer? How much of what we decide for someone who can't speak for herself is for her, and how much is so we can go on living? Usually both are true. Honesty means not pretending it's only the first.

Motifs: what a clergyman's daughter kept writing

Charlotte published only three novels in her lifetime (The Professor appeared after her death), and they are almost all about the same person: a woman with no money and no beauty, living by her wits and her work, with a fire inside her too big for the small place the world allows her. She had been a governess herself, had taught at a boarding school in Brussels, and had fallen hard for a married teacher there, Constantin Héger, to whom she wrote letters he did not answer. That experience became the colder, lonelier Lucy Snowe of Villette (1853), a novel that withholds any "Reader, I married him" and ends instead with a storm at sea, leaving us to guess whether the man she loves comes home.

The second obsession is passion against restraint: Rochester is fire, St John is ice, and Jane wants to burn without being burned to ash. The third is hunger, literal and otherwise. Lowood's burnt porridge, the cold porridge on the moor, the hunger for love and recognition are one substance in her books. Reading Villette, Matthew Arnold complained that its author's mind held nothing but hunger, rebellion and rage. He meant it as criticism; it is also an exact description of her subject.

Controversy and criticism

The moral panic. An anonymous review in the Quarterly Review in 1848 (by Elizabeth Rigby) called the book a pre-eminently anti-Christian composition and said its tone of mind was the same one that had overthrown authority abroad and fostered Chartism at home (paraphrased). Read today, that is a measure of its force: in 1847 a poor girl saying "we are equal" was a dangerous sentence.

Woolf's regret. In A Room of One's Own (1929) Virginia Woolf quotes the third-floor passage about women feeling just as men feel, admires it, and regrets that it is jarringly broken off by Grace Poole's laugh. Brontë, she judged, wrote in a rage where she should have written calmly. The anger is what gives the book its force, and also what makes it lurch.

The madwoman in the attic. Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar's The Madwoman in the Attic (1979), which takes its title from this novel, reads Bertha as the embodiment of Jane's suppressed rage. Jean Rhys, born in Dominica, retold Bertha's girlhood in the Caribbean and the marriage itself from her side in Wide Sargasso Sea (1966). Gayatri Spivak's 1985 essay argued that the rise of Jane as a female individualist is achieved over the destruction of a colonial woman, who is written as barely human and in racialised terms. Rochester's thirty thousand pounds came out of the Jamaican plantation economy, and the book says nothing about slave labour.

A convenient ending? The inconvenient wife dies, the man is maimed, the woman gets money: equality costs one death and one mutilation. Some critics read Rochester's injuries as a symbolic punishment. Others answer that, in that era, it was one of the few ways to bring the two of them level. Both readings hold.

Crossovers

  • Philosophy · Jon Elster, Ulysses and the Sirens: Elster's 1979 book studies precommitment: a rational agent who foresees her own weakness under temptation binds herself in advance, as Ulysses had his crew lash him to the mast. Jane's flight before dawn has exactly this shape; she knows one more day would make her say yes. The difference: Ulysses binds himself so he can hear the song and survive. Jane won't let herself hear it at all, and the price is being cast out onto the moor.
  • Sociology · Goffman's "total institution": Asylums (1961) describes how schools, barracks and hospitals strip away identity through uniform dress, cropped hair and public humiliation. Lowood's shorn curls and the stool, and the room on the third floor, share that shape. The difference: at Lowood Jane also finds Miss Temple and Helen.
  • Buddhism · the Middle Way: in the tradition, the Buddha's first sermon names sensual indulgence and self-mortification as the two extremes to avoid. Jane refuses Rochester's indulgence and St John's self-mortification alike. The difference: her goal is not the end of desire but love with desire intact.
  • Philosophy · Kant on duty: the Groundwork (1785) holds that an act's moral worth shows most clearly when it is done from duty against inclination. Jane says principles exist for the moment when body and soul are in revolt, which is the same shape. The difference: her law comes from God, Kant's from reason legislating for itself.

Ten lines to take away

① A ten-year-old hits back once and is locked in a room where a man died.

② Her best friend taught her to endure; she never learned, but she kept the smile all her life.

③ She dared not ask for liberty, only "a new servitude" — the humblest ambition of a poor girl.

④ She sentenced herself with "poor and plain," then used the same words to declare equality.

⑤ The laugh upstairs always came when she was at her most restless.

⑥ A letter written to keep her independence wrecked her wedding and later gave her a family.

⑦ The less anyone cared about her, the more she cared for herself.

⑧ One man who loved her wanted to dress her up; one who didn't wanted to use her. She took neither.

⑨ The lightning-split tree didn't die; it just had to grow alongside something else.

⑩ "Reader, I married him" — and the subject of the sentence is her.
