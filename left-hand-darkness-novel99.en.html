The Left Hand of Darkness

The Left Hand of Darkness · Ursula K. Le Guin · 1969 · Twenty chapters

中文 →

There are no shadows on the ice. Sky and snow are the same grey-white, and the wind outside the tent has been blowing for three days.
Inside, a small stove hums. Two people huddle against the cold: a man from Earth, dozens of light-years from home, and the former prime minister of this frozen planet, convicted of treason by his own country.
The Earthman says nothing aloud. He only speaks the other's name inside his head.
The other sits bolt upright, his face drained. After a long time he says: you spoke in my brother's voice. My brother is dead.

This piece contains complete spoilers: who carries whom out of a labor camp, what happens inside that tent on the ice, who dies at the border fence, whether the starship ever lands, and what the Earthman sees when he finally looks back at his own kind. The weight of this book lies in how a friendship grows, inch by inch, and how an era takes it away. Without telling all of it, there is no way to argue about whether that man was really a traitor.

In one sentence

A man from Earth is sent as an envoy to an icebound planet whose people have no fixed sex; he cannot read them, and he does not trust the one person who is willing to help him, until the two of them are forced to haul a sledge across a glacier together. This is not a thesis novel that imagines a genderless society. It is the story of a man who needs an entire book to learn to see another person as that person actually is, and who learns it almost too late.

Coordinates

Ursula K. Le Guin (1929–2018) was an American writer, the daughter of the anthropologist Alfred Kroeber and of Theodora Kroeber, who wrote the book about Ishi, the last of the Yahi people of California. The year before, Le Guin had published the fantasy A Wizard of Earthsea. The Left Hand of Darkness (1969) belongs to her Hainish Cycle, a future history in which the ancient Hainish seeded humanity across many planets, then lost touch; now a loose league called the Ekumen is gathering the scattered branches back together.

It won both the Hugo and the Nebula, and it is routinely named as a founding work of feminist science fiction. Not because it writes about women, but because it removes the categories "man" and "woman" altogether and looks at what is left.

The story

Act one · Erhenrang: a red keystone and a dinner misheard

The book opens by telling you it is a report. Its writer says he will make it like a story, because he was taught as a child that "Truth is a matter of the imagination" (opening of chapter one). He is Genly Ai, a Terran, the Ekumen's First Mobile, its lone envoy to this world. The natives call it Gethen; the Ekumen calls it Winter, because it is deep in an ice age and even its summers bite.

Genly came alone. His ship waits in orbit at the edge of the system, his colleagues asleep in stasis. He has lived in the kingdom of Karhide for a long time now, learned the language, met a great many people, and still has not got the king to say yes: join the Ekumen, trade and talk with dozens of worlds.

The story begins with a parade. In the capital, Erhenrang, a new arch is being closed, and King Argaven XV sets the final keystone with his own hands. By ancient custom the mortar for a keystone was mixed with blood and ground bone; now it is merely dyed red. By Gethenian standards the day is hot. Genly stands in the crowd and watches the prime minister, Estraven, sweating inside heavy ceremonial robes.

That evening Estraven invites Genly to dinner, and the book's first crucial misunderstanding takes place. Estraven tells him that he is no longer speaking for Genly's cause before the king. Genly is baffled, and on guard. Nobody here says anything straight. Everything is wrapped in shifgrethor, the Gethenian code of prestige and face, under which you may not give someone direct advice or contradict them openly without insulting their standing. Genly feels he has been fobbed off. Setup ① In his report he writes that Estraven's manner at the table was womanly: all charm and tact, no substance. He dislikes this "womanliness," and so he does not trust the man.

Here we need to know what the Gethenians are. They are ambisexual. For most of each month they are in somer, a latent state with no sexual drive and no expressed sex. Only for a few days at the end of each cycle do they enter kemmer, a kind of estrus. When two people in kemmer meet, their hormones act on each other: one becomes male, the other female, and neither knows in advance which way they will go. The same person may be a mother one time and a father the next. When kemmer passes, everything returns to neutral. To Gethenians, someone like Genly, permanently in kemmer and permanently one sex, is a pervert.

The next day Estraven is proclaimed a traitor. He has argued for giving ground in a long-running border dispute with the neighbouring nation of Orgoreyn. He has three days to leave Karhide or be killed on sight. His replacement is the king's cousin Tibe, a man whose smile always shows his teeth, who stirs up fear of Orgoreyn in radio speeches. Only now does Genly half-understand that last night's remark was not a brush-off but a warning. What he still cannot work out is why Estraven would take that risk for him.

Right after this act, the book inserts a folk tale from northern Karhide. Setup ② Two brothers vow to be lifelong kemmer partners, which local custom permits only until a child is born. Their lord orders them apart; the elder kills himself in despair; the younger is driven out, branded as the cause of his brother's death. Deep inside a blizzard he meets his dead brother, who takes his hand and asks him to stay. He tears himself free and comes back alive, with one hand ruined by frost. On first reading it seems a bit of local colour.

Genly is granted an audience with the king. Argaven is suspicious and highly strung, and certain that an alien must have a scheme behind him: why send just one of you? Setup ③ Genly's answer is the Ekumen's whole method. One alien is a curiosity; two would be an invasion. Alone, he cannot change this world, but this world can change him; alone, he must listen as well as speak (chapter three, paraphrased). The king gives no answer and leaves him in the cold.

Act two · Otherhord: one question, one "yes"

Sidelined, Genly travels to Otherhord Fastness in the eastern hills, home to adepts of the Handdara, Karhide's old religion. It has no god and no dogma, and what it prizes most is not knowing. The Fastness keeps a group of Foretellers: pay their price and ask a question, and they will answer it, and the answer is said never to be wrong.

Genly pays, and asks: will Gethen be a member of the Ekumen within five years?

The Foretellers sit in a circle in the dark: celibates, a madman, one person deliberately brought into kemmer, and Genly, the pervert. The Weaver who leads them is called Faxe. The ritual knots every nerve in the room into a single strand, and Genly is nearly swept into a shared hallucination. At the end, Setup ④ Faxe gives the answer: yes.

Afterwards Faxe explains that the Handdara keep Foretellers precisely to show how useless it is to know the answer. Ask the wrong question and the answer means nothing; what makes life possible is never knowing what comes next (chapter five, paraphrased). Genly walks away holding his "yes," with no idea what it will cost.

Act three · Mishnory: an efficient city, a sealed truck

Meanwhile the exiled Estraven crosses into Orgoreyn and finds shelter with an old acquaintance, the Commensal Obsle. Orgoreyn is Karhide's opposite. Karhide is a half-feudal kingdom of semi-independent domains. Orgoreyn is made up of thirty-three administrative districts, with registrations, papers, rations and committees everywhere, and a secret police called the Sarf.

Genly decides Karhide is hopeless and crosses the border himself. He is held for lacking papers, but is soon sent on to the capital, Mishnory, and welcomed warmly by the Commensals. He stays in the luxurious house of the Commensal Shusgis and goes to one banquet after another. In Orgoreyn people say what they mean. There is no shifgrethor. Genly finds it a relief.

Estraven tracks him down and warns him once more, as bluntly as anyone could. You are a chip in a factional game; people in the Sarf want you gone; call your ship down now and let the fact of it protect you. Genly refuses. His reason is principled: the Ekumen wants a world to join freely, not to be frightened in by a ship falling out of the sky. And he still does not trust the man. Why would a prime minister who has just been thrown out of his own country care so much about me?

A few nights later the Sarf take Genly from Shusgis's house. He is drugged and interrogated, then crammed with other prisoners into a sealed truck. It drives north for days in winter, unheated; the prisoners stay alive by pressing together for warmth, and not all of them make it. The destination is Pulefen, a "Voluntary Farm" in the far north: a labor camp. Orgoreyn announces to the outside world that the alien envoy has died of illness.

At Pulefen the prisoners are dosed every day with drugs that suppress kemmer. They do hard labor on thin soup. Genly is also given interrogation drugs, and grows weaker by the day. He is dying.

Act four · The Gobrin Ice: a sledge, a tent, a name

One night, someone lifts him out of his bunk.

It is Estraven. He has followed Genly north, got himself hired at the camp as a guard with forged papers, and on his night watch he loads the unconscious Genly onto a sledge and runs him into the forest in a state called dothe. Dothe is a trance Gethenians can enter at will, a surge of strength and endurance far beyond the normal, paid for afterwards by thangen, a long, collapsing sleep of exhaustion.

Only one route is left to them. The overland road west to Karhide will be watched. To the northeast lies the Gobrin Ice, a permanent ice sheet; nobody will follow them across it, because nobody believes it can be crossed alive. Estraven has done the sums: there is just enough food, provided everything goes right.

They drag the sledge up onto the glacier between two volcanoes, one of them erupting, its ash falling on the snow. Out on the ice they meet unshadow: cloud diffuses the light so evenly that the ground casts no shadow at all, and the eye can no longer tell flat surface from crevasse. Setup ⑤ Estraven says it is the most dangerous weather the ice has. Sure enough, Genly steps into nothing and drops into a crevasse, hanging by the rope tied to the sledge until Estraven hauls him out.

Here the book begins to alternate between two voices, Genly's report and Estraven's travel journal, and for the first time we see Estraven from the inside. He has believed in Genly all along. He believes the Ekumen could carry Gethen past the level of the nation, so that the standoff between Karhide and Orgoreyn need not teach the planet real war. Gethen has never had a war, only raids and feuds. His fall, his exile, the loss of home and name, are bound up with his having spoken for Genly at court. What he said at that dinner, he meant literally. It was Genly who could not hear a plain truth without the wrapping of face.

In the tent, at last, they begin to speak plainly. Estraven tells Genly his given name: Therem. And then Estraven goes into kemmer.

In a tent that holds two people, there is no avoiding it. Estraven's female side shows itself to Genly, and Genly feels it. They talk, and decide not to touch each other. Not because they don't want to, but because both understand it would turn something newly built and equal into something else. Payoff ① ↑ And at that moment, Genly writes, he finally stops being afraid, and stops forcing the other into the box of man or woman: he sees Estraven as both a man and a woman, and sees that this person is simply this person. The sneer about "womanliness" at the start is here taken back by the man who made it. What he refused to trust was exactly the half he refused to respect; he could not read this person because he knew only one way of looking.

Genly offers to teach Estraven mindspeech, a form of telepathy the Ekumen's people have learned, in which you can only tell the truth and cannot lie. Estraven learns fast. The first time he "hears," he is shaken: Payoff ② ↑ Genly's voice inside his head is the voice of his dead brother, Arek. Only now do we understand why that folk tale sat where it did. Estraven and his brother Arek had once vowed kemmer together; they had a son; then Arek died. The dead brother in the heart of the blizzard, holding on to the living one, was Estraven's own past; and in the same white waste, an alien speaks to him in the dead man's voice.

One night Estraven recites an old Handdara poem, and the book takes its title from it: "Light is the left hand of darkness / and darkness the right hand of light" (the ice chapters, Tormer's Lay). Payoff ⑤ ↑ Later Genly draws him an old figure from Earth in his notebook: a circle, half dark and half light, each half holding a dot of the other. That is you, Therem, he says: two and one, a shadow on snow. The unshadow nearly killed Genly. A world of light without shadow leaves you blind. To see the ground you need shadow; to see a person, you have to see both halves at once.

As the food runs out, they come down off the ice and see the coast of Karhide. Genly later reflects that it is good to have an end to travel toward, but that "it is the journey that matters, in the end" (the ice chapters).

Act five · The border: a fence, a ship, a child's question

Back in Karhide, Genly manages to get a signal up to the ship in orbit through a transmitter. In the village where they are hiding, an old acquaintance Estraven has turned to betrays him. The hunters come. Estraven judges that if he is taken he will become a bargaining chip in Tibe's hands and will compromise Genly's mission. At night, on skis, he goes straight for the border fence into Orgoreyn, knowing full well there are guards there. The guards fire.

When Genly reaches him he is still alive, but only for a little while. By Genly's account, the last thing he says is Arek's name.

What follows goes almost too smoothly. Genly's reappearance, alive, in Karhide exposes Orgoreyn's claim that the envoy had died, and the faction behind it falls. Tibe is out. King Argaven, eager to get ahead of Orgoreyn, invites the Ekumen's ship to land in Karhide. Payoff ④ ↑ The "yes" that Faxe spoke at Otherhord comes true: Gethen will join the Ekumen within five years. The prophecy was right. Nobody told the man who asked that the price of that yes was a life. And that was Faxe's point all along: knowing the answer does not tell you what it means.

Genly asks the king to revoke Estraven's exile. But Estraven died a traitor under Karhide's law, and Genly, before the ship came down, did not insist on clearing his friend's name first. He feels it as a betrayal of Estraven.

The ship lands. The hatch opens; his colleagues step out. Payoff ③ ↑ Genly looks at them and finds them deeply strange: voices too deep or too shrill, like a troupe of large unfamiliar animals of two different species, every one of them in kemmer. In chapter three he told the king that, alone, he could be changed by this world. Here it comes true in a way he never foresaw. After an entire book, he finally looks at Earth's people through Gethenian eyes. The "pervert" is now his own kind.

Last of all, Genly goes to Estraven's home, Estre, and meets his father and Sorve, the son Estraven had with Arek. The boy asks him: will you tell us how he died? Will you tell us about the other worlds, the other kinds of people out among the stars? The book stops on that question.

The fuse

Pull the causes into a single line and the real fuse of this book is not the Ekumen's arrival, nor Tibe's scheming. It is that dinner in act one, where one man told the truth and the other did not hear it, because he looked down on "womanliness." Had Genly understood that night, he would not have gambled on Orgoreyn, would not have ended up in Pulefen, and Estraven would not have had to stake himself to save him. Nor, of course, would the friendship on the ice ever have happened. The whole book is the aftershock of one sentence that went unheard. Learning to hear another person cost twelve hundred kilometres of ice and a life.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow-motion one · The keystone and the mortar

Look again at the opening parade. Le Guin does not hand you an explanatory paragraph about Gethenian biology. She gives you a hot day, a procession, a keystone, a bowl of red mortar, all seen through an outsider's fatigue and discomfort: the clothes are too thick, the speeches too long, the king's gestures too odd. Information is held back on purpose: the reader, like Genly, does not know what they are looking at, only that something is off.

The keystone deserves a second look too. Ancient custom built arches with blood; now only a touch of red dye is left. A society that thinks it has left blood sacrifice behind ends the story having paid for union with one man's blood. The book never draws that line itself; it is one readers can draw. But it is no accident that Le Guin puts a stone that locks the two halves of an arch together on the very first page.

Slow-motion two · The circle in the dark

Throughout the Foretelling, the point of view is locked on Genly, the only outsider in the room and its only "pervert." His mere presence unsettles the circle: a being permanently in kemmer has been dropped into a group of carefully balanced Gethenians. The rhythm is a long compression and a sudden release: silence, darkness, breathing, Genly's senses swelling until he sees Faxe as if filled with light, and then a single word. Yes.

The word lands so hard because it is the only certain answer in the whole book, delivered by a house devoted to teaching people not to want certain answers. Le Guin has a religion opposed to prophecy run a prophecy shop, makes the prophecy come true, and makes the manner of its coming true offer no comfort at all. It is a scene that splits knowing from understanding.

Slow-motion three · At the fence

Estraven's death is written very short and very cold. No long farewell, no deathbed speech. He skis at the fence, there are shots, Genly runs to him, he lies in the snow and says a name.

It is short because the ice was so long. We have travelled with them day by day, counting rations meal by meal, until we can almost hear the stove. Then Le Guin takes it all away in a few lines. She puts the greatest emotion into the fewest words and leaves the verdict with us: was he killed, or did he kill himself? The book gives you enough to believe he went at it knowingly. The guards were right there, and he skied too straight. A man called a traitor all his life refused, this way, to be taken back and used as a pawn.

The idea, worked through: a planet without fixed sex

The book's most famous device is chapter seven, the field notes of Ong Tot Oppong, an Investigator from the Ekumen's early, covert landing party, which reason out the social consequences of Gethenian physiology. Walk the chain seriously:

① Premise: nobody is permanently a man or a woman, and anyone can become pregnant and nurse. ② Consequence: the burden of childbearing does not fall on one half of the population, so there is no "strong half" and "weak half"; a person is judged only as a person. ③ Sexual drive is gathered into a few days of kemmer each month; the rest of social life carries no sexual tension, no flirtation, no gendered dominance. ④ The notes go further and speculate that Gethen's lack of war may be linked to the absence of a continuous "masculine" aggression, and that the Gethenians may be the product of an ancient Hainish genetic experiment.

Which links hold? From ① to ② the logic is tight: if anyone can be tied down by pregnancy, a division of labor by sex has no foundation. ③ is plausible too. ④ is the weakest, and the novel itself takes it apart. Its Gethenians have feuds, raids, torture and labor camps, and Orgoreyn's centralized state is steering the whole planet toward its first real war. The absence of war was never biologically guaranteed; once the machinery of the nation-state grows, war follows. Ong Tot Oppong's notes are a "scientific report," and the novel proves the report incomplete.

It should be said honestly: this is a fictional premise, not biology. Nature does have animals that change sex (some fish switch from female to male depending on the makeup of the group), but nothing in mammals resembles a sex randomly assigned each cycle by a partner's hormones. Gethenian physiology is a stage built for a thought experiment. Le Guin herself said as much in her essay "Is Gender Necessary?" (1976): the book is a thought experiment that removes gender to see what remains.

Two other devices deserve a sentence each. The ansible is the Ekumen's instantaneous communicator: messages are not bound by the speed of light, so the league can confer across light-years. But ships carrying people can only approach light speed, so a round trip costs many years. A message arrives instantly; a person takes a lifetime to travel. That is why the Ekumen sends one envoy, and why he has to put down roots. Mindspeech, the form of communication in which you cannot lie, enters the book on the ice, exactly when two people have finally stopped speaking through face.

What it forces you to think about

One · Was Estraven a traitor?

You are a nation's prime minister. You believe an alliance from outside could spare this world a war that is otherwise coming, but your king does not believe it, and your rivals are rallying people with fear of the neighbours. You give ground on the border, speak up for an alien, and are convicted of treason. Do you regret it? If someone else did it, would you call them a traitor?

First, the strongest case that he was. A prime minister's duty is to his own country and its people, not to whatever he personally takes "humanity" to need. The people of Karhide never authorized him to give the border away, nor to stake the nation's future on an alien of uncertain origin. If every official may override the community he serves on the strength of his private idea of the greater good, the community is left with nothing; today's "vision" is tomorrow's excuse for every autocrat. And his eventual victory came by way of a corpse and a chain of political events he could not control. That is hardly a glorious road.

The other side: what he betrayed was a "loyalty" leading his country to disaster. Tibe's patriotism runs on hatred. On the journey Estraven says, in effect, that what he loves is Karhide's land, its towns and its people, and that hating another country is not the same as loving your own. If a nation is being led into a war it has never known, the person who refuses to follow is the one taking that nation seriously.

Where does the author stand? She has the law convict him, has him die at the border, and on the last page has his son ask about "the other worlds." His name is not cleared, but what he wanted arrives. Bring it home: in a company or a team, you know a decision will go badly, you say so, and you are called someone who "can't see the bigger picture." Whose picture?

Two · Should Genly have called the ship down sooner?

You are in a foreign country. Someone warns you that unless you use the power you hold, you will disappear. But your entire mission rests on one principle: the other side must consent freely. Press the button and you live; leave it, and what you believe in stays intact. Do you press it?

First, the strongest case for calling it. Principles serve outcomes. The Ekumen wants Gethen to join; if Genly dies in a labor camp, that goal slips by years. He nearly did die, and the man who saved him did die. A ship landing is not a conquest. It simply makes it impossible to go on pretending aliens don't exist. That is presenting a fact, not applying force. To refuse is to put your own moral fastidiousness ahead of other people's lives, and Estraven paid the bill for Genly's principles.

The other side: a league joined under pressure is a different thing from its first day. The Ekumen sends one unarmed envoy precisely so that the other side says yes from a position where it could always say no. Break that rule once to save yourself, and every later "we had no choice" gets easier. And in the end Gethen did choose yes, unthreatened. The principle won.

Where does the author stand? She lets the principle win, while making sure we see who pays for it. The one who held firm lived; the one who covered for him died. Bring it home: you hold to "no back doors" and "no favours." Is someone near you paying for your clean hands?

Three · If nobody were a man or a woman, would you want to live there?

Picture a society where nobody is expected, restricted or sized up because of their sex; anyone might bear a child, anyone might become prime minister; for most of the year nobody looks at you sexually at all. The price: you no longer have a stable sex either, and the person you love may have swapped places with you by next month. Would you choose it?

First, the strongest case for no. Gender is not only a cage. For many people it is a way of knowing themselves: their body, their desire, how they love and are loved. Many find belonging in it, not constraint. A world that turns all of that into a monthly lottery frees nobody; it only trades one certainty for another uncertainty. Besides, Gethen is no utopia. It has labor camps, secret police, blood feuds and a taboo on suicide; take sex out of human nature and the rest of it can still wreck things perfectly well.

The other side: most of the limits on you today simply would not exist there. Nobody would refuse to hire you because you might get pregnant; nobody would tell you not to cry because you are a man; you would be judged only by what you did. As for belonging: Gethenians have families, vows and love. They just don't need a permanent box to keep those things in.

Where does the author stand? She does not write Gethen as an ideal state, yet it is there that her narrator first learns to treat a person as a person. She doesn't want you to move there; she wants your own world to look strange when you come back. Bring it home: when did you last assume how someone would think because of their sex?

Motifs: what Le Guin kept writing about

Le Guin returned again and again to a few things. First, balance: light and dark, life and death, male and female are not opposed poles but halves that need each other. The Equilibrium of the Earthsea books (from 1968) is the same obsession in fantasy dress, where every spell a wizard casts moves the scales of the world. Second, the outsider and the colonized: her father was an anthropologist, and her fiction keeps sending in someone from outside who must learn to listen. The Word for World Is Forest (1972), in which Terran colonists clear an alien planet's forests and enslave its people, is the angriest face of this. Third, departure and return: the hero of The Dispossessed (1974) leaves an anarchist moon for its wealthy mother planet, and the book's refrain is, roughly, that the true journey is return.

The Left Hand of Darkness is the most personal link in that chain. The other books ask how a society should be organized; this one asks how two people can actually see each other. Late in life she translated the Tao Te Ching, and the Handdara's wisdom of not-knowing and the old poem of light and dark have echoes throughout her work.

Controversy and criticism

The pronoun "he." Every Gethenian is referred to as "he," so readers almost inevitably read them as men: king, prime minister, guard, Foreteller, all seem male. In the 1976 essay Le Guin defended the choice, arguing that English had no good neutral pronoun. When she revised the essay in 1987 she added extensive annotations conceding that the defence did not hold, and saying she would now prefer other solutions. When she collected her earlier story "Winter's King," she switched to feminine pronouns for its Gethenians.

Default heterosexuality. The two partners in kemmer always end up one male and one female; the book never shows two people in kemmer becoming the same sex. In the later annotations Le Guin admitted this needlessly locked the Gethenians into heterosexuality.

Too little of the "female side." Critics note that we almost never see Estraven or any Gethenian mothering, raising children or living a domestic life. Most of the book is politics, travel and ice, traditionally "men's" stories. In a book that removes gender, the protagonist's experience is still coded male.

Genly's misogyny. Remarks like "womanly" make some readers uncomfortable. Defenders say that is the design: the narrator's prejudice is what the book sets out to dismantle, and he is changed by the end. Critics answer that the irony is not always clear, and that the text sometimes seems to stand where Genly stands.

Pace. The first half's court and committee politics move slowly and come thick with names; many readers say the book only really took hold of them on the ice.

Crossovers

  • Anthropology · Margaret Mead, Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies: in 1935 Mead compared three New Guinea peoples and argued that "masculine" and "feminine" temperaments vary with culture. Le Guin runs the experiment in reverse: Mead holds biology fixed and varies culture; Gethen fixes a cultural situation and varies biology. Both try to pry nature from nurture, and the later disputes over Mead's fieldwork are a reminder of how far a thought experiment sits from the field.
  • Evolutionary biology · The bluehead wrasse: remove the male from a group of these reef fish and the largest female becomes male within days. Sex is triggered by social situation, the same shape as kemmer's "decided by the partner." Unlike it: the fish change once, one way; Gethenians start over every month.
  • Sociology · Goffman's "face-work": in 1955 Erving Goffman analysed how people jointly maintain each other's face in interaction. Shifgrethor reads like those rules pushed to the limit. Unlike it: Goffman's face is cooperative; under shifgrethor even a piece of advice can be an insult.
  • Psychology · Sandra Bem's psychological androgyny: Bem's 1974 inventory treated masculinity and femininity as independent dimensions, so a person could score high on both. Estraven nearly embodies it. Unlike it: Bem later criticized her own framework for still reproducing the two boxes, a turn strikingly like Le Guin's revision of her own essay.

Ten lines to take with you

① The greatest distance in this book is not light-years; it is one true sentence at a dinner that went unheard.

② Genly distrusted Estraven for being "womanly," and only later found that what he distrusted was the half he looked down on.

③ One alien is a curiosity, two an invasion; so only one is sent, and one can be changed.

④ The Foretellers said yes. They never said what the yes would cost.

⑤ Orgoreyn speaks plainly and efficiently, then sends you to a labor camp; Karhide never says what it means, and never hurts you.

⑥ "Light is the left hand of darkness": a snowfield without shadows drops you into a crevasse, and a person seen without both halves cannot be seen at all.

⑦ Mindspeech cannot lie, so its very first words carried a dead brother.

⑧ In the tent, two people chose not to touch each other, and it is the most intimate moment in the book.

⑨ He skied very straight at the fence, and his name was never cleared.

⑩ The ship lands, Genly looks at his own kind and finds them the monsters; and a child asks him: tell us about the other worlds.
