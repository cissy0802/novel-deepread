Novels, Deep-Read · Jin Yong · No.36

The Legend of the Condor Heroes

Condor Trilogy · Book One · Jin Yong (Louis Cha) · serialized 1957–1959

中文 →

An inn at Zhangjiakou, on the road north. A young man from the steppe, dark from the sun, awkward in the local speech, is eating alone. A filthy little beggar sits down at the next table — you cannot see a face under the dirt, only the eyes, which are very bright. The young man offers to buy him a meal. The beggar orders, and orders, each dish more expensive than the last, and the bill comes to nineteen taels and change — a year's living for an ordinary family. The young man pays without blinking.
The beggar says it is cold. The young man takes off his black sable coat and hands it over.
The beggar says he has no money for the road. The young man gives him the last four ingots of gold he owns.
On his way out the beggar looks at the rare red horse tied at the door — the one the young man broke himself, out on the grasslands. He unties it and hands over the reins.
He has no idea who this beggar is, and no intention of finding out.

This page contains complete spoilers — how Yang Kang dies and in which temple, who really kills Guo Jing's teachers, where the Nine Yin Manual ends up, who wins the second Contest of Mount Hua, and what Guo Jing says to the dying Genghis Khan on the last pages. The premise of this site is that everything worth discussing lives past the ending. If you want to keep the suspense, read the novel first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

In the last decades of the Song dynasty, a wandering Taoist names two unborn boys after the year of China's greatest national humiliation, so that neither will ever be allowed to forget it; a massacre then splits their families apart, and one boy grows up poor on the Mongolian steppe with a mother who tells him everything and seven crude, honest teachers, while the other grows up as a prince of the enemy court. What the two of them turn into over the next eighteen years is the whole of this book. And the thing that lights the fuse is not a war between empires — it is one woman who could not bear to let a stranger freeze to death in the snow.

Coordinates

Jin Yong (1924–2018), born Cha Leung-yung, was a newspaperman his entire working life. The Legend of the Condor Heroes is his third novel, serialized a column at a time between 1957 and 1959 in a Hong Kong daily. It is a newspaper serial before it is anything else: every instalment had to end with a reason to buy tomorrow's paper, which explains both its propulsive rhythm and most of its structural faults. In 1959 he founded his own paper, Ming Pao.

It is among the most widely read popular novels ever written in Chinese, and the opening volume of the Condor Trilogy (Condor Heroes → The Return of the Condor Heroes → The Heaven Sword and Dragon Sabre). Jin Yong twice revised his complete works — once in the 1970s, again in 2003 — so two people discussing this novel are not necessarily discussing the same book. English readers had to wait until Anna Holmwood's translation A Hero Born in 2018 for a complete trade edition.

The story

Act One · Niu Family Village: two names and a wager

Outside the Song capital, two households live side by side. Guo Xiaotian and Yang Tiexin are sworn brothers, descendants respectively of a bandit-hero from Water Margin and of the Yang family generals — Jin Yong plugs his novel into the two most popular story-lineages in Chinese folk memory on page one. Both men train with weapons, both are poor, both hate the Jin empire that has held the northern half of the country for eighty years while the emperor sits in the south listening to music.

A Taoist arrives out of the snow: Qiu Chuji, of the Quanzhen sect, carrying the head of an official he has just killed for collaborating. He drinks with them, learns that both wives are pregnant, and names the unborn children on the spot — Guo's son will be Guo Jing, Yang's will be Yang Kang. Together the two syllables name the year 1127, when Jin armies broke the capital, carried off two emperors, and ended the Northern Song. Let the names remind these boys every day of their lives.seed ① He also leaves two short daggers, each engraved with one of the names.seed ②

Yang's wife, Bao Xiruo, is the sort of person who carries an injured sparrow home. One day she finds a badly wounded young man in the snow, hides him, feeds him medicine, waits for him to heal.seed ③ He is Wanyan Honglie, sixth prince of the Jin. He recovers, leaves, and then does something very patient: he pulls strings back home so that a Song officer will lead soldiers against these two households as traitors.

Guo Xiaotian is killed on the spot; Yang Tiexin vanishes, presumed dead. The pregnant Li Ping is dragged off by the officer; Bao Xiruo is "rescued" by Wanyan Honglie and taken north. One woman's soft heart costs two families everything.

Meanwhile Qiu Chuji brawls in a Jiaxing wine-house with a band of local fighters — the Seven Freaks of Jiangnan, seven schoolless, foul-tempered street masters led by the blind Ke Zhen'e, among them a thieving scholar, a horse-breaker, a shopkeeper, and one woman. When the misunderstanding clears they make a bet out of pure stubbornness: each side will find one of the boys, teach him for eighteen years, and bring him back to this same wine-house on the same spring day to fight — to settle whose teaching is better. Qiu Chuji gets lucky; Yang Kang is right there in the Jin palace. The Seven Freaks search for six years before they find Li Ping and her son on the Mongolian grasslands.

Act Two · The steppe: a boy who cannot learn anything

Guo Jing grows up under Mongol skies. Nothing about him is quick: not his speech, not his reflexes, and least of all his training. Seven teachers take turns and despair — he cannot manage a clever feint, cannot memorize a sword form, and Ke Zhen'e calls him a block of wood. Jin Yong makes a genuinely counterintuitive bet here: for two hundred pages his hero is good at nothing.

Two things change that. One is Ma Yu, a Quanzhen master who climbs up at night to teach the boy in secret — not a single move, only how to breathe, how to sleep on a cliff face, how to bank the energy inside him. Two years later Guo Jing suddenly "opens up," and everything the Freaks taught him abruptly has something underneath it holding it up. This is the book's theory of martial arts arriving for the first time: technique is outside, inner strength is inside, and without the inside the outside is decoration.

The other is darker. Hiding on the steppe are a husband and wife known as the Twice Foul Dark Wind — Chen Xuanfeng and Mei Chaofeng, former disciples of Huang Yaoshi of Peach Blossom Island, who stole half of the Nine Yin Manual and eloped with it, and who now kill by driving fingers through a human skull. The Freaks collide with them; one of the seven dies, and Mei Chaofeng is blinded in the fight. Years later, cornered one night by Chen Xuanfeng, the ten-year-old Guo Jing shoves the little engraved dagger into the man's navel — the one spot his training left soft.payoff ② ↑ Qiu Chuji left that blade as a memento of a national humiliation; it turns out to be the entire arsenal with which a boy too dim to memorize a sword form kills a first-rank master. The stab is the whole novel in miniature: Guo Jing never wins by being smart.

Other things happen out there. He becomes a favourite in the camp of Temujin — soon to be Genghis Khan — swears brotherhood with the Khan's fourth son, learns archery from the great marksman Jebe, and on one hunt brings down two eagles with a single arrow. The Khan betroths his daughter Huazheng to him and gives him rank. At this point in his life Guo Jing is a Mongol. That fact does not come due until the final page.

Act Three · Zhangjiakou: a meal worth a year's wages

The eighteen years are up; Guo Jing rides south. At Zhangjiakou he meets the little beggar, buys the meal, and gives away the coat, the gold and the red horse. Afterwards the beggar washes and changes and reappears as a girl with a very good face, who gives her name as Huang Rong.

It is worth being clear about what this scene is doing. Huang Rong is not begging. She is setting an exam. She is the only daughter of Huang Yaoshi, master of Peach Blossom Island; she has been flattered and manipulated her whole life, and she knows better than anyone that how a person treats a useless filthy child is how that person actually is. Guo Jing does not notice he is being tested. He does not notice there is such a thing as a test. That failure to notice is the reason for everything he gets afterwards.

Act Four · The northern capital: the young prince, and the Iron Spear Temple

What has Yang Kang become? The son of a Jin prince: silk, tutors, the best instructors money can find, quick at everything, and a genuinely capable fighter — Qiu Chuji did teach him well. On the street he happens on a contest where a girl, Mu Nianci, is fighting to choose a husband. He beats her, takes one of her shoes as a joke, and refuses to marry her. He is a prince; one does not marry a street performer.

The old man running the booth is Yang Tiexin, who did not die eighteen years ago. Inside the palace he recognizes Bao Xiruo — her garden has been laid out to match the village she came from, her rooms furnished with the old farm tools, nothing altered. Wanyan Honglie spent eighteen years rebuilding, in exact detail, a home she can never go back to.

Husband and wife recognize each other and try to leave. The prince's men close in. Yang Tiexin kills himself; Bao Xiruo falls across his body and follows him.payoff ③ ↑ The moment she knelt over a stranger in the snow, she was rescuing her own destruction — and Jin Yong kills her at the exact spot where the softness first went off. She has no capacity to hate Wanyan Honglie and no capacity not to follow Yang Tiexin; both are true, so she can only die. Her tenderness is the whole of what makes her lovable and the whole of what makes her lethal. All this happens at the Iron Spear Temple outside her old village, a ruin dedicated to a long-dead general famous for his iron spear.seed ④

Yang Kang stands there, watches both of his blood parents die, and goes back to being a prince. His reasoning is not stupid: the man who raised him for eighteen years loves him, really loves him, and he is being asked to give that up in an afternoon for a dead pauper he never met and a conquered people he has never belonged to. It is the hardest question in the book, and Jin Yong does not answer it for him — he simply lets him keep walking.

Act Five · Hong Qigong and the Dragon-Subduing Palms

Travelling south, Guo Jing and Huang Rong have their dinner stolen at an inn by an old beggar missing an index finger: Hong Qigong, chief of the Beggars' Sect, the Northern Beggar, one of the five greatest fighters alive. (He cut the finger off himself, to cure his greed for food. It did not work.) Huang Rong takes hold of that weakness and cooks for him, dish after impossible dish, until his conscience forces him to pay for his meals in martial arts. He teaches her a footwork art and teaches Guo Jing the Eighteen Dragon-Subduing Palms — a set with almost no moves in it, nothing clever, everything carried by raw inner force. The fit between the man and the art is the book's most elegant piece of design: Huang Rong could never learn it, because her nature bends; Guo Jing can, because he does not.

They also collide with Ouyang Ke, nephew of the Western Venom, Ouyang Feng — a dandy travelling with a retinue of women in white and a basket of snakes, and unwilling to leave Huang Rong alone. That thread will kill people later.

Act Six · Peach Blossom Island: the old man in the cave

Huang Rong takes Guo Jing home to meet her father. Huang Yaoshi, the Eastern Heretic, is a man who has converted all of his grief into contempt for the world. His wife once memorized the stolen half of the Nine Yin Manual in a single reading and wrote it back out for him from memory; the effort killed her, and she died having just given birth to Huang Rong. Afterwards he crippled every one of his disciples and drove them off the island. His "heresy" is a posture built over a loss he has nowhere to put. What that grief produced for his daughter is a garment called the soft hedgehog armour — silk on the inside, a dense field of barbs on the outside, injuring anyone who touches her. He makes her wear it against her skin at all times.seed ⑥

In a cave on the island sits an old man who has been imprisoned there for fifteen years: Zhou Botong, the Old Urchin, junior brother of the founder of the Quanzhen sect. Out of him spills the prehistory of the whole book. Twenty-five years ago the five strongest people alive met on Mount Hua to fight over that same manual — Huang Yaoshi, Ouyang Feng, the Southern Emperor Duan Zhixing, Hong Qigong, and Wang Chongyang of Quanzhen. Wang Chongyang won, took the manual, and sealed it so that nobody could practise it. Because the book is itself a blood debt: it was written by a Song civil servant assigned to print the Taoist canon, who taught himself to fight out of the scriptures, killed people, and had his entire family wiped out in revenge. He fled into the mountains for forty years and worked out, move by move, the counter to every one of his enemies' arts — and came down to find that all of them had died of old age. Understanding that the forty years had been spent on nothing, he wrote the manual.

Zhou Botong finds Huang Rong too sharp for his taste and will only play with the slow boy. He teaches Guo Jing to fight with both hands independently — left and right running separate attacks, one man doing the work of two. The art has an entrance requirement: anyone with a complicated mind cannot learn it, because you cannot genuinely split yourself into two people who are not thinking about each other. Huang Rong tries and fails. Guo Jing gets it immediately. Then Zhou Botong does something worse: sworn never to practise the Nine Yin Manual himself, he recites the whole thing aloud and bullies the boy into memorizing it, calling it nonsense syllables and forbidding him to ask what any of it means. Guo Jing has a poor memory and enormous patience. He learns every word and understands none of them.seed ⑤

Act Seven · The wager settled, and a week behind a wall

The eighteen years come due at the wine-house in Jiaxing, and the two young men finally meet. How does the bet end? Qiu Chuji concedes.payoff ① ↑ But the sting is that the result has almost nothing to do with teaching. Qiu Chuji produced a first-rate fighter; the Seven Freaks produced a mediocre one. What separated the two boys was the snowy night Li Ping told her son about again and again, and the snowy night Bao Xiruo could never bring herself to describe. The names were a wager that memory could be installed; the answer eighteen years later is that whether you remember has nothing to do with your name and everything to do with whether anyone was willing to finish the sentence. Neither the man who named them nor the men who trained them decided anything. The two mothers did.

What follows is the densest stretch of the novel, one disaster shoving the next:

  • Five of the Seven Freaks are killed on Peach Blossom Island — the hands are Ouyang Feng's and Yang Kang's, but the scene is arranged to look like Huang Yaoshi's work. Blind Ke Zhen'e survives and is certain of the killer's identity. The killer, as far as he knows, is Huang Rong's father. A crack opens between Guo Jing and Huang Rong that neither can close.
  • Huang Rong is crippled by a blow from Qiu Qianren, chief of the Iron Palm sect. Guo Jing carries her across the country to the retired Southern Emperor, now the monk Yideng, who heals her at the cost of years of his own power — willingly, because of an old debt: Zhou Botong once had an affair with his consort Ying Gu, and when her injured child was carried to him he refused, out of jealousy, to save it. The child died. He became a monk to spend forty years paying for one refusal.
  • Under the Iron Palm peak the pair find a treatise on war left behind by the Song general Yue Fei. It will become a very awkward gift by the end of the book.
  • Guo Jing, badly wounded, hides with Huang Rong for seven days in a sealed room in an abandoned house. Half the novel's hidden plot arrives through that wall, spoken by people who believe they are alone.

Act Eight · The Iron Spear Temple: a protective garment kills a man

Yang Kang keeps stepping down. He steals the Beggars' Sect's staff of office and tries to seize the leadership; it fails. To be taken on as Ouyang Feng's disciple, he finishes off Ouyang Ke where he lies pinned under rocks — reasoning, correctly, that a man who has just lost his heir will want a replacement. He is never cornered into any of it. Every time, he calculates, and he calculates well.

The reckoning comes at the Iron Spear Temple.payoff ④ ↑ Yang Kang, thinking a blind man harmless, boasts to Ke Zhen'e and narrates the killings on Peach Blossom Island in his own words. In the scuffle he strikes Huang Rong and the barbs of the soft hedgehog armour open his palm — and the barbs are carrying venom from Ouyang Feng's snakes.payoff ⑥ ↑ Huang Yaoshi made that garment so that nothing could reach his daughter. In the end it stops no blow at all; it simply poisons the man who throws one.

Two lines fall due in the same night: he dies in the temple where his father killed himself, and the thing that kills him is a father's attempt to protect a daughter. The heaviest verdict the book passes on Yang Kang is not that he was wicked, but that he never belonged anywhere — the blood father's temple, the adoptive father's ambition, the girl who loved him, and the master he chose for himself all become worthless within a few hours. Mu Nianci sits with the body, carrying his child.

Act Nine · Mount Hua, the steppe, and a king's last question

Ouyang Feng forces Guo Jing to write out the Nine Yin Manual. Huang Rong tells him to comply — and doctors it as he writes: order scrambled, forgeries inserted. Ouyang Feng takes the corrupted text away, practises it in reverse, and becomes unbeatable and insane in the same motion.payoff ⑤ ↑ Zhou Botong's meaningless syllables blossom twice here: once as the inner power that finally lifts Guo Jing into the first rank, and once as the quiet destruction of the manual's authority as a text. The thing five masters fought over for twenty-five years turns out to be void the moment one person is willing to lie.

At the second Contest of Mount Hua the winner is, absurdly, the madman — walking on his hands, every technique inverted, unanswerable. As the others concede, Huang Rong asks him one question: who are you? Ouyang Feng stops. He cannot remember. He runs off down the mountain with his head in his hands, shouting the question at nobody. The title of greatest fighter alive ends up with a man who does not know his own name.

The northern account comes due as well. Genghis Khan intends to march south against the Song and wants Guo Jing to lead — he has Yue Fei's treatise, he understands siegecraft, he distinguished himself in the western campaign and saw for himself what a stormed city looks like afterwards. To force his hand, the Mongols plan to use his mother. Li Ping kills herself in front of her son, spending her life to take the choice away from him.

The last scene is on the grass. The dying Khan calls Guo Jing to his horse and asks: I have taken more territory than any man in history — am I a hero? Guo Jing does not soften it. He says: you killed too many people; the wider your empire, the thicker the bones beneath it; the man who deserves the word is the one who leaves ordinary people better off. The old Khan has no answer. He mutters a question about whether his whole life was a mistake, and dies that day. Guo Jing and Huang Rong turn their horses south.

Laid end to end, the entire novel hangs on one absurdly small fuse: it is not that a war between empires found its way to a village, but that a woman could not bear to let a stranger freeze. Because of that, Wanyan Honglie saw Bao Xiruo; because he saw her, he wanted her; because he wanted her, two households were destroyed; because of that, one mother gave birth on the steppe and the other walked into a palace; and out of that came the wager, the two educations, and the meeting eighteen years later. On the surface this is a book about nation and duty. What it actually audits is intimate: one act of kindness, compounded over eighteen years into an entire national myth.

Three scenes in slow motion

1 · The meal at Zhangjiakou: an exam disguised as generosity

The craft of this scene is that it is built entirely out of giving things away: a meal, a coat, gold, a horse, each more valuable than the last, the rhythm escalating — while Guo Jing's reaction stays constant, which is to say absent. Jin Yong does not write hesitation, does not write regret, does not even let him feel virtuous. A person's actual character only exists in the moments when he does not know anyone is looking.

Better still is the direction of the information gap. The reader and Huang Rong both know this is a test; only Guo Jing does not. That produces an unusually comfortable place to watch from — we are smarter than the hero and we envy him anyway. The whole novel lives in that seat. And note the serial writer's hook: a poor young man has just given away everything he owns to a beggar. What does he do tomorrow? You buy the next paper. Jin Yong's best scenes are usually also his best cliffhangers; that is not a coincidence, it is a skill beaten into him by a daily deadline.

2 · Seven days behind a wall: a martial arts novel turned into a chamber play

Guo Jing is gravely wounded, and the two of them shut themselves into a room barely wider than a man is tall. The rule is seven days and nights without moving, without a sound, without being discovered. For tens of thousands of words the protagonists can do nothing but listen — while in the outer room, wave after wave of people arrive: enemies, relatives, frauds, a simpleton, each of them unspooling a piece of the plot.

The engine of this genre is action, and Jin Yong removes his hero's capacity for action entirely at the midpoint, leaving only hearing. Tension goes up rather than down, because the danger changes from "cannot win" to "cannot move." Information floods in, because people only tell the truth when they are sure there is no audience. And the reader is forced to share the protagonists' helplessness in real time. Formally it is nearly a stage play: one set, characters entering and exiting, everything carried by dialogue. Getting that into a serialized fight novel without losing the audience is the most underrated thing in the book.

3 · The last conversation: a thousand pages collapsed into two men talking

There is no final duel. The weapons are put away and what remains is a dying old man and a young one, talking on the grass.

The power of the scene comes from the collapse in scale: armies, a sacked city, decades of feuding, all compressed into a handful of exchanged sentences. And Jin Yong's handling is restrained — Guo Jing makes no speech, his lines are short and blunt to the point of insolence; the Khan is not made to repent, only to ask once whether he was wrong, and then to die. The book's last note is a question mark, not a verdict. There is also a piece of honesty that is easy to miss: the man asking has blood on him too. Guo Jing rode in that western campaign and supplied the siege plan. He is not a clean witness for the prosecution; he is standing on the same field, asking a question he cannot answer either. That single fact is what keeps the ending from being patriotic instruction.

The book's core device: martial skill as externalized character

What this novel actually invents is not a technique but an exchange rate that converts morality into combat power. Roughly four rules:

  • The split between inner force and technique. Technique can be stolen — half a manual was enough to make two murderers unstoppable — but inner force cannot; it can only be accumulated day by day. Cheating therefore has a ceiling. Ma Yu spends two years teaching Guo Jing how to breathe and not one move, and it is the most consequential instruction in the book.
  • Some arts select for temperament, not talent. Fighting with both hands requires that there be no second voice in your head, and the cleverest character in the novel cannot do it. The Dragon-Subduing Palms require a nature with nothing held in reserve, which is exactly Guo Jing. In this universe, "slow" is redefined as a scarce kind of purity.
  • Practising wrongly consumes the self. Ouyang Feng works through a corrupted text in reverse, rises to the top of the world, and comes apart — power and identity turn out to share one account, and drawing too fast overdraws it.
  • The Five Greats and the Contest of Mount Hua: a public, periodic ranking. Five people fight once to fix the order of the world, and again a quarter-century later. It makes the whole landscape legible — the reader always knows who outranks whom. This is the load-bearing engineering of popular fiction: establish a coordinate system everyone understands, then put the story inside it. And then Jin Yong dismantles his own instrument: the second contest is won by a lunatic, who runs away.

One honest note: "character converts directly into strength" is a moral fairy tale, not a fact about the world. Honest people do not get stronger by being honest. But that is precisely why generations keep rereading this book — it offers a universe in which virtue pays, and the payment is visible and measurable. You know it is false. You want to stay a while anyway.

What it forces you to think about

Question 1
Is the difference between Guo Jing and Yang Kang a choice, or an allocation?

Take the case for Yang Kang as far as it will go. He entered the palace at three. The prince raised him for eighteen years: tutors, masters, indulgence — and the love is real; nothing in the novel suggests it is a performance. Then a stranger in rags turns up and says: I am your father, and what you must now do is surrender your name, your money, your rank, your mother's security and the only man who ever doted on you, in order to claim a dead pauper and a conquered people. How exactly is an eighteen-year-old supposed to manage that? His mother never told him the truth, and by the time she wanted to it was too late. A large part of Yang Kang's evil is this: with no psychological preparation of any kind, he was asked to commit an identity suicide overnight.

Now the other side, equally hard. None of what he does afterwards is explained by confused identity. He kills Ouyang Ke to secure a good master. He steals the Beggars' staff to become a chief. He helps slaughter five old people on Peach Blossom Island who had never wronged him, to flatter a man he wanted as a teacher. He is not a lost young man caught between two fathers; he is an opportunist who converts each identity crisis into a payout. His birth handed him a problem. The choices were his, and he made them fast and accurately.

Set the two sides side by side and something more uncomfortable shows through: Guo Jing's goodness may not be his own achievement either. He had a mother who told him everything, seven poor but upright teachers, and a grassland with nothing to tempt him. He is never seriously tested — there is no moment in this novel where doing the right thing costs Guo Jing something he genuinely cares about. Yang Kang was tested that way and failed. Which leaves the real question: how much of your integrity is yours, and how much of it is simply that nobody ever named your price?

Question 2
Hong Qigong has killed more than two hundred people. What gives him standing to judge anyone?

Surrounded on Mount Hua, Qiu Qianren turns on the crowd: which of you dares say he has never killed, never done a shameful thing? Whoever dares, come and fight me. Nobody moves. It is the sharpest line in the book, and it belongs to the villain.

What makes it strong is that it exposes the premise of judgement. Most condemnation of the wicked is issued from the position "I have never done that" — and once that position is shown to be empty, condemnation becomes the lucky speaking to the unlucky. In this world nearly everyone has blood on their hands; when the righteous kill it is called clearing out vermin, and when the wicked kill it is called murder, and often the only difference is who is narrating. The challenge is not aimed only at a room full of fighters. It is aimed at every moral advantage any "us" has ever claimed over any "them."

Hong Qigong's answer is not a rebuttal; it is a ledger. He says he has killed more than two hundred people, that he remembers each one, that every one of them deserved it, and that if a single name on the list did not, he will pay for it himself. He does not claim to be clean. He claims his books are open, auditable, and open to being overturned. That is a far better answer than innocence, because it moves moral standing from purity to accountability.

But it leaves a hole. He keeps the ledger and he audits it; there is no third party. A man who has never believed himself wrong and a man who writes down every entry and stands ready to be called on it can look identical from outside. And note that Yideng later takes Qiu Qianren in and gives him a road to repentance, while Hong Qigong needs no repentance because his accounts are already square. So is a clear conscience the end point of morality, or its most comfortable exemption? Anyone who feels sure they are on the right side is worth a private minute answering Qiu Qianren first.

Question 3
Can a conqueror's achievements and his dead be entered on the same balance sheet?

Argue for Genghis Khan first, at full strength. The steppe he inherited was a place of raiding, abducted women and winter starvation. He unified it, gave it written law, built a working system of posts and administration, and opened a trade corridor across the continent. By any standard applied to statesmen, he ranks among the most consequential people who ever lived. And he is genuinely good to Guo Jing — recognizes a slow boy, betroths his daughter to him, hands him command. There is nothing false in that affection. His last question is not vanity; it is a man who built for a lifetime asking for a ruling.

Guo Jing's side is equally hard, and it is not "killing is wrong." His argument is about units of measurement: when the cost of an achievement is counted in human lives, the people who call it great are generally not the ones being counted. How much territory you hold is a metric the winner chose. Whether the survivors live better is the only metric both sides share.

And here is the embarrassment Jin Yong refuses to hide: Guo Jing is on that balance sheet himself. He besieged cities for the Mongols and applied Yue Fei's tactics to somebody else's walls. He is not a clean prosecution witness; he is an accomplice. Which makes his words heavier, not lighter — saying "this was wrong" costs nothing if you never took part; saying it after taking part, after collecting every benefit, and to the face of your benefactor, is dismantling your own position.

Bring it forward: almost every order we live inside has, underneath it, a set of people who were never asked. Every argument about "the price of development" and "necessary sacrifice" is a version of the Khan's question. The book's contribution is not an answer. It is the old man's silence.

Question 4
Guo Jing's betrothal to Huazheng: is keeping your word a virtue, or a virtue paid for with other people's lives?

He agreed to marry her. Nobody tricked him and nobody forced him; he nodded, and that settles it. "What I say holds" is the entire core of this character — he is not clever, not handsome, not articulate, and the one asset he owns is that his word can be posted as collateral. A promise that lapses because you later met someone you liked more was never a promise.

The other side is closer to how people actually live: he did not know what he was promising. A teenager who had never seen a second world nodded at a political marriage arranged by a khan. Locking three lives to a sentence spoken in ignorance is not fidelity; it is funding the asset called "I am a man of my word" out of other people's happiness. And nobody wants to be the party a promise is performed upon.

The novel's handling is an elegant dodge: Huazheng releases him, and his mother's death severs the Mongol thread, so he never has to decide. Jin Yong lets circumstance solve his hero's problem — he does not dare make a character built on fidelity face one genuine case where fidelity destroys someone, because the moment he chooses, Guo Jing has to be rewritten. The everyday form of this is the most familiar thing in the world: the path you set for yourself at twenty, and the promises you made to other people before you became someone else — do they still hold?

The obsession Jin Yong kept returning to

Lay his fifteen books side by side and one thread pulls steadily tighter: the question "which side am I on" and what it does to a person over time.

Guo Jing is the gentlest version — Han by blood, raised Mongol, indebted to Temujin, and in the end he chooses the south. Jin Yong charges him nothing ruinous for the choice; he simply rides away. By the time of Demi-Gods and Semi-Devils, the same question is pushed to its limit in Xiao Feng: Khitan by birth, raised Han, wanted as a weapon by both, and the only way he can void the problem is with his own life. Between Guo Jing and Xiao Feng, Jin Yong rewrites national identity from an honour into a terminal illness. And by The Deer and the Cauldron he hands the lead role to Wei Xiaobao, a scheming street brat who neither believes in loyalty nor cares which people he belongs to — and thereby cancels the value system of the previous fourteen books. A writer spending a career demolishing what he himself built is very rare in popular fiction.

The second thread is the definition of the hero itself, which keeps deforming. Guo Jing is the most upright version — and the line most often quoted about him does not actually appear until the sequel:

"The greatest of heroes serves his country and his people."— from the sequel, The Return of the Condor Heroes, spoken by Guo Jing to Yang Guo

Condor Heroes itself never states it that neatly; it simply builds a man in the shape of the sentence. The sequel's Yang Guo is the rebellious version, The Smiling, Proud Wanderer gives us the escapist version, and The Deer and the Cauldron has no hero at all. Jin Yong's confidence in the whole idea declined steadily as he aged.

Criticisms, honestly

Without flattery: the book has real faults, and its critics are not wrong.

  • The economics of the lucky break. Every jump in Guo Jing's ability comes from outside: Ma Yu supplies inner force, Hong Qigong the palms, Zhou Botong the manual and the two-handed art, Huang Rong the intelligence, Huang Yaoshi and Yideng the favours. The complaint is blunt but fair — a slow hero can clear the game slowly only because the author keeps mailing him presents. This is a structural consequence of serialization: every stretch needs a fresh climax and a fresh upgrade.
  • Huang Rong's ceiling, and the coupling of women with softness. Huang Rong is the most intelligent person in the book and solves nearly every problem in it, yet her capability is confined to the function of assisting Guo Jing; in the sequel she becomes a suspicious, partisan matron who treats Yang Guo badly. Look backwards and a pattern is already there: Bao Xiruo's kindness destroys two families, Mu Nianci loves a man she knows to be rotten until it kills her. Female feeling is repeatedly written as the source of catastrophe. The defence is that the male characters are equally schematic — vehicles for principles rather than people.
  • The nationalist frame. The Song–Jin–Mongol setup is Han-centred, with the humiliation of 1127 installed as the highest moral coordinate. In fairness, Jin Yong also gives Guo Jing a Mongol childhood, writes the Khan's affection as genuine, and ends the book with a rejection of conquest — he does not turn the other side into monsters. But national feeling is unmistakably the novel's power source, and his later books visibly work to dismantle that frame.
  • Which version are you reading? The 1950s serial, the 1970s revision and the 2003 re-revision differ substantially. Most famously, the serial contained a separate female character whom Jin Yong later folded into Mu Nianci; the 2003 text adds a strand of romantic subtext between Huang Yaoshi and his female disciple Mei Chaofeng, which readers largely disliked. Before arguing with anyone about a detail in this novel, establish which edition they read.
  • The status fight. Martial arts fiction was long treated by Chinese literary academia as disposable entertainment; in the 1990s that reversed. A Peking University professor taught Jin Yong seriously and rated him highly; a 1994 anthology of twentieth-century Chinese fiction placed him among the masters, ranked ahead of several canonical modern novelists, which caused a long-running quarrel; and in 1999 the novelist Wang Shuo published a piece in a national newspaper grouping Jin Yong's fiction with three other pop-culture phenomena as the era's "four vulgarities." The dispute has never been settled, and is itself now part of Chinese literary history.
  • The author's own politics. Jin Yong's editorial positions at Ming Pao drew criticism from several directions; in the 1980s he served on the committee drafting Hong Kong's Basic Law, where the political model he proposed provoked strong controversy, and he resigned from that role in 1989. Stated here as verifiable fact, without judgement.
  • History borrowed, not observed. Wang Chongyang, Qiu Chuji, the Quanzhen sect and the Dali emperor Duan Zhixing were real (Qiu Chuji did make an enormous journey to meet Genghis Khan), but the martial arts, the sect feuds and Yue Fei's treatise are invented. Read it as history and you will go wrong; read it as a historical novel and you will underrate it.

Ten sentences to take away

1 · The fuse is absurdly small: not two empires colliding over a village, but one woman unable to let a stranger freeze in the snow.

2 · The two names were a wager that memory could be installed; eighteen years later the answer is that remembering depends not on your name but on whether anyone finished the sentence.

3 · The engraved dagger was meant as a memento of national shame; it ends up being the entire arsenal with which a slow boy kills a first-rank master.

4 · The meal at Zhangjiakou is an examination, and everything turns on the fact that Guo Jing does not know examinations exist.

5 · Wanyan Honglie's love is genuine — the villain's devotion never gets discounted, which is what makes Yang Kang's choice genuinely hard.

6 · Yang Kang dies in the temple where his father killed himself, and what kills him is a father's attempt to protect a daughter.

7 · Huang Rong cannot learn to fight with both hands and Guo Jing can: in this universe, being slow is redefined as a scarce kind of purity.

8 · Five masters fight over a manual for twenty-five years; it ends up intact in the one head that never wanted it, and its authority is dissolved by a girl's lie.

9 · "Who here dares say he has never done a shameful thing" is the sharpest line in the book and it belongs to the villain; the answer to it is not a claim of innocence but an open ledger.

10 · There is no final duel — only a dying man asking whether his whole life was a mistake. The last mark is a question, not a verdict.
