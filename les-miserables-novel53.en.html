Les Misérables

Victor Hugo · 1862

In October 1815, every door in Digne closed on the same man. Each time he held out the yellow passport, it was handed back and the door shut. The last door he knocked on belonged to the bishop. It had no lock, because the bishop had given orders that it never be locked.
That night he slept in the first clean sheets of his life. Before dawn he got up, put the silver from the table into his knapsack, and went over the garden wall.
At daybreak the gendarmes brought him back. The bishop came forward looking almost cross with him for leaving in such a hurry: the silver was a gift — but how could he have forgotten the two candlesticks?

In one sentence

A man who served nineteen years at hard labour for stealing a loaf of bread is bought back, on his release, by a bishop who hands him two silver candlesticks; he then spends the rest of his life under one false name after another doing good — saving a town, buying a child out of slavery in all but name, sparing at a barricade the policeman who hunted him for a lifetime — and still never gets the law or his own son-in-law to concede that he is a good man, and dies alone in the light of those two candlesticks.

Coordinates

Victor Hugo (1802–1885) was the commanding figure of French Romanticism. Les Misérables appeared in 1862 after nearly twenty years of work, with a political rupture running through the middle of it: after Louis-Napoléon's coup of 1851 Hugo went into exile, and most of the book was finished on an island in the English Channel. A man his own country had expelled wrote the book demanding that country look at its own bottom rung.

Its place in literary history is unlike anyone else's: this is the last and largest of the nineteenth century's put-everything-in novels — melodrama, social survey, historical essay, theological argument, a treatise on the Paris sewers, an etymology of criminal slang, all under one cover. It is not a model of realism (Flaubert's line was heading the opposite way) and it is not a forerunner of modernism. It is what the craft of the novel looks like when one man pushes it to its outer edge with everything he has. It was also among the century's bestsellers: queues outside the bookshops on publication day.

The story

Act one · Nineteen years, and two candlesticks (1796–1815)

Jean Valjean was a tree-pruner from Faverolles whose widowed sister had seven children. One winter there was no work; he broke a baker's window and took a loaf. Five years. He refused to accept it, escaped four times, and each attempt was added to the sentence — of the nineteen years, fourteen were the price of not sitting still. This is the book's first piece of arithmetic: the law begins by punishing hunger and ends by punishing his reaction to the punishment.

Released in the autumn of 1815, he carries a yellow passport stating what he is, a piece of paper that keeps him standing at any door for less than three minutes. The Bishop of Digne, Myriel — the town calls him Monseigneur Bienvenu, "welcome" — feeds him, gives him a bed, and serves him on silver. In the night he steals the silver; at dawn the gendarmes bring him back. In front of them the bishop tells a lie, saying the silver was a gift, and adds the two candlesticksseed ①. When the gendarmes have gone, the bishop says something to this effect:

With this silver I have bought your soul back for you; it no longer belongs to evil, it belongs to good.Bishop Myriel to Jean Valjean, Vol. I, Book 2 · paraphrase, not a quotation

The sentence does not turn him good on the spot. The opposite: that same afternoon he robs a young Savoyard chimney-sweep called Petit-Gervais of a forty-sou coin — the boy's money rolls to his feet, he puts his shoe on it and shouts the child away. By the time he comes to himself and runs after him, it is dark and no amount of calling brings the boy back. He weeps for the first time, on his knees. Putting the turn here rather than in the bishop's dining room is one of the book's shrewdest decisions: forgiveness does not take effect on delivery; it waits until a man commits something he cannot forgive himself for, and only then bites. And legally it is this coin, not the loaf, that makes him a repeat offender and hunts him for the rest of his life.

Act two · Monsieur Madeleine: a false name holds up a town (1815–1823)

Eight years later the town of Montreuil-sur-Mer has a universally respected citizen called Monsieur Madeleine. He improved the local process for making black glass jewellery; a poor town now has a factory, a hospital, schools. He becomes mayor, and nobody knows who he is.

Two things happen that he cannot control. First: old Fauchelevent is pinned under an overturned cart, the mud is giving way, the jack will not come in time, and Madeleine crawls under and lifts the cart on his backseed ②. The crowd cheers. One man standing outside the crowd changes colour — Inspector Javert. He knows that kind of strength; he has seen it in the prison hulks at Toulon.

Second: Fantine. A Paris working girl, abandoned by a student who had tired of her, she leaves her daughter Cosette with the Thénardiers, innkeepers at Montfermeil, and comes home to work in Madeleine's factory. The forewoman discovers she has an illegitimate child and dismisses her — Madeleine knows nothing about it, and this is exactly where Hugo refuses to let him off: a good man need only look away once, upstream in a structure, and people die downstream. The Thénardiers' letters keep asking for money. She sells her hair, then two front teeth, then herself; when she claws the face of a well-dressed man who torments her in the street, Javert arrests her. Madeleine arrives and orders her released, and Javert defies a mayor to his face for the first time.

Fantine is already coughing blood. Just as Madeleine promises to fetch the child, Javert comes to denounce himself: he had written a letter accusing the mayor of being the convict Jean Valjean, and now knows he was wrong — the real Jean Valjean has been caught at Arras, an old man called Champmathieu, identified by three former convicts.

What follows is the book's most famous night; Hugo's chapter title means "a tempest in a skull". Confess, and the factory closes, the town is out of work, Fantine has no one. Stay silent, and a confused old man goes to the galleys for life in his place. He lights the fire and puts it out, takes the candlesticks down and puts them back. At dawn he takes the coach to Arras. In the courtroom he interrupts the trial, gives his name, then turns to the three old convicts and tells each of them where his scars are and what he was called inside. He is not confessing so much as proving, in public, that he knows them better than the court does. He walks out of the courtroom and back to the hospital. Fantine is waiting to see her daughter and sees Javert instead — she dies without her. He is arrested, and escapes.

Act three · The bucket in the woods (1823)

Here the novel drops an entire volume of Waterloo into the road. Thirty pages of battlefield, and then the camera settles on the night: a man who goes through the pockets of the dead drags a badly wounded officer out of a heap of bodies and helps himself to his watch and his cross. The officer is Colonel Pontmercy, and the one thing he retains from that night is that someone saved his life and the name of that someone was Thénardierseed ③. Valjean, meanwhile, is back at Toulon: he climbs the yard to save a sailor about to fall, then loses his own footing, goes into the sea in front of everyone and does not come up. He trades a rescue for a legal death — a piece of arithmetic he uses all his life.

Christmas Eve, 1823, Montfermeil. Cosette, eight years old, is sent to the spring in the woods for water. The bucket is taller than her legs; every twenty paces she has to set it down; she is too frightened to look behind her. Then she feels the bucket go light: a hand has taken the handle from behind and is walking beside her. She does not know this man, and she gives him her hand. He sits in the inn all night and sees exactly how the household treats her — broken plates are charged to her, the trip to the spring after dark is hers, the Thénardier girls have dolls and she has a lead knife. Next morning he pays fifteen hundred francs and takes her away, buys her the big doll from the shop window, and puts her into a small black mourning dress — her mother has just died, and this is the one time anyone treats her as a child with a motherseed ④.

They hide in Paris. Javert tracks them to the Gorbeau tenement and seals the street; Valjean climbs a wall with the child on his back, drops into a garden, and hears a voice call him "Monsieur Madeleine" — the gardener is Fauchelevent, the old man he once freed by lifting a cartpayoff ② ↑. The good deed that nearly exposed him becomes, eight years later, the only place on earth that can hide him. This is not coincidence, it is Hugo's accounting: every kindness is on the books, it simply settles late. Fauchelevent smuggles him into the Petit-Picpus convent, and for the next five years he is a gardener and Cosette is a boarder.

Act four · Marius: a father's last instruction (around 1830)

Marius Pontmercy is the colonel's son, raised by a royalist grandfather on the story that his father was a disreputable traitor. By the time he learns the truth — that his father came every year to stand at the back of a church and look at him from a distance — the man is dead, leaving a note: at Waterloo a man named Thénardier saved my life; if you can find him, do everything you can for himpayoff ③ ↑. Marius breaks with his grandfather, goes from royalist to republican, and joins the Friends of the ABC (in French, ABC puns on abaissé, the abased). And every day in the Luxembourg Gardens he sees a white-haired man walking with a girl.

Then comes the grimmest scene in the book. A poor family calling themselves Jondrette moves in next door; Marius bores a hole in the wall and watches them set up an ambush — invite a charitable "Monsieur Leblanc" in, then bolt the door and extort him. Monsieur Leblanc arrives with his daughter: the two people from the gardens. In the same instant that Marius recognises the girl, he hears the head of the gang announce himself: my name is Thénardier. He is holding the pistol the police gave him for a signal, and he cannot fire it — by his father's last instruction he owes this man a life. In the minutes he hesitates, the knives are out next door; Javert breaks the door down in the end, and Valjean goes out of the window.

Thénardier's elder daughter, Éponine, is in love with Marius, and finds Cosette's address for him anyway — knowing that handing it over means handing him to someone else. What follows is the novel's most lyrical stretch: two young people falling in love for a few weeks in an overgrown garden.

Act five · The barricade (5–6 June 1832)

General Lamarque's funeral turns into an insurrection. Republicans build a barricade out of prised-up paving stones, an overturned omnibus and a door. This is a real event — the June Rebellion of 1832, not the Revolution of 1789 — and it was crushed in a little over a day. On the barricade are Enjolras and his fellow students; old Mabeuf, a botanist who has sold his books to eat, who climbs up alone to hold the flag when everyone else flinches and is shot off it; and Gavroche, a Paris street child of eleven or twelve who is the son the Thénardiers threw away. Javert infiltrates as a spy, is recognised instantly by Gavroche, and is tied to a post.

Éponine follows in men's clothes. A soldier levels his musket at Marius; she puts her hand over the muzzle, and the ball goes through her palm into her chest. Dying in his arms, she gives up the letter she has been carrying — Cosette's letter to him, which she intercepted and kept. Her last request is that he kiss her on the forehead once she is dead.

The cartridges run out. Gavroche takes a basket out past the barricade into open ground and empties the ammunition pouches of dead soldiers one by one, singing a street song that makes fun of Voltaire and Rousseau. The soldiers miss several times and each time he stands up and goes on singing. The last shot hits him, and the song stops in the middle of a line.

Valjean arrives. He has read Cosette's blotting paper backwards in a mirror and knows she loves a young man who is going out to die. He enters the barricade in a National Guard uniform, never fires at a man, and spends the night shooting hats off heads and carrying the wounded down. He asks Enjolras for one reward: let me deal with the policeman. He walks Javert into an alley, cuts the ropes, tells him he lives at number seven Rue de l'Homme-Armé, and fires a shot into the airseed ⑤. The men inside hear the shot and assume it is done. At dawn the barricade falls and almost everyone on it dies. Marius is unconscious; Valjean lifts a grating and carries him down into the sewer.

Act six · The sewer and the candlesticks (1832–1833)

Hugo stops again for a whole volume on the history of the Paris sewers before letting anyone move. Valjean walks for hours in the dark with an unconscious young man on his back and steps into a stretch of sinking filth where the water reaches his lips before his feet find bottom. The grating at the outlet is locked. A voice speaks out of the dark: Thénardier. Taking him for a colleague disposing of a body, he sells him the key with complete professionalism — and quietly tears a strip from the coat of the man on his back, to blackmail somebody with later.

Javert is standing on the bank outside. Valjean asks for one thing: let me take this boy to his grandfather's house first, then do what you like with me. Javert agrees, follows him, waits downstairs while he goes up and while he comes down — and then gets into a carriage and lets him go home. He walks to a bridge over the Seine and stands there a long timepayoff ⑤ ↑. He has believed one thing all his life: there is no justice outside the law, and a criminal is always a criminal. And now he owes a convict his life, and the convict asked for nothing in return for it. Both facts are true at once, and his head has room for one. He leaves a note containing several suggestions for improving prison discipline, and goes over the parapet.

Marius lives. The grandfather capitulates in tears and consents to the marriage. On 16 February 1833 the couple are married, and Valjean says his thumb is injured and he cannot sign the register. He will not put a false name on a true document. The day after the wedding he takes Marius aside: I am an ex-convict, and Cosette is no relation of mine. He does not defend himself with a single word; he does not mention Monsieur Madeleine, or the sewer. Marius is shocked, disgusted, and wrong about nearly everything — he assumes this man robbed Monsieur Madeleine, and that he killed Javert that night at the barricade. He does not throw him out. He simply makes the visits shorter: daily at first, then every other day; the fire in the parlour stops being lit, and a chair is taken away. He talks a man out of existence by being polite, which is far more modern than a door slammed. And the household is living on the six hundred thousand francs Valjean buried in the woods at Montfermeil.

The old man stops coming. He stops eating and lies down to wait. In those days he does one thing: he opens the small valise he has never let out of his sight, takes out the little heap of children's clothes kept inside — a small black dress, small stockings, small shoes — lays them out on the bed one by one, and goes down on his kneespayoff ④ ↑. It is the mourning dress he put on her the day he took her out of Montfermeil. What he has been keeping is not his daughter; it is the one evening in his life when somebody needed him.

The ending is brought about by the worst man in the book. Thénardier comes to blackmail Marius with two "secrets": your father-in-law is an ex-convict (Marius knows), and he murdered a man — here is the strip of cloth torn from the corpse's coat. Marius takes the cloth, holds it against the coat that was cut off him, and the tear fits exactly. The "corpse" was himself, and the man who carried him through the sewer is the man he has spent two months quietly showing the door. Thénardier extorts his money and sails for America to become a slave trader — and so his father's debt is discharged.

By the time the two of them run to the Rue de l'Homme-Armé, the old man can no longer finish a sentence. He asks Cosette to sit closer and tells her for the first time that her mother was called Fantine and how much she suffered. The two silver candlesticks are burning at the bedsidepayoff ① ↑ — the objects a bishop added as an afterthought eighteen years earlier, carried from a convict's knapsack to this bed through six names and one sewer. He dies in their light. In a corner of Père-Lachaise there is a stone with no name on it; someone once wrote four lines of verse there in pencil, and a few rains took them off.

Wind the whole chain back to one fuse: not the loaf — the lie the bishop told in front of the gendarmes. The loaf only put a man in the galleys, which was routine; the period was full of such men, who came out, went on stealing, died in a ditch, and squared the ledger. What lights the next thousand pages is a man of standing saying something untrue in front of an officer of the law, with no conditions attached. Everything after that is the aftershock of that lie: Madeleine's factory, Fantine's hospital bed, Fauchelevent's cart, the bucket at Montfermeil, the shot fired into the air at the barricade, Javert's last step off the parapet. Hugo's wager is that mercy is not a result but a cause — it does not end a story, it detonates one.

Two scenes in slow motion

1 · The bucket: giving a child's fear a unit of weight

Hugo writes Cosette fetching water entirely in measurables: the height of the rim against her, how many paces before she has to set it down, the cold of the water slapping her calves, the sound in the woods that might be somebody following, the counting she does in her head so that she will not cry. Fear is translated into weight, and weight is something a reader's body reads directly. Then the decisive stroke: the bucket stops being heavy. She has not heard footsteps; what she registers first is the load going away — a hand has closed on the handle.

It is the book's finest instance of sensation first, explanation afterwards. Had it been written as "a man came out of the trees", it would be an ordinary meeting. Written as the weight vanishing, it becomes a child's entire definition of rescue: being saved means the thing in your hand suddenly goes light, and you don't yet know why. The gesture recurs for hundreds of pages — settling Fantine's debts, taking Champmathieu's sentence, carrying Marius through the sewer, each of them a handle taken out of somebody else's hand.

2 · The parapet: the one time we go inside Javert

For most of the novel Javert has no interior at all; he is a man seen from outside — the hat brim, the nasal voice, the walk. Then, in the second-to-last volume, Hugo gives him a chapter whose title means "Javert derailed", like a train leaving the rails. He stands at the parapet with the black, circling water below. He is going through a list: he released a convict, which is dereliction; but he released him because the convict had released him first, which is a debt of gratitude. And his world has exactly two boxes, lawful and unlawful, with no box labelled gratitude. He tries to fit Valjean into "criminal" and cannot; he tries to fit himself into "good policeman" and cannot. Hugo's rhythm here is extremely slow, coming back to the same point over and over like a man who cannot get out of his own sentence. The note he leaves behind contains suggestions for improving the treatment of prisoners — a man optimising the institution at the moment he has decided to die. That detail is crueller than the jump: he is not killed by conscience but by the kind of wholeness that cannot hold a contradiction.

The central device: a machine that converts morality into numbers

On its surface Les Misérables is an overloaded lumber room — Waterloo, convents, slang, sewers, ready at any moment to stop the story for forty pages. But its central device is single: the novel translates every moral question into a number first, and then shows you how the numbers fail to reconcile.

Part one: a narrator who stops to talk. This is what modern readers find hardest — the story is taut and the author suddenly inserts forty pages of Waterloo. But the detours are not a loss of control; they are the other half of the machine. If Hugo means to prove that circumstance makes the man, he is obliged to write the circumstance too. The sewer volume is not background material, it is argument: he wants you to believe that a city putting its filth underground where it need not be looked at, and a society putting people into the galleys where they need not be looked at, are the same piece of engineering. You may find it ponderous, but it is logically necessary — and the cost is real: the book's pace is repeatedly cut off by its own reasoning.

Part two: names. In the course of the book he is convict 24601, Monsieur Madeleine, convict 9430, Monsieur Fauchelevent, Urbain Fabre, the old man of the Rue de l'Homme-Armé. The law recognises names, and every name in this book is false; only the actions are true. At the end he will not even sign a marriage register, and claims an injured hand instead. The unmarked stone is therefore not a sad flourish but the argument taken to its conclusion: if a man's identity can only be issued by a file, then the most honest file is a blank one.

What it makes you think about

Question one
The bishop's candlesticks: a solution, or an evasion? If a man is saved by running into a saint, what about everyone who doesn't?

Take the case for "evasion" as far as it will go. Valjean is saved because he happened to knock at the door of possibly the only bishop in France who would lie to the police for a convict. That is not a system, it is a lottery. Inside the same book, Fantine does not win it: she is honest too, she works too, she loves her child too, and what she meets is a forewoman, a bully in the street and a policeman — so she sells her hair, her teeth, herself, and dies in a hospital. Worse is the political afterlife of this kind of story: it defines the way out for the poor as being seen by a kind individual, so that nothing has to change except the supply of kind individuals; the people holding the resources move from the position of being questioned to the position of being thanked. Under every story about a rich man paying a poor student's fees sits the same structure: a question that tax and policy should have answered gets answered for the reader by one moving act of private generosity. And the odds on the lottery are never printed.

Now take the other side as far as it will go. First, Hugo never offered the candlesticks as social policy — his preface is a political indictment: so long as law and custom go on manufacturing hells on earth, so long as poverty degrades men, hunger ruins women and darkness stunts children, books of this kind cannot be useless. He is demanding legislation; the candlesticks are only what he can give one particular man. Second, and harder to answer: institutions distribute from a list — bread, a bed, work, due process — and one thing is not on the list, namely someone deciding out loud that you are not a thief. What actually traps a man just out of prison is usually not the absence of food but the uniformity of the look that confirms he is still what he was, and no service counter issues that. Third, the two operate on different clocks: reform takes a decade, and a man takes one afternoon to slide past recovery. That afternoon he had already robbed Petit-Gervais. The candlesticks did not prevent it; they made it the last time.

Question two
Should Javert have let Valjean go? If the law is wrong, what is the person enforcing it supposed to do?

Put Javert's case better than he puts it himself. On what authority does he release the man? His private judgement that this one deserves it. But "the officer decides by private judgement who gets punished" is precisely what the rule of law exists to abolish. A Javert who can make an exception for Valjean is a Javert who can make one for the mayor's nephew, or for a man who once did him a favour; discretion is the doorway every corruption comes through, and an exception granted because "he's actually a good man" is procedurally identical to one granted because "he's my brother". Worse still, what Valjean gave him was not evidence but a debt — a policeman releasing a suspect who once saved his life is not mercy in any era, it is the perfect form of a bribe. His suicide therefore has a cold honesty to it: he has discovered that he is no longer reliable, and he believes an unreliable enforcer is more dangerous than a criminal. He does not ask the system for an exemption; he deletes himself from the system.

Now the other side, at full strength. What this law asks of this man is that he be held until death for a loaf of bread and a forty-sou coin. When the proportion between act and penalty is that absurd, "strict enforcement" stops being neutral and becomes an active choice. Javert is usually read as a machine, but he is one of the most wilful people in the novel: he volunteers for the pursuit, volunteers the denunciation, volunteers his own confession at Arras — his rigour is a passion, not a numbness. "I'm only following the rules" is not available to him: no rule sent him after a man who had been mayor for eight years and made a town prosperous; he went himself. And what he finally collides with is not the simple proposition that the law is wrong. It is something more uncomfortable: there exists a kind of goodness that is neither legal nor illegal, for which the law has made no provision at all, and the position he has occupied all his life cannot see that part of the world.

The modern reruns are dense and mostly unsolvable in shape: refusing an application through the proper channel when it plainly should be granted; applying the policy to someone who missed the deadline by three days; enforcing to the letter a rule you know is being used in the wrong place. The common factor is that whoever makes the exception carries the whole risk personally, while whoever follows the rule can hand the responsibility to the rule. Javert's bridge offers no answer, only an invoice — he takes on neither the responsibility of the exception nor the responsibility of continuing, and ends up carrying a third thing instead. Hugo is plainly on Valjean's side (he spent a lifetime writing against the death penalty), and he still gives Javert an entire chapter to finish his reasoning before he dies: he does not write the opposition as a fool, he writes a man backed into a corner by his own integrity.

Question three
The barricade of 1832: knowing it will fail, do you go up?

Take the case against, at full strength. The facts are unkind: the rising was put down in a little over a day, Paris did not join in, almost everyone on the barricade died, and the regime did not move an inch. They died, and no worker's wages rose and no law changed because of it. On the bill there is a child of eleven or twelve — Gavroche did not choose martyrdom, he followed the adults into the street and went out to pick up their cartridges. This is what every noble-failure story should be asked about: the people who decide to die are the ones who have read books and can write manifestos, and the first to die is usually the one who understands least what is happening. And one step further: Enjolras is calm, pure and intolerant of disagreement, and readers can supply for themselves what that combination grows into in other periods of history. Hugo knew it too — he spends a long passage distinguishing a riot from an insurrection, which tells you he understood that the two look identical from outside, and that the test for telling them apart usually only becomes available once the result is known.

Now the case for, also at full strength. First, hindsight makes failure obvious and nobody had it in advance: the revolution of 1830 was the same barricades and much the same men, and it won; the evidence available to the insurgents supported "this could work". Requiring people to act only when the odds are clear is a verdict of irrationality on everyone who ever moves first — and without a first there is no second. Second, a defeated rising is not zero: these men died unnoticed and the surviving record of them is essentially this book, which turned a hopeless street fight into the most famous barricade of the next century and a half. Some returns do not settle in the current period. Third, there is an asymmetry built into the dilemma: the people who stay home are also choosing, but the consequences of that choice are spread over many years and many people, and nobody itemises them.

In daily life the shape is: knowing nothing will change, do you say the dissenting thing in the meeting; knowing it means you're finished there, do you hand in the letter. The question is never "will it work", it is "are you willing to bet before the result is in". The novel does not answer for you, but it lets Marius live, marry and inherit, while Enjolras dies at a first-floor window. Hugo declines to let the purest man survive, which is probably the least convenient part of his honesty.

The recurring obsession

Lay Hugo's books side by side and the same fixation keeps surfacing: how does a person the world has ruled to be no longer human prove that he is — and by what right was the ruling made?

He was writing this very early. The Last Day of a Condemned Man (1829) is nothing but the monologue of a man about to be guillotined, and withholds his crime so that you cannot tell yourself he deserved it; Claude Gueux (1834), about a prisoner who kills a warden, is effectively a petition for abolition. This was not a literary pose: he appealed publicly for the American abolitionist John Brown, and after the fall of the Paris Commune in 1871 he announced from Brussels that he would shelter hunted Communards, for which his house had its windows broken. Valjean's condition — the law has decided, and the law is wrong — he wrote for half a century.

The other obsession is whether revolution and mercy can coexist, and he never dropped it. In Ninety-Three (1874) the republican commissioner Cimourdain sentences to death the pupil he loves as a son, Gauvain, because Gauvain let the enemy marquis escape — a marquis who had just carried three children out of a fire; at the instant the blade falls, Cimourdain shoots himself. It is almost the Javert scene rewritten, except that this time Hugo kills principle and compassion together.

Criticism and controversy

The objections carry real weight, several of them from the best writers of his own generation, and there is no need to cover for the book:

  • His peers found it vulgar, nearly unanimously. It was an enormous commercial success and the French literary world was cutting: Flaubert privately judged that the book contained neither truth nor greatness; Baudelaire published an appreciative review and used contemptuous words about it in a letter to his mother; Barbey d'Aurevilly attacked it; the Goncourts were dismissive; Lamartine wrote a long, courteous essay whose burden was that genius can also be dangerous. In 1864 the Vatican placed it on the Index of forbidden books. Readers and critics split violently over this novel, and are still split a hundred and sixty years later.
  • It is long, and deliberately so. Waterloo, the constitution of convents, a treatise on slang, the history of the sewers — dozens of pages at a time standing in front of the plot. The defence ("these are arguments, not ornament") holds; so does the cost: the book asks readers to keep trusting the author's good faith for several hundred pages, and a great many put it down somewhere inside Waterloo. More than one later translation has simply moved the digressions into an appendix.
  • Too much coincidence, too much melodrama. The Thénardiers appear to be tied to the protagonists with a rope: the man robs Marius's father, mistreats Valjean's adopted daughter, has a daughter who loves Marius and a son who dies on Marius's barricade, and is personally waiting at the sewer outlet. By realist standards these are faults; by Hugo's providential design they are the structure — and a reader is entitled to refuse the trade.
  • The women. Fantine is written as a spotless martyr; after Cosette is lifted away from the water bucket she has almost no will of her own — she is hidden, moved, betrothed; Éponine's worth is realised almost entirely in taking a bullet for a man who does not love her, and her death is made beautiful. In this book women are rescued, sacrificed and loved, and very seldom permitted to choose. The defence — that Hugo is filing a case on their behalf, and that "hunger ruins women" is one of his three indictments in the preface — is true; but filing a case and letting a person be a subject are two different things.
  • Its politics are questionable. The classic criticism from the left: the sympathy for the poor is boundless, while the way out lands on personal moral conversion plus the goodwill of the rich — one good industrialist opening a factory, one good bishop giving alms — with organised change appearing only as a barricade that was always going to lose. It is a book indicting inequality and also a book that encourages the belief that inequality can be cancelled out by kindness. Hugo's own position moved: a royalist in youth, later a republican who paid for it with nearly twenty years of exile, and who did not return until the empire fell.

Ten sentences to take away

1. The loaf got him five years; refusing to accept it got him nineteen. The law punishes hunger first and then punishes the reaction to the punishment.

2. What ignites this book is not suffering; it is a bishop saying something untrue in front of the gendarmes with no conditions attached.

3. The pardon did not take effect on delivery: he robbed a child hours after taking the candlesticks, and it was that robbery that switched them on.

4. He performs one gesture all his life — taking the handle out of someone else's hand. The child's bucket, Champmathieu's sentence, Marius in the sewer: the same hand each time.

5. Monsieur Madeleine looked away once, and a factory girl was dismissed, sold her hair and her teeth, and died in a hospital. Negligence upstream is paid for downstream, in lives.

6. Javert is not a villain; he is a man with two boxes, lawful and unlawful. He jumps because something arrived that fits in neither.

7. His last act is to write suggestions for improving the prisons — he never doubted the institution, he only concluded he was no longer fit to serve it.

8. Marius throws nobody out. He shortens the visits, stops lighting the fire, removes a chair — the standard modern method for handling what one cannot digest.

9. What finally clears Valjean's name is a blackmailer at the door. The truth arrives too late to give him a single extra day.

10. The nameless stone is not a melancholy flourish; it is the conclusion: if identity can only be issued by a file, the most honest file is blank.
