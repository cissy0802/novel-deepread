Novels, Deep-Read · Vasily Grossman · No.120

Life and Fate

Life and Fate (Zhizn i sudba) · Vasily Grossman · completed c. 1960 · first published 1980

中文 →

Late summer, 1941. A Jewish ghetto in a small Ukrainian town. An elderly woman doctor sits up at night writing to her son by the light of a candle stub; outside, a few old streets have been fenced in with wire.
She knows the letter probably won't get out, and that she probably won't live to see the autumn. She doesn't write about how to save herself. She writes about her neighbours' faces these past months: who helped her, who moved into her rooms, who suddenly stopped recognising her in the street.
By the end she has only one thing left to tell him: live. The letter will take more than a year to reach him. By the time it does, she will have been shot outside town with thousands of the town's other Jews, and buried.

Full spoilers throughout — who dies in the house at Stalingrad, who walks into the gas chamber, who is interrogated in the Lubyanka, who gets a phone call from Stalin and then signs a letter he will regret for the rest of his life. The weight of this book sits on its ending: its cruellest move is not showing the brutality of two totalitarian states, but showing a decent man who has just been brave once go soft the moment he is forgiven.

In One Sentence

In the winter of 1942–43, under the walls of Stalingrad, one family — the Shaposhnikovs — is scattered across the front line, the home front, a German concentration camp and a Soviet labour camp; Grossman sets Hitler's barbed wire and Stalin's barbed wire side by side in the same book and asks a single question: in a state built to grind people into cogs, how much freedom can a person keep — and where does that last bit of freedom come from?

Bearings

Vasily Grossman (1905–1964) was born into a Jewish family in Berdychiv, Ukraine, trained as a chemist, and became a Soviet writer in the 1930s. During the war he was a correspondent for the army paper Red Star, spent months at Stalingrad, and later went with the army into Poland, where he wrote one of the first accounts of the Treblinka death camp. His mother was murdered in the massacre of Berdychiv's Jews in September 1941. The letter in the opening is the one he wrote for her.

Life and Fate is the sequel to his Stalin-era novel For a Just Cause (1952; newly translated into English as Stalingrad). The characters carry over; the spirit is turned inside out. He finished it in 1960 and submitted it to a magazine. In February 1961 the KGB came to his flat and took the manuscript, the typescripts, even the typewriter ribbons. By Grossman's own record, the Party's chief ideologist, Mikhail Suslov, told him the book could not be published for two or three hundred years. He died in 1964. A copy hidden by friends was photographed onto microfilm and smuggled out; it appeared in Switzerland in 1980 and in the Soviet Union only in 1988. The slot it occupies in literary history is "the War and Peace of the twentieth century" — a common label, and not an unproblematic one; more on that later.

The Story

Act One · A Family Spread Across a Map

The book turns on an old woman: Alexandra Vladimirovna Shaposhnikova, an intellectual of the old school, upright to the point of stubbornness. Stalingrad is under siege and her house has burned; she has been evacuated with her eldest daughter, Lyudmila, to Kazan on the middle Volga.

Lyudmila is married to the physicist Viktor Shtrum, a Jew and a leading figure at an Academy of Sciences institute, which has also been moved to Kazan. Lyudmila's son by her first marriage, Tolya, is an artillery lieutenant. He is wounded, taken to a hospital in Saratov, and dies after surgery. By the time she gets there he has already been buried. She sits by the new grave all night, tucking clothes around her dead son, talking to him, scolding him for not dressing warmly enough — as if he were still a small boy who felt the cold. Grossman doesn't show her weeping; he shows her spending a whole night looking after someone who no longer needs looking after. From then on, everyone in the family feels that Lyudmila has turned to stone.

The second daughter, Yevgenia — Zhenya — is a painter: young, proud, unwilling to settle. She has divorced her husband, Krymov, an Old Bolshevik and political commissar whom the Party has sidelined for years. Now she is in love with Novikov, a tank corps commander, a steady officer who rose from the bottom, massing his forces on the steppe for a great counter-offensive.

Alexandra's grandson Seryozha, still in his teens, is a soldier inside Stalingrad. And there is an old family friend, the army doctor Sofya Osipovna, Jewish, unmarried, loud-voiced and soft-hearted; at the end of the previous novel she was captured by the Germans outside Stalingrad together with an old revolutionary called Mostovskoy.

The net is cast very wide: a German camp, a Soviet camp, the institute in Kazan, the ruins of Stalingrad, the Lubyanka, a sealed freight train. More than a hundred characters, nearly all tied to this family by some thread.

Act Two · Two Kinds of Barbed Wire

The novel opens in a German concentration camp. Mostovskoy is held here, a white-haired Old Bolshevik whose faith has never wavered. In his barracks are an old Menshevik, Chernetsov; a born leader, the Red Army major Yershov; and an oddity — Ikonnikov.

Ikonnikov was a Tolstoyan in his youth, found God and lost Him, and walked through the Ukrainian famine and the starved villages of collectivisation. He keeps pressing some closely written pages on Mostovskoy. What they say, roughly, is this: Seed ①every cause in history fought in the name of the Good — the capital-G Good that comes with a programme: religious, revolutionary, national — ends in bloodshed, because once you are sure you hold the Good you have a reason to destroy whoever stands in its way. What actually survives in human life is something else: a small kindness with no reason behind it, no programme and no hope of return — an old woman handing water to a wounded enemy, say, or a soldier sharing his bread with a prisoner. Such kindness is feeble, like rain; it cannot beat any machine. But it has never been wiped out. Mostovskoy thinks it is muddled preaching and tosses the pages aside. In this act they read like the ravings of a crank.

The camp's underground is run by a handful of Soviet Communists. They need someone to lead an uprising, and Yershov is the obvious choice — everyone defers to him. But they don't trust him: his father was branded a kulak during collectivisation. So they use their network to slip Yershov's name onto a transport list for Buchenwald. Inside the Germans' wire, the Soviets are still carrying out their own purge.

Meanwhile, in a labour camp in the Soviet north, Lyudmila's first husband, Abarchuk, is felling timber. He was arrested on false charges and remains a pure believer: he is sure he was taken by mistake and everyone else is a real enemy, and he defends the very system that locked him up. Grossman alternates the two camps, a German chapter then a Soviet one, written in almost exactly the same way: numbers, roll calls, hunger, informers, guards, prisoners selling each other out. This is where the reader first feels the discomfort: the two worlds look far too alike.

Later the SS officer Liss summons Mostovskoy for a night-time talk. He doesn't hit him. He calmly explains that the two of them are mortal enemies, yet when you look at us, you see your own face. Both sides have one party, one leader, one ideal of cleansing humanity on history's behalf; your camps and our camps are two versions of the same idea. Mostovskoy wants to answer and finds he has nothing to say that doesn't sound recited. It is the book's most famous conversation, and one of the main reasons it was banned.

Act Three · A Letter

Kazan. Shtrum receives the letter from the opening. His mother, Anna Semyonovna, describes the days when the Germans arrived: a neighbour who was taking tea with her yesterday saying today that this is what Jews deserve; people already eyeing her furniture; someone she barely knew bringing her food in secret. In the ghetto she goes on treating patients. She writes that she is not afraid, only sad for him, and that being his mother has been her happiness. Seed ②In the last lines she tells him to live, over and over.

Shtrum reads it many times alone and doesn't show his wife. He feels he owes his mother something, and can't say what.

At the home of his colleague Sokolov, a few intellectuals gather in the evenings — a historian named Madyarov, a translator named Karimov — and over tea they get bolder and bolder: about 1937 and the purges, about collectivisation, about what real freedom of speech would be like. Afterwards each of them goes cold: is someone in this room going to report it? On those evenings Shtrum feels he is living like a human being. And in those same weeks, a problem in nuclear theory that has tormented him for a long time suddenly comes clear — a new mathematical framework, beautiful almost beyond reason. He knows it is the most important thing he will ever do.

At Sokolov's, too, he slowly falls in love with Sokolov's wife, Marya Ivanovna — shy, gentle, always listening. Both of them know it can go nowhere.

Act Four · House Six Stroke One

Stalingrad, the factory district in the north of the city. The Germans have pushed through to the Volga, but one building, shot full of holes, still sticks out into their lines, held for weeks by a small band of men: "House 6/1." Its commander, Captain Grekov, is coarse, a superb fighter, and answers to no one. In his house there is no rank; people say whatever they like, even about Stalin. Seryozha is here, and so is a young radio operator, Katya. Under the shelling, the two of them fall in love.

Higher up, the house is judged "politically unreliable," and a commissar is sent to bring it into line: Krymov, Zhenya's ex-husband. Krymov is a sincere revolutionary who remembers Lenin's day and has watched comrades taken one by one; he believes in the Party and knows it has wronged many people. He asks Grekov: what is it you actually want? Grekov says he wants freedom. Krymov hears the weight of the word, and its danger.

That night, sleeping in the house, Krymov is grazed on the head by a bullet. He doesn't see who fired, but he is sure it was Grekov. Back at headquarters he writes a report. Seed ③As he writes, he feels he is doing his duty, the way the Party taught him: when there's a problem, write it down and hand it in. Grekov's house will be investigated; Grekov will be dealt with.

Before any investigators arrive, the Germans launch their great assault. On the last day Grekov finds an excuse to send Seryozha and Katya out of the building with a message — he has seen what is between them and wants them to live. Soon after they leave, the house collapses; Grekov and everyone with him die inside. The report is never needed.

Act Five · Encirclement

November: the Soviet counter-offensive. Novikov's tank corps is to break into the German line at a set hour. The hour comes, and the phone keeps ringing: his superiors, pressing him again and again. He can see the German anti-tank positions ahead that the artillery has not yet silenced, and he knows that if he goes now his tank crews will burn by the dozen. He orders the guns to fire for another eight minutes. Eight minutes, in an operation like this, is disobedience. When he attacks, the breakthrough succeeds with far fewer losses than expected. His commissar, Getmanov — a seasoned Party operator — praises him warmly to his face, and then puts the disobedience into a report.

That same winter, Sofya Osipovna is packed into a freight wagon heading west. It is full of Jews, old and young, every kind of person; among them is a six-year-old boy, David, who had been sent to his grandmother's for the summer holidays. The train travels for days. Sofya has never had children, and by instinct she keeps David close. When it stops, there is a band playing and a station that looks almost real. On the platform the Germans call out: doctors, step forward — stepping forward means living. She is a doctor. She does not move. She does not let go of David's hand.

That same winter, Krymov is arrested at the front and taken to the Lubyanka in Moscow. He is charged with old links to Trotskyists. For days and nights the interrogators won't let him sleep; later they beat him. Payoff ③ ↑Only weeks before, he was sitting at a headquarters table writing a report on Grekov; now he sits across a different table listening to someone read out a report written about him. The kind of paper he once filled in has now landed on his own head — Grossman adds not a word of comment, only lets the two tables face each other across the pages. What tortures him more is that the interrogator mentions something from the past he told no one but Zhenya. He thinks: it was her.

Zhenya, meanwhile, is with Novikov, so happy she hardly believes it. When she hears that Krymov has been arrested, she goes to Moscow without stopping to think, and stands in line all day at the window where parcels for prisoners are handed in, among women who don't know whether their husbands and sons are alive, and passes a parcel through. She writes to Novikov: she won't be coming back; she cannot abandon Krymov while he is in trouble.

The parcel reaches the cell. Krymov looks at what is inside and knows she sent it. He understands two things: she did not betray him; she has come back. In his cell, he weeps.

Act Six · A Phone Call

The institute returns to Moscow. Stalingrad is won, and the air, if anything, turns colder: the institute quietly begins reshuffling staff, and most of the names taken off the list are Jewish. Shtrum's beautiful new theory is attacked as idealism cut off from practice, as "Talmudic" abstraction. He is told to make a public self-criticism at a meeting of the whole institute.

On the day of the meeting he puts on his suit and tie, sits at home, and in the end doesn't go. It is the bravest day of his life. Then it all comes, piece by piece: he loses his post and his laboratory, friends stop calling, even Sokolov pulls away. Every night he waits for the knock on the door; he and Lyudmila have thought through every detail of what happens if he is taken.

Then one day the telephone rings. A voice with a Georgian accent asks how his work is going, says he is working in a very interesting direction, asks whether he needs anything. It is Stalin himself — the Soviet Union needs nuclear physics. Overnight, everything comes back: the laboratory, the funding, a car and driver, the beaming greetings of the directors; those who attacked him hardest are the first to shake his hand. Shtrum feels an elation that is close to humiliation.

Not long after, some of the institute's leaders ask him to sign a collective letter. Western newspapers have spoken up for two eminent doctors who were convicted, and this letter, in the name of distinguished scientists, will declare that the men really were guilty. Shtrum knows they are innocent. He knows he can refuse — he refused only months ago, when he had nothing; now he has everything to lose. He signs.

Payoff ② ↑That night he thinks of his mother. Her letter has been with him since Act Three, and he has always felt he owed her something; now he knows what. She told him to live, and what she wanted was never for him to save his skin but for him to live as a human being — which is exactly how she lived, in the ghetto, to her last day. Planted in the first half of the book, the letter reads at first as a farewell; here it turns over and becomes a judgment on her son. Shtrum vows that next time will be different. Grossman doesn't tell us whether it is.

The Germans at Stalingrad surrender. Columns of prisoners are marched through the ruins, and the townspeople hate them so much they shake; some rush forward to hit them. A Russian woman pushes to the front with a piece of bread in her fist — she is going hungry herself — and seems about to throw it at them. Instead she presses it into the hand of a German soldier who is wasted almost to nothing. She can't explain why. Payoff ① ↑This is what Ikonnikov wrote on those pages: kindness without a reason. The pages were dropped on the barracks floor in Act Two; Ikonnikov himself was later shot for refusing to help build a gas chamber. Grossman lets his theory come true in a woman who has never heard his name — not because she read anything, but precisely because she didn't think at all.

Novikov is summoned to Moscow "to explain himself," his fate unknown. Alexandra Vladimirovna returns to Stalingrad and stands for a long time in front of the ruins of her home. The book closes in a forest in early spring: the ice not yet melted, the trees still black, life already beginning to stir.

The Fuse

Looking back, the fuse of this book is not at Stalingrad but in Berdychiv: a mother, about to die, writing to tell her son to live. The whole novel, its hundred-odd characters and dozens of threads, is an answer to the question her letter leaves unspoken — what does it mean to live? Grekov's house, Novikov's eight minutes, Zhenya's parcel, the hand Sofya won't let go, the shovel Ikonnikov refuses to lift: each is one answer. Shtrum's signature is another.

Three Slow-Motion Scenes

Slow Motion One · A Whole Chapter That Is Only a Letter

The mother's letter is a chapter of its own, early in Part One, with not one word from the narrator: no setting, no description of Shtrum's face as he reads; the letter is simply put in front of us. It is the only time the book lets someone already dead speak in the first person.

Its power comes from a mismatch of voice. The writer is describing the most terrible things — neighbours, property, wire, the shooting that is coming tomorrow — in the tone of a mother reminding her child to eat and not to worry. The less she cries out, the less the reader can bear it. Grossman's own mother died in Berdychiv; he never received a letter from her. This chapter is the letter he wrote in her place. Knowing that makes it nearly unreadable — but the novel itself never says so. It lets the letter be only a letter.

Slow Motion Two · Behind the Door

The chapter in which Sofya and David enter the gas chamber is one of the closest any twentieth-century novel came to that place. Grossman doesn't stop at the door; he goes in with the crowd: the undressing, the hair being cut, bare feet on cold concrete, the bodies pressing tighter, the door shutting.

He gives the point of view to Sofya, and the last pages stay pressed against her body: the weight of the child in her arms, his breathing, his eyes that understand nothing. The boy goes first; she is still holding him. She has never been a mother. In this moment, she feels she has become one.

The passage is written almost entirely in short sentences and bodily sensations: cold, crush, breath, hands. There is no indictment of the killers, not a single adjective doing the reader's outrage for them. The power comes from Grossman refusing to look away — where others cut at the door, he writes what lies behind it; and what he finds there is not horror itself but what a person is still giving at the very last.

Slow Motion Three · When the Phone Rings

Stalin's phone call is a historical event written as a bodily reflex. Before it rings, Grossman spends many pages on Shtrum's isolation: no one calls, footsteps on the stairs make his heart jump, so the ring itself lands as a shock.

Then, over the following chapters, Grossman lets the miracle slowly go bad. Everyone is kind to him now; he starts to enjoy it, and catches himself making excuses, in his head, for the people who were attacking him yesterday. The most frightening thing about this stretch is that the reader is relieved too. We are glad along with Shtrum, which is why, when the letter is put in front of him, we suddenly understand what we were glad about. Persecution could not break him. Favour did.

The Book's Core Device: A Mirror and a Definition

On the surface, Life and Fate is a Tolstoyan panorama: a family, a war, a hundred characters, long passages where the author stops to reflect. But its skeleton is two things.

  • The mirror structure. Soviet and German scenes come in pairs: the concentration camp and the labour camp, Liss's night talk and the Lubyanka interrogation, German antisemitism and Soviet antisemitism, one report and another. Grossman never says outright, "These are the same." He just hangs the two photographs side by side. The person who does say it is Liss — an SS officer. The most dangerous conclusion is handed to the least trustworthy speaker, so the reader can neither simply agree nor simply dismiss it.
  • A definition of freedom. The book keeps returning to one idea: what makes a person human is something in them the state cannot take — not political rights, but the private, unrepeatable part. In one reflective passage Grossman says, in effect, that life itself is freedom; the totalitarian state wants to make people into interchangeable parts, and human resistance is the insistence on being different. Victory in this novel isn't measured along the front. Grekov's house loses its battle and is the freest place in the book; Shtrum wins everything back and, at that moment, is at his least free.

It needs saying plainly: "Nazism and Stalinism are two sides of one mirror" is this novel's literary argument, not a historian's conclusion. How to compare the two regimes has long been fiercely debated by historians — the terms of comparison, the difference between death camps and labour camps, the difference in ideological aims — and none of it reduces to a sentence. Grossman's achievement was to be the first, inside a country that forbade the comparison, to set them side by side. Nor does he let his characters simply accept Liss: Mostovskoy is left speechless, but not persuaded.

What It Makes You Think About

One · Disobeying an order would save hundreds of lives — but what if your delay throws the whole operation off?

In front of Novikov is a timetable handed down from above, and an anti-tank line he can see with his own eyes, not yet destroyed.

The strongest case for disobeying: the order was written by people hundreds of miles away using information hours old, and the living men are right in front of you. A commander's job is not to keep to a timetable; it is to win, and to spend as few lives as possible doing it. If every general charged on schedule, the schedules would be rosters for a slaughterhouse. Novikov was right, and the outcome proved it.

The strongest case for obeying: you can see only your own stretch of the front. An encirclement is dozens of units meshing like clockwork; your eight minutes may be eight minutes in which the next corps has its flank exposed. You can afford to disobey precisely because everyone else is keeping time. Novikov's gamble paid off, but if everyone believed they could see the whole picture, there would be no whole picture.

Grossman is plainly on Novikov's side, but he has Getmanov's report follow right behind — in that system, even the right thing has a price. Have you ever been told "the process says ship it now" when you knew it wasn't ready? Did you hold it back?

Two · You've already been brave once. Do you owe anyone a second time?

When Shtrum refused to recant, he had nothing. When he is asked to sign, he has a laboratory, students, a theory that might change physics, and a family that will go down with him.

The strongest case for signing: the doctors' fate won't change for want of one signature; your refusal will save no one and will only ruin you and the people around you. Your theory is useful to humanity; your students need to eat. No one is obliged to sacrifice everything for an empty gesture. Shtrum has already proved he is no coward — last time, refusing meant something. This time it doesn't.

The strongest case for refusing: a totalitarian state never runs on one person's signature; it runs on everyone thinking "mine doesn't matter." The whole point of this letter is that it needs "distinguished scientists" — it needs exactly his kind of respectability. His signature isn't an empty gesture; it is the fuel the machine actually wants. And past courage can't be drawn on in advance: if bravery could be banked, it wouldn't be bravery.

Where Grossman stands is clear: he has Shtrum think of his mother the moment he has signed. But he doesn't make him a villain; the chill here is a sympathetic chill. Have you ever been asked to co-sign a statement at work you didn't agree with? After you'd refused once, was the second time harder?

Three · Does a refusal that changes nothing mean anything?

Ikonnikov is sent to work on a building, learns that it is a gas chamber, and puts down his tools. The Germans shoot him. The next day, others carry on building.

The strongest case that it means nothing: the meaning of a moral act lies in its consequences, and this refusal had none — the gas chamber was finished, not one fewer person died, and one more did. Real resistance is staying alive, organising, waiting for the moment, as the underground Communists in the camp do. Martyrdom is a luxury; the conscience it comforts is the martyr's own.

The strongest case that it means everything: some acts matter precisely because they don't look at the consequences. If "someone else will do it anyway" counts as a reason to do it, every crime will find its hands. Ikonnikov shows that the machine is not irresistible; it needs every person to nod, and a person can refuse. And remember what the consequence-minded underground actually did with its realism: it sent Yershov to his death.

Grossman's answer lies in the structure: he has Ikonnikov's refusal and the woman's bread answer each other across hundreds of pages — two small acts that change nothing about the war, and the two most human things in the book. Have you ever refused to do something "someone else would do anyway"?

Four · Love one person, or stand by another who has fallen — which?

Zhenya loves Novikov and no longer loves Krymov. But Krymov is in the Lubyanka, and she may be the only person left in the world who will bring him a parcel.

The strongest case for staying with Novikov: pity isn't love, and putting pity in love's place is unfair to both men. She already left Krymov once, out of honesty with herself; going back now is guilt, not desire. Novikov has done nothing wrong and is abandoned anyway. And in those years, standing beside an "enemy of the people" could drag her down with him.

The strongest case for going back: in a country where everyone is rushing to disown the arrested, refusing to disown them is the rarest thing there is. Novikov will grieve, but he will live; without her, Krymov may have no reason left to.

Grossman doesn't judge her. He just makes the queue at that window long and cold, and lets you see her standing among women who have made the same choice not to disown. Has anyone you know gone back to care for someone they no longer loved, once that person was in trouble? Was that love?

Motifs: What Grossman Kept Writing About

Grossman kept returning to three places. The first is Berdychiv. His breakthrough story, "In the Town of Berdichev" (1934), is about a pregnant woman commissar in the Civil War who is billeted on a poor Jewish family, gives birth, and leaves the baby to go back to the fighting; back then he was writing about the revolution's call on a person. In Life and Fate the same town has become his mother's grave. For years after her death he kept writing letters to her; this novel is the longest of them.

The second is that a person can't be flattened. For a Just Cause was still written inside the Stalin-era frame, in which state, Party and collective are forces for good; in Life and Fate the same characters' centre of gravity shifts to the one irreplaceable thing in each individual. His unfinished late work Everything Flows goes further: a man back from the camps looks at the country, and the book describes the Ukrainian famine and takes direct aim at Lenin.

The third is kindness. He wrote about the dead in his Treblinka report, and in the essay "The Sistine Madonna" about how a painting, in the postwar world, reminds people what it means to be human. Life and Fate is the summit of this line: it trusts no capital-G Good, and stakes all its hope on small, reasonless kindnesses.

Controversy and Criticism

"The War and Peace of the twentieth century" is praise and criticism at once. Grossman plainly takes Tolstoy as his model: a family, a war, reflective chapters — on antisemitism, on obedience — set into the narrative. Some critics feel this makes the book old-fashioned, the cast too large to keep straight, the essays an interruption that can read like editorials; others think those essays, especially the one on antisemitism, are the book's backbone.

Not everyone accepts the "mirror." Some critics argue that placing Nazi Germany and Stalin's Soviet Union side by side blurs real differences between them — in ideological aims, in industrialised genocide. Others think it is exactly where Grossman was bravest, writing from inside the Soviet Union. Putting the argument in an SS officer's mouth makes it more complex as literature, but it hasn't settled the debate.

The author's own record. Grossman was not a dissident from the start: in the 1930s and '40s he was a successful Soviet writer, and For a Just Cause was heavily revised to conform on its way to print. According to his biographers, in early 1953, during the "Doctors' Plot," he too put his name to a related collective letter, and was ashamed of it afterwards; the chapter in which Shtrum signs is often read as the author's judgment on himself.

The text itself. The first Western edition of 1980 was based on incomplete microfilms, with gaps and misreadings in places; later editions corrected it gradually. English readers have mostly known the book through Robert Chandler's 1985 translation. In 2013 Russia's security service handed the manuscripts confiscated in 1961 over to the state literary archive.

Crossovers

  • Social psychology · Milgram's obedience experiments: most subjects raised the "shocks" all the way to the top, because each step was only a little more than the last. Shtrum slides from refusal to signature down the same staircase. The difference: Milgram's subjects faced a man in a lab coat; Shtrum faces a state.
  • Political philosophy · Arendt's "banality of evil": Arendt (1963) argued that the people who carry out mass murder are often thoughtless bureaucrats, not demons; a few years earlier Grossman had already given Eichmann a brief appearance, as a man of efficiency. The difference: she probes an absence of thought, he an absence of kindness.
  • Chinese philosophy · Mencius on compassion: Mencius said that anyone who sees a child about to fall into a well reaches out without thinking — not to win the parents' favour, not for reputation. Ikonnikov's reasonless kindness has the same shape: it happens before calculation. The difference: Mencius wants that sprout of goodness "extended" into a whole ethics and politics; Ikonnikov holds that goodness, once extended into a system, starts to kill.
  • Political philosophy · Havel's greengrocer: in "The Power of the Powerless" (1978), a shopkeeper puts "Workers of the world, unite!" in his window just to avoid trouble, and the regime lives on millions of such signs. Shtrum's signature is one of them. The difference: Havel says taking the sign down is where living in truth begins; Grossman never writes that step.

Ten Lines to Take Away

① A mother about to die said one word to her son: live. This book spends hundreds of thousands of words explaining it.

② Two kinds of barbed wire, German and Soviet, and Grossman writes them in the same hand.

③ The most dangerous line is given to an SS officer: when you look at us, you see your own face.

④ A house about to collapse had no ranks; it was the freest place in the book, and the first to be flattened.
