The Little Prince

Le Petit Prince · Antoine de Saint-Exupéry · published in New York, April 1943

Deep in the Sahara, an aircraft with a broken engine, a thousand miles from any inhabited place. The pilot, asleep on the sand, is woken by an extremely small voice. The voice says: please, draw me a sheep.
He draws one. Rejected — that one looks ill. Another. Rejected — that is a ram, it has horns. A third is too old. Losing patience, he scribbles a box with three holes in it and says the sheep is inside.
The child bends over the paper for a long moment, and his face lights up: yes, that is exactly the one I wanted.

In one breath

A child who lives on an asteroid barely bigger than a house runs away from home after a quarrel with the only rose he has; he passes six planets occupied by grown-ups, lands on Earth, and learns from a fox who asks to be tamed that it is the time he wasted on his flower that makes his flower matter; so he makes an arrangement with a snake, leaves his body lying on the sand and goes home — and the pilot who hears the whole story still cannot answer, years later as he writes the book, the one question that counts: did the sheep eat the flower or not?

Coordinates

Antoine de Saint-Exupéry (1900–1944) did not become a writer and then take up flying. It went the other way. He was one of the first airmail pilots, flying the Toulouse–Dakar run and the night mail over the Andes, and in 1935, attempting a record flight, he put his plane into the Libyan desert and survived several days on almost no water with his mechanic before a Bedouin on a camel found them. The aircraft broken down in the sand at the start of The Little Prince is not an invention.

The book was written in the United States in 1942–43. France had fallen; he was an exile in New York, on bad terms with the Gaullists and the Vichy camp alike. Le Petit Prince appeared in New York in April 1943 in French and in English at once, with the author's own watercolours; France itself did not see it until 1946. On 31 July 1944 he took off from Corsica on a reconnaissance flight and did not come back; the wreck of his P-38 was only confirmed off Marseille early in this century.

Its slot in literary history is an odd one: it looks like a children's book, but it is built like a fable — a moral device assembled from the fewest possible strokes, which then refuses to give you the answer. The dedication says so outright. He dedicated the book to his grown friend Léon Werth, a Jewish man then hungry and cold in occupied France, and added: to Léon Werth, when he was a little boy. This is not a book posted from childhood to childhood. It is a book posted out of the war in 1943.

The story

Act one · A hat, a boa, and a sheep in a box

At six the narrator drew a picture: a boa constrictor swallowing an elephant whole, bulging into a lump. He showed it to the grown-ups and asked whether it frightened them. They said, why would anyone be frightened of a hat. So he drew a second version, a cross-section, with the elephant visible inside the snake. The grown-ups advised him to put drawing aside and get on with geography, history, arithmetic and grammar. Seed ①

So he learned to fly aeroplanes — a trade that requires arithmetic. For decades afterwards, whenever he met an adult who seemed clear-sighted, he tried the "hat" on them, and every single time the answer came back: that is a hat. So he would switch to bridge, golf, politics and neckties, and the adult would be delighted to have met such a sensible man. That is how he lived, sensibly and alone, until six years ago, when his engine failed over the Sahara.

He had drinking water for eight days. He slept the first night on the sand, and at first light a very small voice woke him: a small person with golden hair, asking for a sheep. In the least conversational situation imaginable, the book delivers the least reasonable request imaginable — and the rule of the whole book is set right there: it never explains how anything came to be. It simply happens.

All three sheep come back rejected. On the fourth attempt he loses patience, draws a box, and says the sheep is inside. The child peers in and says: that is exactly the one I wanted. Payoff ① ↑What makes this beautiful is how quietly it lands: the drawing that the entire world took for a hat has waited two chapters for the one reader who can see the sheep in the box. A cardboard box sorts this book's readership in a single stroke — either you see the sheep or you see the box. Everything said later is an expansion of that one moment.

Act two · B-612: three volcanoes, baobabs, and a flower with a cough

Bit by bit he gets the child's history out of him: an asteroid catalogued B-612, no bigger than a house. The number carries a small aside — it was spotted in 1909 by a Turkish astronomer who presented his discovery to an international congress in Turkish dress and was believed by nobody; in 1920 he gave the same demonstration in a European suit and the room accepted it. The first time the book names the defect of grown-ups, it does not preach. It tells a joke.

The planet has three volcanoes that come up to his knee, two active and one extinct, useful for heating breakfast; he rakes them out every week, the dead one included — "one never knows." It also has baobabs, which are a menace: the seeds are invisible in the soil, and once they take hold the roots split the planet apart. So every morning after washing, he goes out and pulls the new shoots, and misses none. On this absurdly small planet, the whole of morality is: pull up daily what has to be pulled up.

He also loves sunsets. The planet is so small that moving his chair a few steps gives him another one. He says that one day he watched the sun go down forty-three times — some editions say forty-four. Then he adds: you know, when a person is very sad, they come to love sunsets.

Later, a seed blew in from somewhere and sprouted. It grew slowly and refused to appear half-finished, choosing its colours behind the bud, arranging its petals, and on the morning it finally opened — at exactly sunrise — it yawned and said it was not properly done yet. She was a rose. She had four thorns and called them claws, and said she was not afraid of tigers. Seed ②She objected to draughts and wanted a screen; she was cold at night and wanted a glass globe; she coughed, and the cough arrived, more often than not, exactly when pity was required.

He watered her, covered her, sheltered her, did every one of it, and felt worse and worse. What he later tells the pilot is the cruellest self-accusation in the book: he was too young then to know how to love her — he listened to what she said instead of watching what she did. Her awkward demands were her way of putting perfume on his life, and all he retained were the demands.

He decides to leave. Before going he gives the planet a last cleaning: the volcanoes raked out, the baobab shoots pulled, the flower watered one final time. He tries to put the glass globe over her and she refuses — put that ridiculous thing away, I have my four claws. She does not let him see her cry. The first time she asks for nothing is the moment he has already decided not to stay.

Act three · Six planets: a taxonomy of grown-ups

He leaves with a migration of wild birds, calling on the way at six asteroids numbered 325 to 330. One grown-up each, each sealed inside his own loop.

The king rules everything, the stars and the sunsets included, but he is a reasonable man: he issues only orders that are certain to be obeyed. You want a sunset? You wait for the favourable moment, at dusk. There is one old rat on the planet; he proposes to make the prince minister of justice, to sentence the rat to death and then pardon it, since there is only the one and it must be used sparingly. An authority that issues only orders guaranteed to be carried out can never be disobeyed — the shortest and most poisonous piece of political science in the book.

The vain man wears a hat built for lifting in acknowledgement, and hears nothing that is not praise: the prince claps, the hat comes off; claps again, off again. After five minutes the prince finds the game monotonous and leaves. The drunkard sits among empty bottles and full ones. Why do you drink? To forget. Forget what? That I am ashamed. Ashamed of what? Of drinking — three exchanges, a circle with its own tail in its mouth.

The businessman is busy counting stars; he has reached five hundred and one million, six hundred twenty-two thousand, seven hundred thirty-one, and they are his, because he thought of it first. He writes the number on a slip, locks it in a bank, and goes on counting. The prince says: I own a flower and I water her every day; I own three volcanoes and I rake them out every week — I am of some use to my flower and my volcanoes, and you are of no use to the stars. It is the book's most frontal attack on ownership, and it works by bookkeeping rather than sentiment.

The lamplighter lives on a planet that turns once a minute, so he must light the lamp and put it out every minute, with no time even to breathe. The rule never changed; the planet simply spun faster while the order stayed word for word the same. He is the one man the prince respects: he is busy with something other than himself. The prince would like him as a friend, but the planet is too small for two.

The geographer sits at a great book and never leaves his desk; fieldwork is the explorer's business. The prince mentions his flower, and the old man shakes his head: we do not record flowers, flowers are ephemeral. Seed ③It is the first time the prince hears that word applied to her, and something turns over in him: I left her there alone. On his way out the geographer recommends Earth, which has a good reputation.

Act four · Earth: a snake, five thousand roses, and a fox who asks to be tamed

He comes down in the African desert and meets nobody at all, only a snake. The snake talks in riddles: I can take you farther than any ship; whomever I touch, I send back to the earth he came from. It adds that if one day he misses his planet too much, it can help. Seed ④The only actual weapon in the book is laid on the table halfway through, and announces its own use.

Then he comes to a garden and stops dead. Five thousand roses, every one identical to his. He lies down in the grass and cries: he had believed he possessed the only flower of its kind in the universe, and it turns out to be an ordinary rose — that, plus three knee-high volcanoes, one of them out.

The fox appears at exactly this moment.

The prince asks it to play; it says it cannot, it has not been tamed. What does that mean? The fox supplies a definition, and not a mood but a mechanism: to tame means to create ties — to me you are still just a boy like a hundred thousand other boys, and to you I am a fox like a hundred thousand other foxes; but if you tame me, we shall need one another, and you will be unique in all the world to me, as I shall be to you.

Then it grounds the tie in a physical object: I do not eat bread, wheat is of no use to me, wheatfields say nothing to me, and that is sad. But your hair is golden — once you have tamed me, the colour of the wheat will bring you back to me, and I shall love the sound of the wind in the wheat.

How does one tame? With patience. Sit a little way off, come slightly nearer each day, and say little, because language is a source of misunderstandings. And keep a rite: come at the same hour every day. If you come at four in the afternoon, I shall begin to be happy at three; but if you come at any hour at all, I shall never know when to dress my heart.

On the day of parting the fox says it is going to cry. The prince says: you are the one who wanted to be tamed, and you have gained nothing by it. The fox says it has gained something — the colour of the wheat. Then it sends him back to look at the five thousand roses once more. He goes, and tells them: you are beautiful, but you are empty; nobody would die for you. My rose alone matters more than all five thousand of you, because I watered her, put her under a globe, sheltered her from the wind, killed caterpillars for her (all but the two or three saved to become butterflies), because I listened to her complaining, and to her boasting, and sometimes to her saying nothing at all. Because she is my rose.

The fox's secret is one sentence:

"What is essential is invisible to the eye; one sees clearly only with the heart."— Chapter 21, the fox (rendered here from the French)

Then it adds the footnote that is the actual centre of gravity of the book:

"It is the time you have wasted on your rose that makes your rose so important."— Chapter 21, the fox (rendered here from the French)

And finally the line that falls like a verdict: you become responsible, forever, for what you have tamed. You are responsible for your rose. Payoff ③ ↑The join here is exact. The geographer called her ephemeral in order to strike her from the ledger; the fox turns the same fact over — precisely because she will die, precisely because the hours you put into her are unrecoverable, she is the only one. One word, "ephemeral": one man uses it to excuse himself, the other to bind himself.

Two smaller encounters follow. The railway switchman sorts thousands of travellers left and right while lit trains thunder past, and nobody aboard knows what they are looking for; only the children press their faces to the glass. The pill merchant sells a thirst-quenching tablet, one a week and you need never drink again, a saving experts have calculated at fifty-three minutes; the prince says that if he had fifty-three minutes to spend, he would walk very slowly towards a spring.

Act five · The well, and falling the way a tree falls

The story returns to the desert. It is the eighth day since the engine failed, and the pilot, having heard out the story of the pill merchant, has drunk his last drop of water. Two lines — a child's past and a grown man's deadline — become one line on this day.

They decide to go looking for a well. Looking for a well in a desert a thousand miles from anywhere is absurd, and they go anyway. At nightfall the prince sits down and says: what makes the desert beautiful is that somewhere it hides a well. The pilot suddenly understands why the old house of his childhood held him — a treasure was said to be buried in it, never found by anyone, and the whole house was enchanted by it. Houses, stars, deserts: what makes them beautiful is invisible.

At daybreak they find the well — and not the sort of well a desert should have: it has a pulley, a rope and a bucket, like a well in a village. The pilot hauls, and the old pulley groans the way a weathervane groans after a long sleep. He lifts the bucket to the child's lips. The water does not slake thirst; it is something else: it was born of the walk under the stars, of the arms on the rope, and it is like a gift, like a festival. The book is famously sparing with its abstractions, and lavishly concrete about this one well — because here, for the first time, the fox's sentence is not a maxim but a thing you can drink.

After drinking, the prince begins to settle his affairs. He reminds the pilot: you promised me a muzzle for my sheep. And he says: tomorrow it will be a year to the day since I came down, and the place is near here.

The next night the pilot, his engine repaired, hurries back and finds the child sitting on an old stone wall, talking to something at its foot: tonight, then — the same place? The pilot looks and sees a yellow snake reared against the stones, the kind that finishes a man in thirty seconds. Payoff ④ ↑By the time he has his revolver out, the snake has poured itself away into the sand like water.

The child's face is white as snow. He explains the arrangement: the body is too heavy to carry, and to get back to his planet he must leave the shell here. It will look as though I am dying, and it will not be true — do not come, it would hurt you. It is not like dying, more like shedding an old skin.

He gives the pilot a present. You will look up at the stars, and because I shall be living on one of them and you will not know which, it will be as if all of them were laughing — other people have ordinary stars, and you will have stars that can laugh. As if I had given you five hundred million little bells.

That night he walks to the wall. Nothing can be seen; there is only a flash of yellow at his ankle. He stands still for a moment and does not cry out.

"He fell as gently as a tree falls. Because of the sand, there was not even a sound."— Chapter 26, the ending (rendered here from the French)

In the morning the pilot goes back for the body and does not find it. He does not write death, and he does not write survival. He writes only that the body was gone.

At the end the pilot draws a picture: a stretch of sand, a horizon line, one star above it. He says it is the loveliest and the saddest landscape in the world, and he is drawing it again so that you will know it: if you ever pass that way and meet a child with golden hair who laughs and will not answer questions, please write and tell me.

And then the question he has not been able to put down for six years. He drew the little prince a muzzle so that the sheep could not reach the flower — and he forgot to draw the leather strap that holds it on. Payoff ② ↑So the muzzle can never be fastened. So the sheep in the box may at this moment, a few hundred million kilometres away, be eating a flower armed with four thorns; or it may not. The child's question from the seventh chapter — do sheep eat flowers? — is left standing at the end of the book, not answered by so much as a word. Look up at the sky, the pilot says, and ask yourselves whether the flower has been eaten or not, and you will see how everything changes. And no grown-up will ever understand why this matters so much.

Put the five acts together and the fuse turns out to be a single one, so short it is almost embarrassing to say aloud: a child had a quarrel with a flower and ran away from home. Everything afterwards is the blast wave of that quarrel — the six planets are the long way round, the geographer's word is his first regret, the five thousand roses are his supposed disillusionment, the fox is the explanation he was looking for, the well is that explanation made drinkable, and the snake is the road back. In all that universe he is only looking for one sentence he never managed to say.

Three scenes in slow motion

One · "Draw me a sheep": every explanation deleted

Run it again: the plane is broken, there is water for eight days, a man is asleep on sand. At first light a small person is standing there, and his opening line is not "help" and not "who are you" but a request: please, draw me a sheep. The pilot jumps up rubbing his eyes and looks around — he is a thousand miles from any inhabited place, and this child looks neither lost, nor exhausted, nor dying of thirst.

The craft of the scene is entirely in the refusal to explain. Another writer would spend two pages here: where he came from, why he isn't frightened. Saint-Exupéry gives none of it. He writes only the pilot's reaction and leaves the astonishment for the reader to carry. Better still is the sheer inappropriateness of the request — making the most useless demand imaginable in a life-or-death situation is a way of announcing, on the spot, that the measure used here is not the one you brought with you.

Two · Five thousand roses: break the reader first, then give the antidote

The ordering here is worth studying on its own. The prince sees the garden first, collapses first, lies in the grass and cries first — the author does not let the fox arrive early. Had the fox finished its lecture on taming and then walked him over to the garden, the five thousand roses would be a worked example. Reversed, with the wound opened first and the dressing after, the fox's sentence becomes something that stops bleeding.

And the reason for the tears is written cruelly: he is not crying over lost love, he is crying over lost standing — he had believed he owned the only flower in the universe, which made him somebody; the audit now shows one ordinary rose and three knee-high volcanoes, one of them out. That is vanity, a child's vanity, and the author puts it down without flinching. Because that layer is admitted first, the fox's move — shifting "unique" from scarcity to investment — is not consolation. It is a correction.

Look, finally, at what he says when he returns to the garden: it is all verbs — watered, covered, sheltered, killed caterpillars for, listened to her complaining, listened to her boasting, listened to her silence. Not one adjective is spent praising her. To show that something matters to you, don't describe how good it is; list what you have done for it.

Three · The last step: the camera looks away

The ending is all subtraction. The yellow flashes once; the fall makes no sound because of the sand; the word "death" never appears, replaced by "like shedding an old skin" and "the body is too heavy to carry"; in the morning the body is gone. This is not withholding an answer, it is removing one, so that "he went home" and "he died" remain simultaneously available and permanently undecidable. The narrator himself is kept out of the scene — don't come, it would hurt you — so the reader stands where he stands, at a distance, seeing a flash of light. Every argument this page has provoked in eighty years comes out of that deliberately vacated position.

The last, empty drawing is the final move: sand, a line, a star, and nothing else. It pushes the whole book back onto the picture that everybody took for a hat — whatever you can see in that blank space is what this book is for you.

The device at the centre of this book

One · "Taming" is a definition engine, not a feeling

The French apprivoiser is normally "to tame", a word about mastery over animals. The book's strangest move is to pull it out of that relation and redefine it as something mutual: to tame is to create ties, and so to become unique to one another. It has a cost (you will cry), a method (patience, few words, sit nearer each day), a rite (the same hour), and a consequence (permanent responsibility). The thing most easily written as fog — love — is here written as an executable procedure, one a ten-year-old can repeat back. The entire moral weight of the book rests on this single word, and it holds precisely because it is a word with a definition rather than a passage of lyricism.

Two · The six planets are a sorting machine

The six grown-ups are not six characters but six ways of sealing yourself into a loop: authority issues only orders that cannot fail, vanity hears only praise, the drunkard drinks to forget his drinking, the businessman counts what he can never use, the geographer records what he will never go and see. Five loops and one exception — the lamplighter is equally absurd, but he is busy with something outside himself. The machine lets the satire accumulate: by the third planet the reader has learned the sentence pattern, so the later ones can be shorter and shorter, and the saved space goes to the fox.

Three · A narrator who draws

The illustrations are not decoration, they are part of the plot: the boa is drawn by the narrator, the sheep and the box are drawn on the spot, the muzzle without its strap is his, and so is the final empty landscape. He is a man who was talked out of drawing by grown-ups, so every picture is a piece of his defence. Binding image and text into one causal chain is what makes the book formally singular — remove the drawings and half of it collapses. He also keeps admitting that he may be misremembering, that he draws badly, that he is afraid of becoming like the grown-ups: an unreliable narrator who doubts himself is exactly the narrator this book needs, since the subject is a man slowly losing his childhood eyesight.

What it forces you to think about

Question one
"It is the time you wasted on your rose that makes her so important" — is that the truth about love, or a beautiful way of saying sunk cost?

Take the objection at full strength first, because it is strong. Economics has a name for it: the sunk cost fallacy. Money and hours already spent and unrecoverable should not bear on what you decide now — yet people stay in a job that is hollowing them out, or a relationship that stopped being happy, because they have "put so many years in". By that standard the fox's line is dangerous: it converts "I paid" straight into "therefore it is worth it", which is the exact bolt that holds so many people in place. The fact of the five thousand roses is never refuted. The fox simply teaches him not to look at it.

Now take the fox at full strength. The sunk cost argument assumes that value is external and prior to you, and that your job is to estimate it correctly. But some values are not discovered, they are made — a relationship, a child, a craft, a city you have lived in for twenty years. They matter because the hours have grown into them; an identical rose is not the same rose. The fox does not say "your payments have made her objectively better". It says "your payments have made her yours". Pricing that class of thing as if substitutes existed is itself the miscalculation.

The hard part is the boundary. The book supplies no standard, only a usable question: are you staying because those years grew something in you, or only because you cannot bear to write off the bill? The first is the rose. The second is the businessman's ledger of stars.

Question two
"You become responsible, forever, for what you have tamed" — is that ethics, or a contract with no exit clause?

The fox goes in with its eyes open: it says plainly that it will cry when he leaves, and asks to be tamed anyway. That amounts to an unaccommodating claim — forming ties in the certain knowledge of being hurt is worth it. The case is strong: a life without taming is genuinely safer, and genuinely empty; among a hundred thousand identical foxes, not one belongs to anybody.

The other side is not light either. Taken literally, "forever" leaves responsibility without a boundary: a dog that lives fifteen years, a marriage that has become an obligation, parents growing old, a friend you no longer recognise. "You tamed it" can legitimately demand the rest of a person's life, and nothing in the formula says when the demand becomes excessive. Sharper still, the prince does not himself manage it: he tames the fox and then walks away without looking back. The verdict-like sentence is spoken by the character who has just been left — is it ethics, or the only thing the one left behind can say?

Back to daily life: nearly everyone is the fox and the prince at once. We want our "forever" honoured by others, and negotiable for ourselves. The book does not resolve the asymmetry. It only sets it out very clearly.

Question three
Is the book's verdict on grown-ups fair?

The position is unambiguous. Grown-ups, told about a new friend, never ask what his voice sounds like or whether he collects butterflies; they ask how old he is and what his father earns. Say "I saw a house of red brick with geraniums in the windows" and they feel nothing; say "a house worth a hundred thousand francs" and they cry out, how lovely. The satire is exact, and everyone nods.

But notice what happens in the instant of nodding: every reader automatically files himself on the side of those who have not yet become grown-ups. This is the book's most suspect feature — it offers an extremely comfortable self-identification at a cost low enough to require changing nothing. You can run your quarterly numbers and remain quite certain there is a little prince inside you. Kundera's term for the emotion enjoyed for its own sake is kitsch, and this is where serious readers' dislike of the book tends to be located.

In its defence: what it attacks is not adults but people left with a single measuring stick. The lamplighter is an adult; the pilot who earns his living by arithmetic is emphatically an adult — the ones the book absolves are those still busy with something outside themselves. The author was himself the most disciplined kind of professional, a man on an airmail line who knew perfectly well that the numbers must be run and the route kept. The target is not growing up. It is growing up into one way of pricing things.

Question four
Is that ending a homecoming, or a suicide written beautifully?

The literal facts are not escapable: a child arranges a time and place with a venomous snake a year in advance, sends his only friend away, walks out on the appointed night and is bitten at the ankle. Substitute any real-world instrument for the snake and the passage becomes hard to look at. The objection is specific: it makes leaving the body sound gentle, quiet, very nearly lovely — and the readers most easily persuaded by that beauty are the most fragile ones. "It will look as though I am dying, and it will not be true" is a consolation, and also exactly what anyone unwilling to face a loss most wants to hear.

The other side deserves a full hearing. This is a fable, and the snake is one of its devices rather than a set of instructions. What it offers is not "death is a release" but something much older: the body is a shell, distance is not an ending, and what you have loved will laugh at you when you look up. It was written in 1943, for a friend going hungry in an occupied country, in a year when people failed to come back every day; when death is the ordinary condition, what a book can do is not to pretend otherwise. Nor does it hide the cost: for the rest of his life the pilot listens for a crack in that laughter, and goes on worrying about the flower. Nobody in the book is made lighter by this homecoming.

And in the most ordinary form of all: how do you tell a child that someone is gone? The truth may be too hard; "he has gone somewhere far away" may be a kindly lie. The book takes a third road — it declines to rule, points at the stars, and admits that it does not know either. Whether that road is the right one has been argued for over eighty years, and it is precisely that unsettled position that keeps the book on the children's shelf and the adult shelf at the same time.

The obsession Saint-Exupéry keeps returning to

Line his books up and the same proposition recurs: a person is made by what he is responsible for, not by what he owns.

In Night Flight (Vol de nuit, 1931), Rivière, who runs the airline, has to send men out on flights they may not survive if the night mail is to exist at all, and the book is his interrogation of his own hardness. Wind, Sand and Stars (Terre des hommes, 1939) tells how his colleague Guillaumet walked out of the Andes, sustained not by an instinct for survival but by the very concrete fact that his family needed a body they could claim. Flight to Arras (Pilote de guerre, 1942) turns on a near-certainly useless reconnaissance mission in 1940 and asks why one flies it anyway.

The Little Prince is the smallest and softest of them and says the same thing: what makes a thing yours is the time you spend on it and the responsibility you carry for it. One more note: the rose is commonly read as his wife Consuelo — a Salvadoran woman, volatile, much apart from him. The author never said so, but between the biographical record and the coughing, the complaints and the awkward pride in the text, the reading has become close to consensus.

Criticism, honestly

The book is quoted so relentlessly that its real problems get skipped. Plainly, then:

  • It has been quotation-mined past recognition. "What is essential is invisible to the eye" turns up on mugs and in advertising copy, but in the book it comes with a precondition — you must first have wasted a great deal of time on something. Strip the precondition and it is cheap uplift.
  • The rose raises a gender objection. The only female figure of any weight is vain, petulant, coughs to extract sympathy, and her value is ultimately conferred by what the male protagonist has done for her. The sympathetic reading points out that the prince's own childishness and vanity are handled just as unsparingly, and that the rose has the greater dignity in the farewell scene; but the structure in which her importance is his to decide is undeniably there.
  • The anti-grown-up posture invites the charge of kitsch. See question three: it hands readers moral superiority at almost no cost. This is the most serious literary objection to the book.
  • The ending is ethically contested. Whether a suicide-shaped event written as a homecoming belongs in a book circulated to children has never stopped being argued; others reply that its refusal to avoid loss is exactly why it became the place where many children first thought seriously about death.
  • Translations differ enormously. Apprivoiser has been rendered as tame, domesticate and other things; in the fox's line about time, the French verb is perdu — lost, squandered — which some English versions give as "wasted" and others as "spent", one of them rueful and the other an investment. Readers of different translations are not, strictly, discussing the same book.

Ten sentences to take away

① It is not a book about innocence, it is a book about responsibility — it just happens to use a child as its spokesman.

② The opening drawing that everyone reads as a hat is a filter: from chapter two, the readership is sorted into two kinds.

③ A sheep inside a box satisfies where three lifelike sheep do not, because the part you can see is the part you supplied.

④ Taming is not a sentiment but a practice: patience, few words, the same hour every day.

⑤ Five thousand roses never refute his rose; they change the accounting — scarcity stops counting, spent time starts.

⑥ The geographer calls flowers ephemeral and strikes them out; the fox says that because they are ephemeral you are bound to them. One word, two lives.

⑦ Each grown-up spins on his own planet and none of them stops to look at anyone else; the only admirable one is busy with something outside himself.

⑧ What makes the desert beautiful is that it hides a well — the line that turns the whole argument into something you can drink.

⑨ The ending writes neither death nor survival, only that the body was gone; eighty years of argument come from that deliberate blank.

⑩ What stays with you is not the stars but the strap he forgot to draw — a book about responsibility ending on an unfinished promise.
