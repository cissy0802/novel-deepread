Novels, Deep-Read · Vladimir Nabokov · No.65

Lolita

Lolita · Vladimir Nabokov · first published Paris 1955 · American edition 1958

中文 →

An afternoon in a small New England town. A man who has just looked over a house and intends to leave at once is steered out to the back garden to admire the owner's flower beds.
A mat is spread on the lawn. A twelve-year-old girl is kneeling on it in sunglasses, lifting her face. There is a patch of peeling sunburn on her shoulder and a scabbed graze on one knee.
He will write later that in that instant a girl who died on Corfu twenty-four years earlier came back.
He takes the room that same day. The remaining three hundred pages are his explanation.

This page contains complete spoilers — how Charlotte dies, what lie he tells the girl three days after the hotel, who is following them, what she says to him at seventeen, and the thing the fake foreword tells you on page one and you almost certainly skimmed: Dolores Haze does not live past seventeen. Without knowing what she finally refuses him, the book reads as a love story — which is exactly how a great many people have read it for seventy years. If you want the suspense, read the novel first; this page will wait.

In one sentence

A forty-year-old European teacher of literature lodges with a widow and her twelve-year-old daughter in an American town and marries the mother in order to stay near the child; the mother reads his diary, runs into the road and is killed by a car, and he collects the newly orphaned girl as her stepfather and sole guardian, takes her onto the highway, and for two years controls her with motels, pocket money and the fact that she has nowhere else to go; at fourteen she is taken from him by another man with the same appetite; at seventeen, pregnant and broke, she writes asking him for money, and he comes, hands her four thousand dollars, begs her to leave with him, and she says no; he drives off and murders the other man, writes these three hundred pages in jail, and dies of a coronary a few months later — and she dies that same year, on Christmas Day, in childbirth. The book is that defence document itself, every word of it written by him, including the title on the cover: Lolita is his nickname for her.

Coordinates

Vladimir Nabokov (1899–1977) was born in St Petersburg into an immensely rich, emphatically liberal aristocratic family that spoke three languages at home; he learned to read English before Russian. The revolution drove the family into exile, and in 1922 his father, a Constitutional Democrat politician, was shot at a Berlin meeting while shielding a colleague. Nabokov finished his degree at Cambridge, wrote nine Russian novels under the pen name Sirin in the émigré circles of Berlin and Paris, and in 1940 fled to America with his Jewish wife Véra. He taught European literature for nearly two decades at Wellesley and Cornell — and was a professional-grade lepidopterist who curated butterflies at Harvard's Museum of Comparative Zoology. An exile's lost paradise plus a taxonomist's eye: those two things underlie everything he wrote.

Most of Lolita was written on butterfly-collecting road trips across the American West (he did not drive; Véra drove the whole way). Twice he tried to burn the manuscript and twice she stopped him. American publishers turned it down in series, and in 1955 it appeared from Olympia Press, a small house in Paris. What lit the fuse was Graham Greene, who named it one of the three best books of the year in a London paper; the editor of a rival paper promptly declared it the filthiest book he had ever read, the two of them fought it out in print, and the novel was famous. The 1958 Putnam edition was a phenomenal bestseller; Nabokov never had to teach again, and in 1961 he moved to a lakeside hotel in Montreux, Switzerland, where he lived until his death.

It occupies three distinct places in literary history. First, it is the most famous experiment in unreliable narration in English — the whole book is the suspect's own account, and the reader is locked inside his diction. Second, it is the most beautiful formal game played before postmodernism arrived: puns, anagrams, false names, a dense weave of allusion. Third, it is the most misread novel of the twentieth century, and the scale of the misreading is itself now a cultural fact: the title has become an adjective for a girl child, cover after cover has printed a teenager's legs, and the author explicitly asked that no girl appear on the cover.

The story

Act One · The Riviera and Paris: a man who spent his life explaining himself

The novel does not begin with Humbert. It begins with a fake foreword. An editor signing himself John Ray, Jr., Ph.D. explains that the author of the manuscript to follow — "Humbert Humbert," a pseudonym — died in legal custody of a thrombosis on 16 November 1952, days before his trial. Ray also disposes of several other lives in passing, among them this: "Mrs. Richard F. Schiller" died in childbirth on Christmas Day 1952, in Gray Star, a remote settlement in the far Northwest.seed ① No first-time reader stops here. You do not yet know whose name that is.

Then Humbert speaks, and his first line is the most famous in the book:

"Lolita, light of my life, fire of my loins."— Lolita, Part One, opening of chapter 1

And in the same chapter he states the function of his own prose outright: "You can always count on a murderer for a fancy prose style." It is the one wholly honest sentence in the book, and it is placed at the front, like a warning sign nailed to a trap — and because the sign is there, many readers assume they cannot fall in.

He was born in Paris in 1910, son of a Swiss hotelier; his mother died when he was three. He disposes of her death in a two-word parenthesis: "picnic, lightning" (Part One, chapter 2) — a mother killed by lightning, turned into an elegant phrase. That trick of using style to skate over the place that ought to hurt recurs for three hundred pages; it is only that later the pain being skated over stops being his.

At thirteen, in his father's hotel, he and a girl his own age named Annabel Leigh became obsessed with each other, were interrupted by adults every time, and never finished anything; a few months later she died of typhus.seed ② Humbert hangs his entire subsequent life on her: he says he has been stranded in that summer ever since, that he responds only to a particular kind of girl between nine and fourteen, for whom he coins the word nymphet, complete with a solemn set of identifying criteria. Note that this taxonomy is his private invention — not one other character in the novel ever uses the word. It is page one of a legal defence, not a diagnosis from any real field. The name Annabel Leigh is not idle either: it comes straight from Poe's poem, and Poe married his thirteen-year-old cousin. The first witness Humbert calls for himself is a poet.

As an adult he taught literature in Paris and married a woman named Valeria — because, he admits, she was faintly girlish; she left him for a White Russian taxi driver. He spent time in sanatoria and enjoyed lying to the doctors. During the war he went to America, and in 1947 he planned to spend the summer writing in a New England town called Ramsdale.

Act Two · Ramsdale, 342 Lawn Street: one look, and a summer's diary

The house he was promised burns down, and an intermediary sends him to the newly widowed Charlotte Haze at 342 Lawn Street.seed ③ He walks through it, finds the vulgarity unbearable, and is about to leave when Charlotte steers him out to see the garden — where there is a twelve-year-old girl on the lawn. He takes the room that day.

The hundred pages that follow are the lightest, funniest and, in retrospect, coldest stretch of the book: a middle-aged man's summer diary inside a small house. He logs every passage through a doorway, every outfit, every piece of backchat; he gives her mother nicknames and mocks her décor, her French, her magazines; on the living-room sofa, while the girl is singing, he does something, and then spends two ornate pages presenting it as a private victory in which nobody was harmed. That passage is the instruction manual for the whole novel: he never conceals what he did — he merely makes sure that while you are reading it you have no attention left for judgement.

Dolores — her mother calls her Lo, he calls her Lolita — is an ordinary American kid who chews gum, collects film-star photographs and has a filthy temper. This matters: the novel never writes her as a precocious sprite. She is vulgar, likeable, rude, and exactly the age at which a person has the least defence. The sprite is entirely in his adjectives.

Charlotte packs her daughter off to camp and leaves Humbert a letter declaring herself: marry me or move out today. He marries her, and within a few pages of the wedding is already planning how to be rid of her — he considers drowning her in the lake, takes her out to it, and finds he cannot do it. Not out of conscience: because someone might see. From her he inherits one genuinely useful thing, the late Harold Haze's pistol, which he nicknames Chum and writes about almost tenderly.seed ④

Then the hinge. Charlotte forces his drawer, reads the diary, says almost nothing, writes three letters, and runs out of the house into the road with them — where a car swerving to avoid a dog kills her. Humbert does nothing at that moment. He stands there and lets the world finish the job for him. He later gives this kind of luck a name, McFate: every piece of good fortune becomes a character who arranges his itinerary, so that none of it looks like something he did.

Act Three · The Enchanted Hunters: that night, and three sentences three days later

He drives to the camp to collect her and does not tell her on the road that her mother is dead. He takes her first to a hotel called The Enchanted Hunters, where the desk has one room left, with a double bed, number 342.payoff ③ ↑ The same number as the house on Lawn Street; and by his own count, the motels they stayed in during the first year also came to something over three hundred. The figure appears three times in the book, each time attached to a worse thing: a house with a mother in it, a crime scene, a road without an end. Nabokov pins one number to those three objects to tell you that this is not three lives but one thing expanding.

That night he gives her a pill wheedled out of a doctor, expecting her to sleep like the dead; it does not work and she wakes several times. What happened in the morning he narrates like this: she proposed it, she had learned it at camp, she thought it was a game, he merely obliged. This is the passage to hold at arm's length, and the only place in the book where Humbert genuinely needs to be believed — because if she did not propose it, then he is a man who raped a twelve-year-old and not one sentence of the prose is any use to him. The novel hands you the material for a verdict: this is the defendant's own account, written for a jury after the fact, and he admits in the same chapter that he drugged her. He also leaves a worse sentence for later: for nearly every night of the next two years, once he pretended to be asleep, she cried.seed ⑤

On the hotel porch that night a strange middle-aged man sitting in the dark spoke to him and asked whether that was his daughter. Humbert brushed him off, and so did the reader.seed ⑥

Three days later, at the next stop, she starts asking where her mother is. He tells her: dead, the funeral is over. And then he writes the last sentence of Part One, in which the whole machinery of the book is visible: she cried all night, and in the morning she came to him, because she had nowhere else to go. He writes it as a sad fact. It is a progress report: he built that nowhere over the course of one summer.

Act Four · A year, a map: a crime written up as tourism

For a full year they drive a loop around America, staying in motels. This is the strangest part of the book's reputation: it doubles as the most precise, funniest and most vicious record of the late-1940s American roadside that exists — imitation-stone filling stations, Indian-themed gift shops, the world's largest something, restaurants with a moose head on the wall, that motel-room smell of disinfectant laid over the previous guest. A European professor of literature turns his full rhetorical apparatus on the vulgarity of a country; the reader laughs a great deal, and only slowly notices that this brilliant travelogue is also an operational route map, and that the reason for never staying anywhere long is that people who see you twice start asking questions.

The means of control are spelled out with no mystery at all. She has no money (the allowance is tied to favours). She has no relatives — he explains to her repeatedly that if he is arrested she goes to a reformatory. She has no fixed adult to appeal to, because everyone who meets them meets them once. The most frightening sentence in the book is not any description of a body; it is an accounting note he records quite calmly: she gradually learned to bargain — she discovered she could name her own price.

He also records what she is like: she plays tennis, and he spends several pages on her service — the one passage in the novel where he forgets he is defending himself, and it is clean, exact, almost merciful; she wants to act; with people her own age she has a lightness he can never enter; in the car she goes silent for hours. All of it comes from him, which is the cruellest fact here: everything we know about Dolores Haze is issued to us by the man who destroyed her.

Act Five · Beardsley and the second trip west: someone is following us

After a year they stop in a college town called Beardsley and she is enrolled in a private girls' school. The theme of this act is that his regime starts to leak: she wants to be in the school play, she meets girls and boys her own age, she lies about her piano lessons. He checks, finds she has skipped every lesson, and they have a row; she runs out of the house, and he finds her on a street corner using a public telephone. She comes back and announces she has decided: she wants to leave this town. He thinks he has won, because leaving is what he wants too. In fact the phone call was the verdict: she had already made an arrangement with someone else.

The play she is rehearsing is called The Enchanted Hunters, by a playwright named Clare Quilty with a co-author credited as Vivian Darkbloom — an anagram of Vladimir Nabokov, the author's signature hidden on his antagonist's work.payoff ⑥ ↑ Humbert finds the title vaguely familiar and fails to place it: it is the name of the hotel from 1947. Quilty is not an opponent who materialises halfway through the book. He was sitting on that porch that night, and he asked whether she was Humbert's daughter. And Dolores has known of him in this town for years — he is her family dentist's nephew, the sort of celebrity whose picture she had on her wall as a child. The play she is in, the lines she is learning, were written by the man who is waiting for her. The move is beautiful because it explains three things at once: why she suddenly insists on leaving, why he can never identify his pursuer, and the hardest fact in the story — her escape is not an escape into freedom but a transfer from one such man to another, and it was the best option available to her.

On the second trip west a car begins to follow them. In motel register after motel register Humbert reads the false names his pursuer leaves, a string of literary jokes at his expense; he copies them out, decodes them, goes half mad and learns nothing. Structurally this is the novel's harshest revenge: a man who lives by style is toyed with, in style, by someone who also has it. In Elphinstone she runs a high fever and is hospitalised; on the third day he comes for her and a nurse tells him she has already left — her uncle collected her.

He searches for three years, driving the route again alone, reading every register a second time. During those years he lives for a while with a middle-aged alcoholic named Rita — the most relaxed relationship in the book, because she is a woman who asks him no questions.

Act Six · Coalmont, seventeen: she says the name

In September 1952 a letter arrives in pencil, signed "Dolly (Mrs. Richard F. Schiller)."payoff ① ↑ A reader with a good memory sits bolt upright at that line: the name was in the foreword, followed by died in childbirth, Christmas Day 1952. Which means the three hundred pages in your hands have been a dead woman's effects since page one; she was dead before you started, and the author told you so, and you did not catch it. This is the finest piece of design in the novel, and what makes it fine is that it is not a twist — it is a fact you were given a chance to know, hidden inside an academic foreword so smug that you wanted to skip it.

She is seventeen, eight months pregnant, living in a place called Coalmont with a husband, Dick Schiller, a hard-of-hearing veteran who fixes cars; they are hoping to find work in Alaska. In the letter she is asking him for money.

He goes, with the pistol. She opens the door. What follows is the most important admission in the book: she is pale, heavy, out of shape, in glasses and slippers, carrying another man's child, and not one adjective from the nymphet apparatus is usable on this page — and he says he loves her; he says he would love her at fifty, he would love her if nothing were left of her but her eyes.payoff ② ↑ The taxonomy he hung on Annabel Leigh, the system that explained his entire life, is cancelled here by its own author: if he really responded only to girls of nine to fourteen, this page could not exist. It is the only time he says something that does not serve his defence, and the price of it is the defence itself.

He asks the question he has chased for three years: who was it. She says the name — Clare Quilty — and then tells him the rest quite calmly. She had arranged it with Quilty from Beardsley; she was in love with him then. He took her to a ranch called Duk Duk, where there were other adults and other children, and wanted her to take part in filming certain things; she refused, and he threw her out. After that she worked, and waited, and eventually married Dick. The tone she tells it in is the most painful thing in the book: no accusation, no tears, an old piece of bad luck. Of Humbert's own two years she says only that he took her childhood.

He puts four thousand dollars on the table — the money from the sale of her mother's house, which was always hers. He begs her to come away with him, anywhere. She says no. She says she would sooner go back to Quilty than go with him. It is the only point in the novel where she decides the plot, and she ends the book with one syllable. Then she asks whether he would like to stay for supper; he walks out and cries in the car for a long time.

The pistol inherited from her dead father is drawn one last time.payoff ④ ↑ He drives to Quilty's disordered mansion. The murder is the strangest passage in the book in tone: undignified, unsatisfying, a farce inside a nightmare. He reads Quilty a poem he has written; Quilty, with a gun on him, discusses literature, complains about the poem and offers a series of trades; the bullets go in and he is still talking, still climbing the stairs, still playing the piano. Nabokov writes the revenge as slapstick because it is slapstick: a man with this appetite kills another man with the same appetite, over a child neither of them wants. What he kills is not a criminal but another version of himself — a man who also knows literature, also writes plays, and took the same child by the same methods.

He drives back on the wrong side of the road and is stopped by police. In jail he writes this book. In the last pages he records one thing: not long after she vanished, he pulled the car over on a mountain road and heard, coming up from the mining town below, the noise of children playing — a blurred, gentle, indistinguishable mass of sound.payoff ⑤ ↑ He stood and listened a long time, and then understood what he had actually done: not that he made her cry for two years, but that one voice was missing from that concord, and he was the one who took it out. Three hundred pages of rhetoric go into the defence, and the verdict, when it comes, is a single acoustic image — more effective than any confession, because it is not a sentence but a gap. The last line of the book is a promise he makes her:

"and this is the only immortality you and I may share, my Lolita."— Lolita, closing words

It sounds like the deepest love in the book. It is one last act of possession: he has written her into something that will not die, and inside it she does not even have her own name — only the nickname he gave her.

Put the six acts together and the fuse is a braking car on a road in the summer of 1947. A driver swerves to avoid a dog and kills a middle-aged woman, and from that second a twelve-year-old has exactly one relative in law, and that relative is the only person who wants her. Everything before — the diary, the marriage, the hesitation at the lake — is preparation; everything after — the highways, the motels, the ledgers, the crying — is aftershock. But the novel is harsher than that: it holds that the real fuse is not an event but a sentence, "she had nowhere else to go." Humbert writes it as an unfortunate fact, and three hundred pages inadvertently prove it was a summer's work. A man can lay no hand on anyone, make no threats, lock no doors, and simply cause every exit to disappear one by one — and then call it fate in his memoirs.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · The look at the lawn: a crime written as a reunion in Eden

Play it again: a man steered out to a back garden, hard light, a mat, a girl in sunglasses lifting her face. When he writes that look he does not write desire. He writes the same child — the girl who died on a beach twenty-four years ago, back again behind a pair of sunglasses.

Why it has power and why it is dangerous are the same reason. Nabokov loads the weight of the moment onto reunion rather than predation, and reunion is one of the most legitimate emotions in literature. There is not one lewd word in the passage; there is loss, time, fate, an old debt repaid — the entire rhetorical set that readers are trained from childhood to sympathise with. Your first reaction is not alarm, it is being moved, and that is precisely the experience the book intends you to have. By the time you notice that you have just shared a forty-year-old man's look at a twelve-year-old, you have already shared it.

One more technical note: he puts the age, the peeling shoulder, the scabbed knee — the concretely childish details — right into the passage. He does not prettify her body; he prettifies his own feeling. Lyricism and evidence are compressed into the same sentence, and that is the basic craft of the book.

Two · The hotel morning: the one place he cannot avoid lying

Play it again: one room with a double bed, a child newly orphaned who does not know it, a sleeping pill that failed, a man pretending to sleep, a room before dawn. Then a piece of narration handled with extraordinary slipperiness: the act skipped, the time skipped, the subject of the sentence handed to her, the verb handed to a joke.

Its power lies in the fact that this is the load-bearing point of the book, and Nabokov deliberately makes it the least credible page in it. Humbert will admit to anything — the drugging, the scheming, the reformatory threats, the nightly crying. The single thing he goes vague about is who started it. Why would a man willing to confess everything turn slippery here? The answer is in plain view: none of the rest would convict him, and this would.

There is a rhythmic trick too: immediately after this chapter he starts describing scenery, roadside attractions, the Coke she wants — flushing the heaviest passage downstream under a mass of bright irrelevant detail. Not concealment: dilution. It is also why readers so often cannot remember what that night actually says on the page. It was drowned by two hundred pages of American highway, and that is the design.

Three · The doorway at Coalmont: the first time the prose lets her stand on her own

Play it again: the door of a shabby house, a pregnant seventeen-year-old opening it, glasses, slippers, unwashed hair, a flat voice. A man with poor hearing is fixing something inside. Four thousand dollars on the table. She says no.

The technique here is subtraction: the adjective density of the entire novel drops to nearly zero for a few pages. No puns, no French, no Poe; the sentences shorten and he begins to talk like an ordinary person. Nabokov uses the collapse of a style to accomplish something about content — that language existed to cover things, and when it can no longer cover them it simply withdraws.

The distribution of information changes too. For three hundred pages she has had no sustained speech; here she does, and everything she says points at one fact — she always had her own judgement and was never allowed to finish a sentence. She does not even hate him, which is worse than hatred: in her accounting those two years are a closed, unrecoverable loss, and at seventeen she has learned not to spend feeling on unrecoverable losses. The reason that "no" is the heaviest word in the book is that it is the first time she has the material conditions for it — a husband, a child coming, a ticket to Alaska, all of it poor. Freedom is not a state of enlightenment. It is having somewhere else to go.

Four · The sound on the mountain road: a verdict one line long

Play it again: a road above a valley, the car pulled over at the rail, a mining town below, autumn, school just out. The noise comes up the slope, nobody's voice in particular, like a sheet of struck metal. He stands and listens.

Why it works: every confession in the book up to this point is written in adjectives, and this one is written with an ear. Everything Humbert is good at — metaphor, pun, verse, allusion — is useless here; all he can do is report a fact, that one voice is missing from the sound. The point of view stays with him, and for once he can explain nothing; the information comes very late, long after the reader has stopped expecting him to speak plainly; and the rhythm has been all action — three years of searching, a murder, a jail — until it suddenly goes quiet. Nabokov hands the moral conclusion of the novel to an acoustic image, because "I am guilty" can be performed and an audible absence cannot.

It is also the book's answer to the tired question of whether he loved her. He did see her once, properly — not her face. He heard that she was not there.

The central device: a defence written by the defendant, plus a foreword that is fake in exactly the right way

The machine has four parts, and none of them is optional.

Part one: the style is the murder weapon. The book is a prison narrative addressed to an imagined jury, and Humbert is a professional man of letters. So the beauty of the language is not decoration, it is plot — he is bribing you with prose to your face, and you are accepting. This gives the book a distinctly ungracious property: the better a reader you are, the more easily you are hooked.

Part two: Dr John Ray's fake foreword. It opens with a stretch of pompous, moralising, thoroughly irritating academic prose announcing that the book has an ethical lesson — which produces one clear reaction in the reader: I do not need this man to tell me how to read. Its function is to make moral judgement look ridiculous before the novel starts, so that you enter the text with your defences dismantled by your own hand. And at the same time it states, flatly and honestly, the most important facts in the book — she is dead, he is dead, here are the dates — so that when you look back, what fooled you turns out to be your own decision not to take a foreword seriously.

Part three: the name. She is Dolores, from dolor, pain; at home she is Lo, on the school register Dolly. Lolita is his usage alone, as he says himself. Which makes the title a trap: every time you say Lolita you are using his word. Nabokov makes the reader an accomplice on the cover, which is the hardest piece of naming in twentieth-century fiction.

Part four: pattern. He was a taxonomist, and his novels are always full of repeating figures: three 342s, two Enchanted Hunters, a run of literary pseudonyms, a minor character whose name is the author's rearranged, an antagonist who mirrors the hero. These figures have a job. Humbert reads them as fate signalling to him; the reader should read them as evidence that someone is designing all this — and that someone is not fate but the author, who is not on his side at any point. In a book with no narratorial voice, the structure is the only narratorial voice there is.

One honest caveat: all of this is literary design, not psychology. The nymphet taxonomy and the causal chain from childhood wound to adult crime are Humbert's self-narration, not knowledge from any field — and the novel's own position is built into the structure: every time he invokes Annabel Leigh to explain himself, something harder to explain follows immediately after.

What it forces you to think about

Question one
Should a book that puts the most beautiful prose in the language at the service of the least forgivable act be written at all?

The case against, at full strength: this is not a book that depicts a crime, it is a book that makes one attractive, and attraction leaks. The evidence is not in the text, it is outside it — in most languages today Lolita means "a sexualised girl child," which is to say that seventy years of reception translated the book into Humbert's column. An author may declare that his protagonist is a hateful person, but what a work does in the world is not settled by the author's declarations. You can ask trained readers to withstand three hundred pages of gorgeous prose; you cannot ask everyone to, and the cost of failing falls on the group with the least say in the matter. A writer has the right to any subject, but "permitted" and "harmless" are two different claims, and merging them means charging the leakage to artistic freedom.

The case for, at greater strength: there is no way to make a person understand what it feels like to be fooled by someone charming except to let them be fooled once. The moral education in this book is not in what it says; it is in what happens to the reader — you laughed at his jokes, you were moved by his lyricism, on certain pages you genuinely forgot she was a child, and then you noticed that yourself. Any treatment that denounced Humbert from outside would let every reader stand safely on the correct side and learn nothing. As for what became of the word, that is the novel being proved right: people are bought by wording, and they do end up describing a victim in her abuser's vocabulary. Citing the diagnosis as the cause of the disease gets the direction of the arrow backwards.

Bring it home: the next time you listen to an extremely articulate person account for something they did — in a meeting room, an interview, a long email — how much of your judgement comes from the evidence and how much from their fluency? That is the faculty this book trains, and it chose an extreme specimen because mild specimens do not train it.

Question two
If a victim was never allowed to speak, does writing her silent expose that silence or repeat it?

This side, at full strength: everything we know about Dolores comes from a man who is lying to us. She has no chapter of her own, and her thoughts surface only where he permits. The result is that in a book titled with the victim's name, the victim carries the least information of anyone in it. Worse, the moments she is quoted at greatest length tend to be the moments she is being rude, mercenary or evasive — precisely the moments most easily misread as complicity. Formal fidelity and real harm can be the same act; and the defence that "her silence is the subject" also conveniently relieves the author of the work of writing her whole. In 1995 a novelist retold the story in her first person, in a book called Lo's Diary, and the existence of that book is itself a piece of criticism.

The other side, at greater strength: any version that grants her an interior would be a compensatory fiction, and compensation is the one thing this story should not supply. The form of the crime is the confiscation of narrative; if the novel lent her a voice, readers would leave consoled by having heard her, when the fact about her life is that she was not heard. And Nabokov does two things that cannot be waved away: he puts her death on the first page, which makes the whole book a dead woman's effects, and he hands the final verdict to the voice missing from a mass of children's noise. Both say the same thing — her absence is not an oversight, it is the empty chair the author placed in the most conspicuous spot in the structure. As for where he stands: a writer who puts his heroine's death in the foreword, and then lets her say no the one time she can decide anything, is hard to read as being on Humbert's side.

Bring it home: news reports, court records, internal investigations at work, old family business — whose version tends to be the one written down in full? Usually the more articulate party, the one with more standing and more time to write. The book turns that asymmetry into a form.

Question three
What is a confession worth when it arrives at the last possible moment?

For its being worth something: Humbert's remorse occurs when it can buy him nothing. He is already arrested, already dying; the book is to be published only after his death; nothing he says can affect a verdict. More to the point, the content of the remorse cancels the defence the whole book rests on: he concedes that the nymphet taxonomy was false, concedes her tears, concedes that what he took was her childhood. A man overturning the theory he justified his life with, and getting nothing for it, is close to the most honest thing a person can offer. There is not one ornamental word in the mountain-road passage, which is exactly why it is credible.

Against, at greater strength: the remorse is itself a composition. He writes it as literature and hands it to posterity with a line about the only immortality they may share — that is not restitution, it is one last conversion of her into material. He calls her Lolita to the end, still using his own name for her, still arranging her posthumous position. He gave her four thousand dollars, but that was the money from her mother's house and was never his; he begged her to come away, which is not compensation either, but one more request. Real restitution has a floor: hand the decision back rather than asking to be forgiven. He cannot confess without making it scan, which shows he never left the aesthetic position at all.

Bring it home: in any public apology, how much is restitution — giving the thing back, giving the power up, accepting the consequence — and how much is image management, expressing contrition beautifully? This book is the extreme specimen: literary quality very high, restitution near zero.

The recurring obsession: what Nabokov wrote about all his life

First, a lost paradise and the unreliable craft of memory. His real first subject is not desire but exile: a boy who spoke three languages lost his country, his language and his house for good at eighteen. The ferocious preservation of detail in Speak, Memory is the same gesture. Humbert props Annabel Leigh at the front of his account because he too is telling a story about a paradise taken away — Nabokov handed his most cherished theme to a man who uses it to swindle, which is the harshest audit he ever performed on himself.

Second, the self-enchanted, superbly articulate narrator who eats another person alive. In Despair, Hermann murders a man he insists resembles him and insists the murder is a perfect work of art. In Pale Fire, Kinbote seizes another poet's poem and rewrites it as his own biography in hundreds of notes. Inverted, in Pnin, a clumsy, lovable émigré professor is narrated out of his own life by a rival who turns up late. They are all doing one thing: using the right to narrate to consume somebody else's life. Lolita is the summit of that line, because what gets consumed is a child.

Third, pattern. He was a taxonomist who spent his life looking at mimicry and repetition — why a butterfly comes to look like a dead leaf. So his novels are full of echoes, mirrorings and coincidences, and he held explicitly that the figures in a life which look like fate are somebody's design. In his books that somebody is the author; it is also why he opposed every Big Idea — he loathed Freud, whom he called the Viennese witch doctor, and loathed treating novels as social diagnosis, because those systems compete for the author's chair.

As for this book's pedigree: in Paris in 1939 he wrote a Russian novella, The Enchanter, unpublished in his lifetime, about a man who marries a dying widow to get at her daughter; and earlier still, in The Gift, a character floats much the same idea for a novel. This was not an impulse. It was a subject he pursued for sixteen years.

Controversy and criticism

One: publication and banning. After American houses declined it as a group, the book was printed in Paris by Olympia Press in 1955, was banned from sale in France for a period, was seized by British customs, and had no British edition until 1959; the 1958 American edition was a publishing phenomenon. This history is usually told as a parable of art defeating censorship, but one thing needs saying plainly: a large share of what made it a bestseller was readers believing they had bought pornography.

Two: the strongest criticism concerns Dolores. She is the major character with the least information attached, almost without independent inner life, characterised largely through consumption — film magazines, stars, gum, Coke. The defence is that this is the shape of dispossession. The retort is hard to dismiss: a girl whose entire substance is supplied by the man who harmed her is, in effect, consumed twice — once in the plot and once in the text.

Three: the question of the source. There was a real case in 1948: eleven-year-old Sally Horner was taken by a middle-aged man and travelled the United States with him as his daughter for nearly two years. The novel contains a parenthesis naming the case and the people in it, so Nabokov knew of it, and a 2018 work of non-fiction argues that its importance was deliberately played down. The counter-evidence is equally solid: he had written The Enchanter, with the same subject, in 1939, so the case can be a piece of material at most, not the origin. The valuable part of the argument is not the attribution but the question it raises — what does a writer who uses a real child's ordeal as material owe her.

Four: technical objections. The commonest concerns the ending: the Quilty murder is played as farce, and two hundred pages of hard realism give way to a dream-logic duet; some readers take this as Nabokov retreating into the game he is best at. The other is that Quilty is too nearly a function — a mirror who supplies a killable scapegoat and gives the reader someone to hate, which quietly commutes the protagonist's sentence at the last minute.

Five: the author's public position is part of the controversy. In his 1956 afterword Nabokov insisted the book carries no moral, that what he wanted was aesthetic bliss; in interviews he repeatedly called Humbert a hateful man while refusing to discuss the work's social meaning or entertain any psychoanalytic reading. Some readers see an artist keeping to his proper business; others see a man who wrote this particular book and then announced he was not interested in its effects, which is a well-mannered way of declining responsibility. The disagreement is about where an artist's responsibility ends, and it has not been settled. That after 1958 even first-rate critics read the book as a love story, that cover after cover printed a girl's legs, and that both film adaptations made her older and more willing than she is on the page, suggests the disagreement is not merely theoretical.

Six: reception in Chinese. For a long time after the book reached the Chinese market it was positioned against its own contents — covers, wrap bands and excerpts all implying erotica — and the loanword took hold mainly as a style label for young girls, almost detached from the novel. The standard annotated complete translation is Zhu Wan's, published by Shanghai Translation Publishing House in 2005; before that, Yu Xiaodan's version circulated widely. Nabokov is brutally hard to translate: puns, alliteration and jokes buried inside words are his text and not its trim, so every version loses part of the money he uses to bribe the reader — which, perversely, leaves a translated reader better placed to see the right-hand column.

Ten sentences that stay

1. The title is his nickname for her. Her name is Dolores Haze, from the root for pain. Every time you say Lolita you are using the murderer's word.

2. The fake foreword on page one tells you she is dead: in childbirth, Christmas Day 1952, aged seventeen. Almost nobody catches it the first time, which makes it the most successful ambush in the book.

3. He says it himself in chapter one: you can always count on a murderer for a fancy prose style. He nailed a warning sign to the trap and caught people anyway.

4. "Nymphet" is a word he coined and nobody else in the novel uses. It is not a category. It is page one of a defence.

5. 342 Lawn Street, room 342 at The Enchanted Hunters, three hundred-odd motels in the first year — one number pinned to a house with a mother in it, a crime scene, and a road with no end.

6. The gun that does the killing was left by her dead father. Even the props in this book are keeping accounts.

7. "She had nowhere else to go," at the close of Part One, sounds like fate. It is a progress report: he built that nowhere in one summer.

8. Her "no" at seventeen is the only time in three hundred pages that she decides the plot. The precondition for saying no is not enlightenment; it is having somewhere else to go.

9. The verdict is not any confession but one voice missing from the noise of children on a mountain road. He made that gap, and it is the only thing in the book he cannot write into poetry.

10. This novel bribes you with beautiful language for three hundred pages. What it is trying to teach is how to recognise the moment of bribery — and its method is to make you accept once.
