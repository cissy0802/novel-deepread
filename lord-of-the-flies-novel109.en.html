At first there are a few flies. Then there are swarms, buzzing, settling on the eyes, crawling over the pile of guts until it wears a coat of black fur.
Behind a curtain of creepers a small, thin boy is hiding. He doesn't dare move, and he can't look away.

This piece contains complete spoilers: who dies, how, who kills them, and who arrives at the end. The weight of this book sits on its last three chapters and its final page: the boys on the island are "rescued," and the world that rescues them is fighting the same war. Without the ending, you can't argue about whom the book is really accusing.

In one sentence

An evacuation plane goes down in wartime and a crowd of English boys aged six to twelve is left on a tropical island with no adults at all; they elect a chief, make rules and light a signal fire, and then, within a few weeks, over an imaginary beast, a supply of roast pork and a pair of spectacles, they kill the two among them who see most clearly.

Coordinates

William Golding (1911–1993) taught for years at a boys' school in Salisbury and served in the Royal Navy during the Second World War, including on D-Day. Lord of the Flies was his first novel, published when he was in his early forties. The manuscript went the rounds of a string of publishers before Charles Monteith at Faber & Faber pulled it out of the reject pile and had Golding cut an opening that described the atomic war and the evacuation outright. It appeared in 1954. Golding won the Nobel Prize in 1983.

Its place in literary history is easy to name: it is a deliberate rebuttal of the desert-island adventure. In R. M. Ballantyne's Victorian boys' book The Coral Island, three English lads shipwrecked on an island triumph over the savages through pluck and good breeding. Golding borrowed the names Ralph and Jack and asked: what if the savages aren't outside the boys, but inside them?

The story

Act one · The conch: one blast summons a society

Ralph is twelve and a bit, fair-haired, and he steps out of a fresh gash in the jungle, the long scar the plane tore through the trees as it came down. The cabin has already been dragged out to sea by a storm; there is no sign of the pilot. Behind him trails a fat boy with thick glasses and asthma whose every other sentence begins with "my auntie." The fat boy tells Ralph, quietly, that at school they called him Piggy, and please don't tell anyone. Within the hour Ralph has announced it to everyone, and they all laugh. The first trust anyone extends on the island is betrayed, carelessly, by a decent boy. Piggy's real name never appears in the book.

Ralph spots a great cream-and-pink shell in the weeds of the lagoon, a conch. Piggy knows it can be blown like a horn but can't manage it himself because of his asthma. Ralph blows, and a deep note rolls along the beach. Children come out of the forest from every direction: the small ones, the "littluns"; a pair of twins, Sam and Eric, who always speak at the same time; and a dark column marching along the sand, a choir in black cloaks and square black caps with silver badges, led by a tall red-haired boy. He is Jack Merridew, and he wants to be called by his surname. One of the choristers, a skinny little boy, faints on the spot. Jack says he's always doing that. His name is Simon.

They need a chief. Jack says it should be him; he's the head chorister. The boys vote for Ralph instead, not because he's clever but because he's good-looking and holding the conch, and the thing that summoned everyone makes him look born to be in charge. To soothe Jack, Ralph leaves him the choir and asks what they want to be. Jack says: hunters.

Ralph, Jack and Simon climb the mountain and confirm that this is an island. On the way down they come upon a piglet tangled in creepers. Jack pulls his knife and raises it high, and then he stops. He can't bring it down; the blow is still too heavy for a civilized child's arm. The piglet wrenches free and is gone. Jack, furious with shame, slams the knife into a tree trunk. Next time.

Ralph, for now, is pure delight. His father is a commander in the Navy, he tells everyone, Setup ①and the Navy knows about every island there is; sooner or later they'll come and get us. It is his comfort for the rest of the book.

Act two · Fire: a pair of spectacles and a missing child

The first assembly settles the rules: whoever holds the conch has the right to speak, and nobody may interrupt. It is a very English arrangement, and Jack is all for it. We're not savages, he says. We're English, and the English are best at everything.

A littlun with a mulberry-colored birthmark on his face holds the conch and says, timidly, that in the night he saw a snake-thing, a "beastie." The big boys laugh, and Ralph says there's no beast. But the word has been spoken.

Ralph has an idea: if they want to be rescued they need a fire on the mountaintop, so a passing ship will see the smoke. A mob of boys charges up the slope, builds an enormous pile of wood, and only then realizes no one has matches. Jack snatches the glasses off Piggy's face Setup ②and uses the lenses to focus sunlight onto dry leaves. The fire catches, while Piggy, now nearly blind, stands by shouting for his specs back. The only fire-making technology on the island sits on the face of the boy everyone despises most.

The fire soon runs wild and burns into the forest on one flank of the mountain. Amid the crackling, Piggy suddenly asks: where's the little one with the mark on his face? No one answers. He is never seen again. The first death on the island happens in the fire the boys lit in order to be saved, and by unspoken agreement nobody mentions him again.

Act three · The mask: the fire goes out, the ship goes by

Life splits in two. Ralph and Simon build shelters on the beach while everyone else wanders off to play; Jack lies in the forest for hours tracking pigs, and something comes into his eyes that Ralph can't read. Simon often slips away alone to a hidden clearing deep in the forest, walled in by creepers, where candle-shaped buds open at night.

On the beach two older boys, Roger and Maurice, kick over the littluns' sandcastles. Afterward Roger follows a small boy named Henry and starts throwing stones at him, Setup ③but he throws to miss, landing them a few yards away. Golding names what makes him miss: the taboo of the old life. Invisible parents, school, policemen and the law still hang around this child like a protective ring, even though the world behind them has collapsed.

Jack daubs his face with clay and charcoal, white and red and black, looks into a pool and sees a stranger. The mask frees him from shame. The boy who couldn't bring the knife down is gone; he laughs, dances, and leads his hunters into the trees.

At that very moment a smudge of smoke appears on the horizon: a ship. Ralph races up the mountain. The fire is cold; the boys meant to tend it have gone hunting with Jack. The ship sails on. The hunters come back carrying their first dead pig, chanting about killing the pig, cutting her throat, spilling her blood, smeared with gore and shaking with excitement. Ralph says only: you let the fire go out. When Piggy joins in, Jack smacks him on the head; the glasses fly off and one lens shatters on the rocks. Jack apologizes, for the fire, not for Piggy.

Act four · The beast: from the water, from the air, from us

Ralph calls an assembly at dusk to put the rules back on their feet. But the meeting keeps drifting back to the beast. Piggy says there isn't one; life is scientific, and the only thing to fear is people. A littlun says it might come out of the sea. Then Simon stands up with the conch, struggles for a long time, and says something nobody understands: "maybe it's only us" (chapter five). Setup ④The assembly howls with laughter; someone calls him batty.

In the uproar Jack yells that the rules can go hang, and the hunters follow him into the dark to dance. Ralph sits where he is and wonders whether to give up being chief. Piggy holds him back: if you quit, what will Jack do to me? Ralph stares at the darkening sea and wishes the grown-up world would send them a sign, anything at all.

That night a sign comes. High over the island an air battle is fought that no one sees, and a parachutist drifts down onto the mountaintop, already dead. His parachute snags on the rocks, and when the wind blows the body sits up, then bows forward again. The twins, on fire duty, see it in the dark and scramble down the mountain: the beast is up there. The grown-up world's answer is a corpse dropped out of its war.

Ralph and Jack lead a search of the island. On the way Ralph spears a boar for the first time, and the thrill of it surprises him. On the walk back they play at pig-hunting, Setup ⑤with a boy called Robert as the pig. They circle him, chanting and jabbing, harder and harder, until Robert is screaming; Ralph is in the ring too, fighting to get close enough to squeeze and hurt. Afterward Jack jokes that next time they should use a littlun. Everyone laughs. That night Ralph, Jack and Roger creep up the mountain, see the shape rising and falling in the moonlight, and run.

Act five · The Lord of the Flies: a pig's head offered to the dark

Jack calls an assembly and asks the boys to vote Ralph out: he isn't a hunter, he's a coward, all he does is talk about rules. Hands up, he says. Not one hand goes up. Tears of humiliation spill out of Jack's eyes; he says he isn't going to play any longer, and walks off alone into the forest. It is the only time in the book the conch wins outright, and the last. Over the next few hours, one by one, the bigger boys quietly vanish. They have gone to Jack.

Jack founds his tribe at Castle Rock, a fortress of pink granite at the far end of the island joined to the rest by a narrow neck of stone. His hunters chase down a sow nursing her piglets, hem her in, and Roger drives his spear into her from behind. Jack cuts off the head and gives an order: Setup ⑥sharpen a stick at both ends. One end goes into the ground, the other holds up the head. It's a gift for the beast, so it will leave them alone.

The clearing is Simon's clearing. Hidden behind the creepers, Simon watches the flies cover the head. In the heat he hears it speak. It calls itself the Lord of the Flies, which is the literal meaning of Beelzebub, the Hebrew name for a prince of devils. In the tone of a schoolmaster it mocks him: did you really think the beast was something you could hunt and kill? You knew, didn't you, that I'm part of you, that I'm the reason things are the way they are. Payoff ④ ↑What Simon couldn't get out at the assembly is said here to the bottom, and the mouth that says it is a rotting pig's head. Golding hands the book's answer to the least fit speaker imaginable and lets it come with a threat: meddle, and we'll do you. Simon passes out, blood at his nose.

That same afternoon the painted hunters storm Ralph's camp, seize burning branches from the fire, and announce that Chief Jack is giving a feast tonight. Anyone may come.

Act six · The dance: the beast crawls out of the forest

Simon wakes and climbs the mountain alone. He finds the body: a pilot, his face rotted, flies going in and out of him, jerked upright whenever the parachute lines pull. The whole island's terror is a dead adult. Simon vomits, then carefully frees the lines from the rocks so the body can rest. He staggers down the mountain to tell the others.

Ralph and Piggy have gone to Jack's feast too, out of hunger and loneliness. Thunder is piling up on the horizon; Jack sits on a log in his paint like an idol and asks who will join his tribe. As the storm breaks the hunters start to dance, and the chant is no longer about a pig: kill the beast, cut his throat, spill his blood. The littluns crowd in, the ring tightens, and Ralph and Piggy, at its edge, find themselves moving to the rhythm.

Then a dark shape crawls out of the forest, crying something about a dead man on the hill. The ring falls on it. Sticks, teeth, nails. Payoff ⑤ ↑The pig-hunting game played on Robert, and the joke about using a littlun next time, come true here: "kill the pig" has become "kill the beast," and the one in the middle of the ring is finally a person. The first time, Ralph still felt ashamed afterward. This time shame doesn't have time to catch up.

When the rain stops the tide comes in, and small luminous creatures in the water gather around Simon's body and bear it gently toward the open sea. The same wind lifts the parachutist off the mountaintop, carries him across the beach, and drops him into the ocean. The beast and the boy who exposed it are taken away on the same night.

Act seven · Castle Rock: the conch in a thousand pieces

The next day Ralph says to Piggy: that was murder. Piggy rushes to deny it: it was an accident, it was dark, he came crawling out like a beast, and anyway we were outside the ring. The twins say they left early. Ralph, clutching the conch, laughs until he shakes.

At Castle Rock, Jack is now a true chief. He has a boy named Wilfred tied up to be beaten, and nobody knows why. He tells the tribe the beast isn't dead; it will come back in disguise. That night the hunters raid Ralph's shelter in a confused brawl. Piggy assumes they've come for the conch. They don't touch the conch. They take Piggy's glasses. Payoff ② ↑The spectacles snatched off his face to light the first fire, cracked once already, are now taken whole. Jack never wanted the symbol of authority; he wanted the thing that makes fire. Without the glasses, the conch is just a shell.

Ralph, Piggy and the twins carry the conch to Castle Rock to demand the glasses back. Piggy is nearly blind and has to be led across the neck of rock. Ralph and Jack fight; the hunters seize the twins and tie them up. Piggy lifts the conch and, with all his strength, puts his last question: is it better to have rules and agree, or to hunt and kill?

High above, Roger is minding a lever wedged under a great rock. With a sense of delirious abandon he throws his whole weight on it. Payoff ③ ↑In act three, when he threw stones at Henry, he still threw to miss; the old world's taboo was still around his arm. A few weeks later the ring is gone, the stone has become a boulder, and the target is a boy. Golding gives Roger not a line of psychology. He just repeats the gesture, and this time it doesn't miss.

The rock strikes Piggy from chin to knee, and the conch explodes into a thousand white fragments. Piggy is knocked off the ledge and falls forty feet onto a square red rock in the sea, where his limbs twitch like a pig's after it has been killed; then the water draws back and sucks him away. Jack screams that he is chief and hurls his spear at Ralph. Ralph turns and flees into the forest.

Act eight · Cry of the hunters: the white cap on the last page

Ralph hides alone in the forest. He stumbles on the pig's head, picked clean now to a white skull, grinning, and smashes it down; it splits in two and goes on grinning at the sky. At night he creeps below Castle Rock, where the twins, forced into the tribe, slip him a piece of meat and warn him: tomorrow the whole tribe will hunt you across the island. And Roger has sharpened a stick at both ends. Payoff ⑥ ↑Ralph can't make sense of it. The reader understands at once: a stick sharpened at both ends was what held up the pig's head, one end in the earth and one in the head. They want Ralph's head. Golding never has anyone say "kill him." He simply lets a tool appear a second time.

At dawn the hunters sweep the island in a line, roll boulders, and finally set the forest on fire to smoke him out. The whole island goes up in a vast column of smoke. Ralph runs through flame and shrieking, bursts out of the trees, sprawls on the sand, and looks up.

In front of him stands an adult. A white-topped cap, a badge on its peak, a revolver at the hip, and offshore, a cruiser. Payoff ① ↑In act one Ralph promised that the Navy would come for them, and the Navy has come, drawn by the smoke of Jack's manhunt, not by the signal fire Ralph spent the whole book trying to keep alive. And the officer himself is in the middle of a war.

The officer grins: fun and games, eh? He asks whether anyone has been killed. Ralph says: only two. The officer's face stiffens. He'd have thought a pack of British boys could put up a better show than this, he says. "Like the Coral Island" (chapter twelve). He asks who's in charge, and Ralph says loudly that he is. Jack starts forward, then stops. In the adult's eyes he is just a little boy with red hair and the remains of an extraordinary black cap. The littlun Percival tries to recite his name and address, the words he once repeated like a spell, and finds they've faded away completely.

Ralph weeps. He weeps for "the end of innocence, the darkness of man's heart" (chapter twelve), and for the fall through the air of his true, wise friend Piggy, and the other boys begin to weep too. The officer, a little embarrassed, turns away to give them time to pull themselves together, and looks out at his trim cruiser in the distance.

The fuse

It looks as though the fuse is Jack: the first time he raises a knife he can't use it, the second time he kills a pig, the third time a boy. But Golding buries it deeper. The real fuse is a word spoken in act two by the little boy with the birthmark: "beastie." A thing nobody has ever seen, and nobody can prove isn't there, becomes the island's greatest source of power. Jack draws off the big boys with "I can kill it," turns fear into ritual with the offering, and secures his rule with "it'll come back in disguise." Simon dies because he tries to say what it is. The beast was never on the island. It was in every moment a child chose to believe in it.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion one · The pig's head speaks

The whole scene is locked inside Simon's senses: blazing sun, sweat, a headache, the buzzing of the flies swelling until it is almost a language. Golding never says whether the head really speaks. We only know Simon is feverish and close to a fit, so the conversation can be hallucination or revelation, and the horror is that you don't have to choose.

Its power lies in changing the speaker. The same truth, stammered out by Simon at the assembly, got him laughed at. Put in the mouth of a pig's head, in a schoolmaster's voice, it carries an authority you can't argue with, and a threat. Golding is telling you that this truth isn't discovered by the wise; it leaks out of evil itself, and the first thing it does is warn the one who knows to keep quiet.

Slow motion two · Two who die on the same night

Simon's killing is written fast: short sentences, plural subjects, "they" surging forward, no single boy named. In the ring there are no individuals, only an action. Then the pace slows almost to stillness. The rain stops, the moon comes out, the glowing creatures in the water gather round the body as if they knew what it was, outline it in silver, and carry it out to sea.

The violence is written as chaos; the dead Simon is painted like a religious picture. A prophet is torn apart by the people he came to save, and the ocean gives him the funeral humanity didn't. In the very next breath the parachutist is blown into the sea too. With two shots Golding says that the one boy who saw the truth, and the false "truth" everyone had mistaken for the beast, were wiped away by the same water, and the survivors are free to keep believing whatever they like.

Slow motion three · The point of view changes on the last page

The whole book is written at the boys' height. Beast, tribe, chief, offering: the reader has grown used to these words. On the last page the camera cuts abruptly to the officer's eyes. To him there is no "chief," only a red-haired little boy in a ruined cap; no "tribe," only a crowd of grubby children daubed with mud. He even calls a manhunt "fun and games."

The cut does two opposite things. It shrinks the boys back into children, and the reader wakes with a jolt: we have just watched six- to twelve-year-olds killing one another. It also lets us see the adult's blindness. The officer is disappointed that British boys didn't put up a better show, while the cruiser behind him is about to return to a far larger hunt. Golding later said something to the effect of: and who will rescue the adult and his cruiser?

The central device: a thought experiment with the adults removed

Lord of the Flies is not a realist novel; it's a fable built as a thought experiment. The design is clear: take away the adult world, keep the children the adults have trained, and see how long the training holds. Each boy works like a component: Ralph is order, Piggy is reason, Jack is appetite, Simon is intuition, Roger is the cruelty waiting to be let off the leash. Golding described his aim, roughly, as tracing the defects of society back to the defects of human nature.

At the heart of the device are three objects, each standing for a kind of power:

  • The conch: rules and legitimacy. It can't hurt anyone; its power rests entirely on everyone agreeing that it has power. The moment Jack says the conch doesn't count at his end of the island, it's just a shell.
  • The glasses: knowledge and productive capacity. They are the only way to make fire, and whoever holds them has roast meat, smoke, and the hope of rescue. That's why Jack's raid takes them and not the conch.
  • The beast: fear. It doesn't exist, and that makes it the most useful of the three: whoever defines it and promises to deal with it can demand obedience.

Which links in the device hold, and which are there because the fable needs them? What holds is the politicization of fear: a threat that can't be disproved favors whoever promises to destroy it, and human history repeats the pattern endlessly. Deindividuation holds too: people with painted faces, inside a ring, do things they wouldn't do alone.

What belongs to the fable should be marked honestly: the speed and thoroughness of the collapse serve the allegory. The book never says how much time passes; from the plot it's a matter of weeks, perhaps a couple of months. There are no girls and no adults, the boys all come from the same hierarchical English school culture, and the island has fresh water, fruit and more pigs than they can eat. Survival pressure is slight, and almost all the violence comes from within. These sterile conditions are meant to prove that violence is innate; for exactly that reason the book can't prove what it sets out to. It only demonstrates a belief with enormous force. This is a fable, not a lab report.

What it forces you to think about

One · If you were one of the boys, would you vote for the one who keeps the fire, or the one who brings the meat?

Everything Ralph wants is far away: a column of smoke that a ship may or may not see, shelters that keep falling down, a rule that you may only speak when holding a shell. Everything Jack offers is close: roast meat tonight, a mask to hide your fear behind, a ring to dance in when the hunt goes well, and a promise that if the beast comes, he'll kill it for you.

The strongest case for Jack isn't that the boys went bad. It's this: Ralph's order never answered the boys' real situation. The littluns scream with fear at night, and Ralph tells them there is no beast. That may be correct as reasoning, but as an experience it is abandonment. Jack admits the fear, gives it a shape, and offers a way to fight it, which is what a leader is for. And look at the results. Ralph guarded the fire, and the ship went past; what finally brought the Navy was Jack's blaze. If a leader who only ever says "think long-term" never once delivers the long-term thing, why should anyone keep going hungry on his say-so?

The case for Ralph is just as hard. Meat runs out; smoke is the only way off. And Jack's cure for fear is to manufacture a bigger fear: he needs the beast to exist, needs traitors to exist, needs Wilfred beaten for no reason. Someone who takes power by meeting the needs of the moment often ends up keeping it by inventing enemies. The fire that "saved" them was lit to burn a boy alive.

Golding's sympathies are obvious, but he never lets Ralph win an argument; by the end Ralph keeps losing hold of why the fire mattered at all. The structure isn't far from us. In any organization someone says "fix the foundations first" and someone says "hit this quarter's numbers." The last time you voted for the meat close at hand, was it because you were hungry, or because the smoke in the distance had grown so faint?

Two · The ones dancing at the edge of the ring: did they kill Simon?

Piggy says it was an accident. It was dark, there was thunder, he came crawling out of the forest like a beast, we were all frightened of the beast already, and we were only on the outside. He isn't lying. He is struggling to stay someone who can go on living.

The strongest case for Piggy: people in a crowd, in terror, in the dark, are genuinely not their ordinary selves, and charging that moment entirely to the individual comes close to demanding that everyone be a saint. Morality has to weigh consequences, too. If the last two boys still defending the rules collapse under guilt now, there will be no one left on the island to keep the fire. Saying "we are murderers" won't bring Simon back; it will only make the survivors give up faster. Denial here is not cowardice but a condition of survival.

Ralph's "that was murder" stands just as firmly. Nobody forced them to go to the feast; they went because they were hungry and lonely. They moved at the edge of the ring, they felt the rhythm, and feeling it was taking part. If "I was only standing nearby" is an excuse, then no act of mob violence has ever had a killer. And the harder Piggy denies it, the more he uses the reason he prizes to defend the least reasonable thing there is.

Golding lets Ralph say "murder" and then shake with laughter: having admitted it, he has no idea what to do with the admission. Life offers plenty of versions. An online pile-on in which you only shared the post. A colleague frozen out at work while you just didn't speak up. Are you more Piggy or more Ralph? And which of them is more likely to let you go on being a decent person tomorrow?

Three · A conch that can't hurt anyone: whom did it ever protect?

Piggy believes in the conch to his last breath. The final time he raises it, he asks: is it better to have rules and agree, or to hunt and kill? Then the rock comes down and smashes the boy and the shell together.

The strongest case that the conch is empty has a Hobbesian chill: covenants without the sword are only words. The conch worked because at first everyone was willing to let it work; once a single boy is unwilling and holds a spear, it's worthless. Ralph and Piggy's mistake wasn't a lack of goodness but naivety: they believed rules protect themselves. To keep Piggy safe, Ralph should have raised his own band of hunters on the first day.

The case that the conch isn't empty is strong as well. In those first weeks nobody was forced at spearpoint, yet the boys took turns to speak, took turns at the fire, built shelters for the littluns. That order was made by the conch, not by violence, only by a group of people willing to believe in the same thing at once. Its shattering doesn't prove it was useless, only that it needed defending; and if Ralph had raised an army, he would have become another Jack. The strength of rules is that they don't rest on the knife. That is also their fragility.

Golding's pessimism is plain, but he also brings the officer on stage with a gun and a cruiser, a conch with a sword behind it, and that conch is fighting a world war. In your own life, how much of your building's bylaws, your company's policies, your group chat's rules runs on people's willingness, and how much on someone behind it who can punish you? Take the second away, and how long would the first hold?

Motifs: what Golding kept writing about

Golding wrote about one thing all his life: people are not spoiled by society; the badness is in us, and civilization is a thin crust laid over it. In "Fable," an essay about this novel, he said something to the effect that anyone who lived through the war years without understanding that man produces evil as a bee produces honey must have been blind or wrong in the head. What he had seen made him stop believing in The Coral Island.

The Inheritors (1955), published the next year, is almost the same subject shot in reverse: through the eyes of a band of gentle Neanderthals we watch modern humans arrive, bringing fire, drink and killing. "We" were the intruders in Eden from the start. In Pincher Martin (1956) a drowning naval officer clings to a rock, and only at the end do we learn he has been dead all along; what refuses to let go is his ego. In The Spire (1964) a dean insists on raising a spire over his cathedral regardless of the foundations or the lives it costs, his faith and his appetite impossible to pull apart. Lord of the Flies is where the line begins, and its barest version: take away every excuse, and you're left with children.

Controversy and criticism

Does it really prove that human nature is evil? The best-known rebuttal comes from the Dutch writer Rutger Bregman in Humankind (2020). In 1965 six Tongan schoolboys took a boat out to sea and were wrecked on the uninhabited island of ʻAta. They stayed more than a year, survived by dividing the work, setting rules and keeping a fire going in shifts, nursed a companion who broke his leg, and were found and brought home in 1966 by an Australian captain. Bregman argues that Golding's book tells a popular story with little evidence behind it. Defenders answer that a novel describes a possibility, and the twentieth century supplied that possibility with all too many real examples.

No girls. The island holds only boys, and only one culture, that of the English public school. Critics say the book passes off a particular male hierarchy and boarding-school discipline as "human nature." Others think that is precisely its most honest feature: it is really dissecting the education designed to produce English gentlemen.

The rhetoric of "savages." The book sets "savages" against "the English," and treats face paint, dancing and tribes as synonyms for degeneration; Piggy even calls the hunters a pack of painted Indians (chapter eleven). Many readers now point out that this binary rests on colonial stereotypes, so that a novel meant to puncture English superiority does it in the language of that superiority.

Class, and a stiff allegory. Piggy's accent and the auntie with the sweet shop mark him as lower down; some argue that Golding, too, gives him no dignity, only a martyr's place. Other critics find the characters too emblematic, each boy matched too neatly to a concept.

The author himself. John Carey's 2009 biography revealed that in a private memoir Golding admitted that, at about eighteen, he had tried to sexually assault a fifteen-year-old girl. In later life he often spoke of a deep evil in himself. That has led some readers to see the book first as a self-indictment rather than a verdict on humankind.

Crossovers

  • Chinese philosophy · Xunzi on "nature is bad": Xunzi held that human nature craves gain, and goodness is artifice, the rites and duties people deliberately construct. Golding's conch and rules are exactly that artifice, and remove them and the appetite shows. The difference: Xunzi trusted ritual to transform anyone through accumulated effort, while the boys invent rituals on the spot, the hunting chant, the offered head, the ring dance, and ritual itself grows toward savagery.
  • Social psychology · Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment: in 1954 Muzafer Sherif took two groups of eleven-year-old boys to a summer camp; competition soon had them raiding each other's cabins and burning flags, and shared goals that required cooperation later dissolved the hostility. Alike in the boys, the camp, the instant tribes; unlike in that Sherif found situations create enmity and can undo it.
  • Psychology · deindividuation and Halloween: in 1976 Ed Diener and colleagues watched trick-or-treaters and found that children in groups, costumed so they couldn't be identified, took extra candy far more often. Jack's face paint is the same mechanism. The difference: a mask lowers the threshold; it doesn't conjure a desire to kill out of nothing.
  • Evolutionary anthropology · Wrangham's two kinds of aggression: in The Goodness Paradox Richard Wrangham separates impulsive "reactive" aggression from planned, coalitional "proactive" aggression, arguing humans are low in the first and high in the second. The hunt for Ralph, with its sharpened stick and coordinated sweep, is the second kind. Unlike in scale: he describes evolutionary tendencies, not a collapse over a few weeks.
