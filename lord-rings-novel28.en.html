The Lord of the Rings

J. R. R. Tolkien · 1954–1955 · one novel in six books, published in three volumes

中文 →

An old man is standing in his own front hall holding an envelope. There is a gold ring inside it. He has just played a joke on the entire village at his one hundred and eleventh birthday party—put the ring on, vanished—and now his bags are packed and one thing is left undone: he promised to leave the ring behind for his nephew.
He reaches out to set the envelope on the mantelpiece. His hand jerks back on its own and the envelope drops to the floor. He bends to pick it up and the wizard in the doorway stops him.
Sixty years ago he found it underneath a mountain, in total darkness. Now he cannot put it down on a piece of wood.
Six books follow, and they are all about that same gesture, moved to other people and other places: what it takes for someone to let go of the thing in his hand.

This page contains full spoilers—how Gandalf goes off the bridge in Moria, why Boromir reaches for it, how Éowyn kills the Witch-king, the fact that Frodo does not throw the Ring into the fire at Mount Doom, how it is actually destroyed, and what the Shire looks like when they get home. The premise of this site is that everything worth discussing in a novel lies past its ending. If you want to keep the suspense, read the book first; this page will wait.

In one sentence

A ring that grants its bearer absolute power and is guaranteed to turn that bearer into the next tyrant is handed to the least ambitious and least capable creature available; the nine-man escort falls apart halfway, and the story splits into three strands that fight three separate wars in ignorance of each other; and when the small person finally stands at the edge of the one fire in the world that can unmake it, he does not throw it in—he puts it on. The world is saved by a knife that somebody else declined to use, in the dark under a mountain, nearly eighty years earlier.

Coordinates

J. R. R. Tolkien (1892–1973) was, professionally, an Oxford philologist—Professor of Anglo-Saxon and later of English Language and Literature, a lifetime spent on Old and Middle English, with fiction as the side project. In 1916 he served as a signals officer on the Somme; two of his closest friends died that year and he was invalided home with trench fever. Middle-earth did not begin with a story and acquire languages afterward; it happened the other way round. He had been inventing Elvish (Quenya, Sindarin) since his youth, and eventually decided a language needs speakers, a history, a mythology—so the world was built to hold the words.

He wrote The Lord of the Rings between 1937 and 1949, for a mundane reason: The Hobbit (1937) sold well and the publisher wanted a sequel. It took twelve years. It is one novel in six books; when it was published in 1954–55, postwar paper was expensive and the publisher was afraid of losing money, so it was cut into three volumes to sell—hence The Fellowship of the Ring, The Two Towers, The Return of the King. Tolkien disliked the word "trilogy" and disliked the third title in particular, on the grounds that it prints the ending on the cover. Modern fantasy essentially begins with this book: before it, an invented world was usually the furniture of a fairy tale or a satire; after it, "a map, a few peoples, a self-consistent chronology and a language" became the factory settings of an entire genre. It is also one of the twentieth century's least critically beloved bestsellers—section nine, honestly.

The story

Act one · The ring taken out of a drawer (Book I)

The Shire is where hobbits live: farmed hills, inhabitants short and round and hairy-footed, several meals a day, no curiosity about anything twenty miles off. Bilbo Baggins went on one long journey with a company of dwarves sixty years ago and came back with a ring in his pocket—put it on, disappear, very convenient. After the birthday party he leaves Bag End and everything in it to his nephew Frodo and goes to Rivendell to retire.

The wizard Gandalf then disappears for seventeen years into old archives, and comes back looking unwell. He has Frodo throw the ring into the hearth; it comes out cold, but a line of Black Speech has surfaced on the gold—one Ring to rule all the others and bind them in the dark. This is the One Ring, forged by Sauron himself, with most of his own power poured into it. It was cut from his hand thousands of years ago, changed owners, killed several of them, sank in a river, and was fished out by a small creature named Sméagol; Sméagol hid under a mountain for five hundred years while it hollowed him into Gollum, and then, in a loss no one can quite explain, it was picked up by a passing Bilbo.

Here Gandalf says something that will come due eighty years later. Frodo says Gollum deserved death, that Bilbo should have stabbed him in the tunnel. Gandalf's answer, in substance: many who live deserve death, and many who die deserve life—can you give it to them? Since you cannot, do not be quick to hand out death as a sentence. Then he adds something stranger: he has a feeling Gollum's part is not finished, and that the pity Bilbo felt that day may end up deciding the fate of a great many people.seed ①

Sauron is already looking. Gollum has been taken into Mordor and tortured, and has given up two words: Shire, and Baggins. Nine Black Riders—the Nazgûl, nine former kings of Men long since eaten hollow by rings of their own, nothing left but will and hatred—ride out.

Frodo leaves the Shire overnight with his gardener Sam and his cousins Merry and Pippin. The journey is slow and badly managed: they are nearly eaten by a willow tree in the Old Forest and rescued by Tom Bombadil, who lives at its edge; after leaving his house they are caught by something that crawls out of a grave on the Barrow-downs, and rescued by him a second time. On their way out he picks a dagger for each of them from the barrow's hoard, and takes the trouble to explain where they came from: they were made long ago in a western kingdom of Men, forged specifically against the Witch-king of Angmar. At the time it reads as an eccentric old man handing out equipment.seed ②

At the inn in Bree they meet a ranger smoking in the corner, known as Strider, whose real name is Aragorn—the last heir of the lost northern line and, on paper, the rightful claimant to the throne of Gondor, currently indistinguishable from a vagrant. He takes them cross-country, and on Weathertop the Riders catch them. Frodo makes the worst mistake of his journey: he cannot stop himself putting the Ring on. The instant he does he can see the wraiths as they truly are, and they can finally see him. The Witch-king drives a blade into his left shoulder—a Morgul-knife, whose broken tip stays in the flesh and works toward the heart, turning a person by degrees into the same kind of thing the Riders are.seed ③ The elf-lord Glorfindel gets him onto a horse and across the ford, the river rises and sweeps the pursuit away, and Frodo arrives at Rivendell unconscious.

Act two · Nine walkers go east (Book II)

Frodo is healed in the house of Elrond, and there he sits in on the most important conversation in the book. The Council of Elrond is almost entirely dialogue and runs a full chapter. Every option for the Ring is raised and struck down: hide it—Sauron will find it; drop it in the sea—time is on his side; give it to someone strong enough to use it—the most attractive option, and the central question of the whole novel, which section six takes apart. What remains is to carry it back to where it was made: the fire of Mount Doom in Mordor, the only heat in the world that can unmake it.

Nobody volunteers. After a long silence, the smallest and least important person present says he will go, though he does not know the way. The mechanics of the entire book are set in that one sentence: every powerful being in the world is in the room, none of them can touch it, so the weakest one walks.

Nine set out against nine Riders: four hobbits, Gandalf, Aragorn, Boromir (elder son of Gondor's Steward), Legolas the elf, Gimli the dwarf. The mountain pass defeats them, which leaves only the road under it—Moria, the abandoned underground kingdom of the dwarves, a city eaten by what it dug up: they mined mithril too deep and woke a fire-demon left over from the elder world, a Balrog. In Balin's tomb the company reads the last dwarvish record, which stops in the middle of a sentence. The pursuit arrives; on the bridge of Khazad-dûm Gandalf turns and stands and tells everyone else to run. The bridge breaks and the two of them go down together.

The remaining eight stagger into the elvish wood of Lothlórien. This is the slowest stretch of the novel and the most necessary one—somebody has just died and there has to be somewhere to grieve. Galadriel shows Frodo and Sam her mirror, which holds things past, things present, and things that may never come to pass. Sam sees the Shire: trees felled, a new mill on the river pouring out black smoke, his old father turned out of his house. He wants to go straight home; Galadriel points out that the mirror is a poor guide to action.seed ④

Then Frodo holds the Ring out to her and asks her to take it. She refuses, and says that she has passed the test, that she will diminish, go into the West, and remain herself. What she refuses is not power in the abstract; it is a version of herself that has just become vivid in front of her. At their farewell she gives each of them a gift; Frodo's is a small crystal phial holding the reflection of a star caught in water, and she tells him it is a light for him when every other light has gone out.seed ⑤

They go down the great river, and on the hill of Amon Hen, Boromir reaches. He is not a villain, and his case is the strongest argument in the book: Gondor is bleeding, the enemy is across the water, and this company is carrying a weapon that could save the city off to be destroyed. He argues, then pleads, then lunges. Frodo puts the Ring on to escape—and sitting on the high seat with it on, he feels the enormous Eye sweeping the world, turning toward him, almost closing on him; he pulls it off at the last second. He decides to go alone, because the thing corrodes the people near him faster than it corrodes him. Sam guesses, nearly drowns in the river, and is hauled into the boat. The two of them head east.

Act three · Horses and trees (Book III)

The company is broken and the story breaks with it, into three strands. Tolkien handles them with a very old technique: not crosscutting, but following each line to its end and then going back for the next one—so when Book III finishes, Book IV's clock is still two weeks in the past.

Boromir dies on the shore. Orcs come for the hobbits, and he stands alone to protect Merry and Pippin, takes many arrows, and admits to Aragorn as he dies that he tried to take the Ring. He is the only person in the book who actually reaches for it and then buys the gesture back with his life. Aragorn, Legolas and Gimli lay him in a boat, send him over the falls, and run across the plains of Rohan after the orc party.

Merry and Pippin tumble into Fangorn Forest and meet Treebeard—an Ent: shaped like a tree, extremely slow to walk and slower to speak, and capable of spending three days getting through one agenda item. The Ents already know what the wizard Saruman is doing: he has dug an industrialized war factory into Isengard and is clear-cutting the forest to feed its furnaces. They finish their meeting and decide to go and break it. When creatures who measure time in centuries decide to move, they move frighteningly fast: they tear open the dams and turn Isengard into a lake of mud.

Meanwhile Gandalf comes back. He and the Balrog fell together, fought from the roots of the mountain to its peak, and he killed it above the snowline and died doing it, and was sent back, grey turned white. He takes the three trackers to the hall of Rohan, where old King Théoden has spent years listening to a counsellor called Gríma—Saruman's ear, who has talked a king into an old man afraid of his own doorway. Gandalf talks him back out. Then Helm's Deep: Saruman's army pins what is left of Rohan behind a wall in a gorge all night, the wall is blown open, and at dawn the king decides to ride out and die decently—at which point Gandalf appears on the slope with relief, and a black forest that has grown overnight at the mouth of the valley swallows the routed army.

Afterward they ride to Isengard and find a ruin standing in muddy water and two hobbits sitting on a broken wall smoking. Saruman is shut in his tower; Gandalf offers him a last chance and he will not come down. Gríma throws a black stone ball down from a window, and Pippin takes it out in the night to look into it—a palantír, one of the ancient seeing-stones, with Sauron on the other end. It nearly kills him with fright, and it also convinces Sauron, wrongly, that Gondor is holding a hobbit prisoner. Gandalf puts him on a horse and rides for the White City.

Act four · Two of them and a hanger-on (Book IV)

Rewind to the days after the breaking. Frodo and Sam spend days lost in a maze of stone. At night Gollum climbs down a cliff face after them—he has followed since Moria. Frodo pins him; Sam wants to kill him and be done; Frodo will not. That refusal is the entire engine of this strand: a creature starved to bone is allowed to live, because the person sparing him heard Gandalf say something a year ago.

They take him as a guide on condition that he swears. He insists on swearing by the Precious—that is, by the Ring itself—to serve its master. Frodo permits it, and says out loud what it means: you swear by it and the oath will hold you; but it will look for a way to twist the oath around onto you.seed ⑥

This is the bleakest stretch of road in the novel: the Dead Marshes (an old battlefield, with a layer of uncorrupted dead faces just under the water), the Black Gate shut fast, and then a detour south into Ithilien. Gollum argues with himself the whole way, one voice telling him to keep his word and the other to take back the Precious. One night his hand nearly comes to rest on Frodo's knee, and for a moment he looks like nothing but something very old and very tired; Sam wakes and snaps at him, and whatever had softened goes back in and never comes out again.

In Ithilien they are taken by a Gondorian patrol whose captain is Faramir—Boromir's younger brother. The scene mirrors the one on Amon Hen exactly: same object, same bleeding city, same chance to use it. Faramir questions the truth out of them and then lets them go, saying he would not pick this thing up if he found it lying by the road. Tolkien gives the two brothers an identical problem and opposite answers, and does not insert a word of commentary in between.

The last road Gollum takes them by is Cirith Ungol—an unguarded pass. It is unguarded because the thing that guards it does not require staffing. On the climb he goes over entirely and leads them into the lair of Shelob: a spider that has been alive since the elder days, older than any orc, interested only in eating. The dark in there has weight. With nothing left, Frodo remembers the little phial.payoff ⑤ ↑ Look back at that parting gift in Lothlórien and the beauty of it is how vague Galadriel was—"when every other light has gone out" sounds like a blessing and is in fact a precise set of operating instructions, and this is the one place in the novel where every other light really has. Better still is the provenance of that light: it is the reflection of a reflection of a star, at two removes, and this small quantity of secondhand old light is what drives back a monster from the first age.

They get out of the tunnel; Shelob comes from behind and stings Frodo down. Sam believes he is dead. What follows is Sam's hour alone: he beats the spider off, and then makes the second most important decision in the book—he takes the Ring off his master and puts it on himself, intending to finish the errand alone. Then he hears orcs talking, and learns from their conversation that Frodo is not dead, only paralyzed, and has already been carried into the tower. He throws himself after them and the door shuts in his face.

Act five · The White City and the Black Gate (Book V)

Cut to Minas Tirith, capital of Gondor: seven tiers of white stone built into a mountainside, with the border of Mordor twenty miles off. Pippin is presented to the Steward Denethor—father of Boromir and Faramir, shrewd, proud, and years past his last good night's sleep. He also owns a palantír and has been using it in secret to watch Sauron. He believes he is conducting reconnaissance. Sauron has been choosing what he is allowed to see.

Aragorn goes another way. He first uses the captured stone to face Sauron directly and let himself be seen—an heir of Isildur holding a reforged sword. Then he takes the Paths of the Dead, a tunnel under the mountains nobody will enter, occupied by an army that broke an oath thousands of years ago and has not been permitted to rest since. He calls in the debt; once it is paid he releases them, and comes north up the river in captured black ships with the levies of the southern provinces. The Riders of Rohan are on the road at the same time. Théoden's niece Éowyn is told to stay and keep the house—the instruction she has heard more than any other in her life. She rides in disguise anyway, with Merry hidden in front of her saddle.

Then the Battle of the Pelennor Fields. Mordor's army invests the White City and the gate is broken open—the Witch-king rides through the arch himself, and Gandalf, alone, is what stands in it. At that moment horns sound outside: Rohan has arrived. On the field, the Witch-king on a winged beast brings Théoden's horse down on top of him, and a small rider steps between them. He laughs and says no living man can hinder him—which is a quotation: a prophecy thousands of years old held that he would not fall by the hand of man, and everyone since has read that as a fancy way of saying he cannot fall. The rider takes off the helmet, hair comes down, and she says that she is no man, and puts her sword into the beast's neck. What makes the killing blow land is a second blade from behind: Merry has come round and driven the unremarkable dagger from the Barrow-downs through the back of the Witch-king's knee.payoff ② ↑ Go back to Bombadil handing out equipment in Act One and you find that Tolkien buried the answer in the paragraph everyone assumes is skippable: that blade was forged by northern Men against this specific enemy and carries spells wound against him, and the moment it goes in, it cuts the stitching that has held him together for millennia, which is the only reason Éowyn's stroke can finish anything. The elegance is in how the credit is divided: not one hero killing one dark lord, but two people who had both been told they had no business on a battlefield—a woman and a small person—each doing half.

Another scene plays out inside the city at the same time. Denethor, certain Faramir is dying, carries his son onto a pyre to burn with him. Gandalf and Pippin get in and drag Faramir out; the old man lies back down on the wood and lights it, holding the seeing-stone. The only character in the novel who is destroyed outright is destroyed by the thing he believed he was acquiring: information.

They win the battle, and everyone knows it is only a delay; what is left cannot hold a second assault. Hence the mad decision: take everyone who can still walk to the Black Gate and offer battle. The purpose is not victory. It is to pull Sauron's attention out of his own country and buy a few more days for two people crawling across it. They form up and are surrounded by ten times their number—a fight nobody expects to win, with the stake held by someone else.

Act six · A mountain, a house, a ship (Book VI)

Sam goes into the tower of Cirith Ungol alone. He does not have to fight: the two orc companies inside have killed each other over the mithril coat taken off Frodo. He finds Frodo stripped and whipped, and gives the Ring back. The gesture is written very lightly, and it makes him the only person in the novel who voluntarily hands the thing over.

Then the crossing of Mordor. There is no combat in this stretch, only thirst. Two figures in scavenged orc-mail walk over a plain burnt to ash, hiding by day and moving by night, with the water bottle getting lighter. The Ring grows heavier the closer it comes to the mountain, until Frodo is crawling. His memory goes backward: first the taste of water, then the color green, and finally he cannot recall what the Shire looked like—there is only the object and the direction. Sam carries him up the last of the slope.

At the edge of the crack. Frodo stops and turns round. He says he has changed his mind, and that the thing is his. Then he puts it on and vanishes. Eighty years, six books and an uncountable number of lives paved that road, and at its end one sentence voids it.

At which point Gollum is on him. He has followed all the way here; he throws himself at the invisible man, bites the finger off, takes the Ring, dances with it on the lip of the crack, mis-steps, and falls.payoff ⑥ ↑ Go back to the oath in Act Four: Frodo said the oath would be twisted back onto the swearer, an offhand-sounding warning that is redeemed with unpleasant precision—Gollum does keep the letter of it (he never once takes the Ring off Frodo; he waits until Frodo has brought it out and declared it his own), and the letter is exactly what carries him into the fire.payoff ① ↑ The larger account settles here too: three acts of mercy—Bilbo under the mountain, Frodo in the stony maze, Faramir at the forbidden pool—and if any one of them had gone the other way, the only figure standing at the crack now would be a new dark lord holding a ring. Tolkien puts the decisive move of his entire novel on the one quality that is never presented as a strength: pity. It is not a strategy; most of the time it looks like a soft-headed habit of making things worse. And it is the only thing in the book that actually succeeds.

The Ring goes in, Sauron disperses, the army at the Black Gate scatters. Eagles pick two unconscious hobbits out of the lava fields. Aragorn is crowned and marries Elrond's daughter Arwen; Faramir and Éowyn meet in the Houses of Healing and later marry—where she makes a second choice, to stop looking for a glorious death.

And then they go home. The least triumphant thing about this book is that it does not stop at the coronation: it puts four hobbits back on the road to the Shire and walks them into a homeland occupied by wire fences, posted regulations and men called Chiefs—trees down, a new mill smoking on the river, Sam's father evicted.payoff ④ ↑ The frame from the mirror in Act Two now matches, panel for panel. What makes it good is not that a prophecy comes true, but Galadriel's warning attached to it: the mirror is a poor guide to action—if Sam had turned round and gone home, the Shire would have ended up like this anyway, and there would have been nobody left able to undo it. Foresight in this novel never provides a shortcut; it only lets you understand afterward that the whole road was necessary. The man behind it is Saruman, who has wandered here after being let out of his tower to spend the last of his appetite for ruin on the smallest place he can find. The four of them organize the locals and drive the thugs out; Saruman dies at the door of Bag End, knifed by Gríma, who followed him for a lifetime and was kicked for a lifetime.

The Shire is rebuilt. Sam replants the trees with a box of earth and a seed Galadriel gave him, and that year's harvest is absurd; he marries, has children, becomes mayor. Only Frodo does not recover. His shoulder goes bad on the same date every yearpayoff ③ ↑—what the broken Morgul-blade left on Weathertop can be postponed by skill and never removed; the book lets that anniversary surface a few times, always in passing, until the reader works out unaided that this person is slowly leaving. Tolkien does not retire his protagonist with honors. He gives him a wound that reminds him once a year, and lets everyone around him get happier. In the end Frodo hands his half-finished book to Sam and takes ship at the Grey Havens with Bilbo, Gandalf, Elrond and Galadriel, and goes west. Sam rides home, walks in, sits down, and a child climbs onto his lap. The last line of the novel is his: "Well, I'm back."

Winding the causes into one fuse: this book does not begin with Sauron forging the Ring, or with Bilbo finding it. It begins with Bilbo standing over a weeping, half-blind, wretched creature with a knife in his hand, and not using it. Everything comes off that. Gollum lives, so he is later caught and tortured and gives up "Shire" and "Baggins," which is why Riders arrive at Frodo's door. Gollum lives, so he leads two people into Shelob's lair and nearly ends the whole thing. And because Gollum lives, there is somebody at Mount Doom to catch the moment of failure. One withheld stroke is both the first link in the catastrophe and the only way out of it—six books spent taking a moral gesture and stretching it into a complete causal chain, to see whether it can stand up on its own.

Three slow-motion scenes

1 · The bridge of Khazad-dûm (Book II)

Start with what Tolkien declines to write. The Balrog is never described cleanly: what the reader gets is torches dimming, a shape inside a shadow at the end of a hall, heat, and the way light changes on the floor as it approaches. Whether it has wings has been argued over for seventy years, because the text only says the shadow reached out like wings. All the dread comes from the point of view being locked onto the hobbits—someone four feet tall, being shoved along at a run, with no time to look back. What he can see is all the reader is given.

Then the rhythm. The retreat is short sentences, running, breath; on the bridge the sentences stop and become one person standing still and speaking. The most disciplined stroke comes after: the bridge breaks, the enemy drops, and just as everyone has been permitted to think it is over, a whip of fire comes up out of the dark and takes him round the knees. The victory is announced once and then withdrawn. Had the Balrog simply fallen, Gandalf would merely have won. With the whip, he wins and goes anyway. Afterward there is no reassurance, no narrator hinting he will return. Tolkien keeps this man dead for two full books.

2 · The edge at Mount Doom (Book VI)

The shortest scene in the novel and the one that most flatly refuses its genre. Six books have been preparing an act of self-sacrifice, and the reader has known since page one what the act is: walk to the fire, open your hand. Tolkien writes the act—and then writes his protagonist unable to perform it.

Three technical choices make it work. First, the point of view is Sam's: we cannot see inside Frodo, only a man turning round, saying two sentences, and disappearing. There is no depiction of inner struggle, because the struggle was written into the previous two hundred pages of crawling. Second, the sentences are flat—no ornament, no scored crescendo, just statement. Third, everything that follows happens in seconds: Gollum lunges, bites, seizes it, dances, mis-steps. The fate of the world is settled by a lunatic losing his footing, and Tolkien has no intention of dressing that up.

He had a name for the structure: eucatastrophe. In his 1939 lecture on fairy stories he argued that the heart of a good tale is not a happy state of affairs but the sudden turn that arrives after everything really has become unsalvageable; the despair has to be real or the turn is cheap. Mount Doom is his own demonstration: let the protagonist genuinely fail, so that the victory is not something he earned.

3 · "Well, I'm back." (Book VI)

Most epics end at the coronation. This one has a hundred pages after it, and they get steadily smaller: from a world to a country, from a country to a county, from a county to a house, from a house to a chair and a child climbing into a lap. It is a deliberately constructed deceleration. Tolkien said in his second-edition foreword that the Shire chapter is not a comment on postwar England and was in the outline from the start—because the story has to return to the small place it left from before the reader can measure what the road cost: the same kitchen, one person short.

The last sentence has no adjectives in it. It can carry the weight of the whole novel because "back" is no longer a neutral word by the time it arrives: Frodo has just permanently not come back. The plainest possible line closes the book precisely because someone is standing beside it who can never say it.

The central device: a ring nobody can use

Rule one: it is a piece of Sauron. He poured most of his own power into the forging, which is why he is formidable and also where he is exposed—destroy the Ring and he disperses into a grievance that can never reassemble. His country, his armies and his fortresses are all staked on an object that can be dropped into a fire.

Rule two: its function is not strength, it is domination. The Ring does not throw lightning. It does one thing—make its bearer's will overrule other wills. So what it offers differs completely from person to person, while translating every desire into the same grammar: do as I say.

Rule three: therefore it cannot be used well. This is the load-bearing link and the most contestable one. The argument runs: the Ring's power simply is domination; the stronger and more capable the bearer, the more thoroughly his intentions get executed; and any benevolence carried through to completion, unobstructed, is indistinguishable in effect from tyranny. Which closes the "give it to someone strong" road automatically—not because good people will turn bad, but because good people will succeed. Tolkien does not argue this. He stages it, five times:

Gandalfpower held out of pity, in order to do goodrefuses, and will not even touch it—he says he would begin from compassion

One object, five offers, five answers; this table is the whole of the book's definition of the word power.

Rule four: it has a will. It chooses its moments to be lost, makes itself heavy, slides off a finger at the worst time—it wants to go back to its owner. The bearer is not looking after an object; he is travelling with something that is awake.

Rule five: Sauron's blind spot. Sauron understands domination and nothing else, so every scenario available to him is "somebody has the Ring, is training it, and will come to contest this position." The thought that a finder might want to destroy it is not one he can produce—not because he is stupid, but because the option does not exist in his universe. That blind spot is what the Council bets on: hand the most valuable thing in the world to the weakest person and send it by the least sensible route.

Honestly, some links are literary need rather than logical result. Hobbit resistance to corruption (Bilbo holds it for sixty years without much harm) is a strength the author set, not one derived from the rules. "Why not fly the eagles to Mount Doom" has been asked for seventy years, and the in-book answers remain retrospective defenses rather than design. And the last step belongs to no plan at all. Tolkien did not regard that as luck—his world has a providence in it that never appears on stage, and victory comes from someone having walked to the end while past bearing, with the remainder not being humanity's department. That is the novel's structure of belief, not its physics; accept it and the book stands, decline it and Mount Doom is an author rolling dice at the decisive moment.

What it makes you think about

Question one
The enemy's weapon is right there and your city is bleeding. Is refusing it principle, or buying your own clean hands with other people's lives?

Take Boromir's side first, and take it at full strength, because that is how Tolkien wrote it. Gondor has stood at the river for centuries on behalf of everyone west of it; his men die on that line daily. Now there is an object that could turn the war tonight, and a set of wise persons who live in forests and valleys and have never had a village burned tell him: don't touch it, it corrodes the soul. His reply is strong—your corrosion is a possibility, my losing the line is a fact currently in progress; to override a certain death toll in the thousands with a hypothetical internal risk to one individual is itself a species of arrogance, and the person proposing it is never the one who pays for it. He even has precedent: humans have always survived by taking the enemy's weapons—the bow, gunpowder, radar. Why is this one different?

The other side's answer is not a moral lecture but a technical judgment: this weapon's function is its side effect. A bow is neutral and can be put down afterward; the Ring's entire content is domination, and it has no separable phases called "using it" and "being changed by it." Tolkien runs a cold check on the claim in the plot—Saruman is the sample who took that road to the end. He studied Sauron's methods, copied his organization and replicated his industry, all in order to oppose Sauron more effectively; by the time he was ready he was a smaller Sauron, still standing there thinking of himself as the resistance.

The domestic version is everywhere: emergency powers extended temporarily, the tactics you learn in order to beat a competitor, the monitoring installed to protect a team. Every instance is as justified as Boromir's and every instance genuinely works; the question was never "will I abuse this," it is "what shape will this tool have made of the organization using it, ten years on." Tolkien never says Boromir was wrong. He simply lets him die before he works out what he was wrong about—and hands his brother the same question, which the brother gets right.

Question two
He failed at the finish and the errand succeeded anyway. Should a person be judged by the outcome, by the will, or by what he did before he reached his limit?

The severe case first, put harder than its advocates would: he didn't throw it. Every mile, every corpse, every body that took a blade for him existed to get him to that spot to perform that one motion, and one second before it he announced the thing was his. He did not merely fail; he changed sides—"the Ring is mine" is Sauron's grammar. Had a lunatic not happened to be there, Middle-earth would now have a new dark lord named Frodo Baggins. And a moral ledger that cancels a betrayal because the outcome came out well is a ledger in which luck substitutes for virtue.

The defense is equally hard. Tolkien took the charge seriously in his letters, and his position in substance is that a person is judged by whether he has given everything he had to give; that at that place, at that hour, Frodo's will had been ground flat by an external force, and it was no longer a choice because there were no longer options. Defining someone by his conduct past the limit is itself a form of cheating—what belongs to him is the many days of crawling that got him to the limit. And he did make a free, decisive choice, only not at the fire: he declined, again and again, to kill Gollum. He could not throw the Ring away, but he preserved the creature that threw it away for him.

The everyday scale: the moment a person under long pressure finally goes—drinks, breaks a promise, says the unsayable thing, walks out of the job. Does that define him? "He held until the last day" and "he broke" are two ways of recording the same event, and which one you use often decides whether someone gets to be a person again. Tolkien has all of Middle-earth use the first. He is also honest enough to write that Frodo uses the second—which is why Frodo cannot stay in the Shire.

Question three
Mercy saved the world once. Is it a strategy you can rely on, or a gamble that happened to pay, staked on other people's safety?

The case against is concrete: sparing Gollum produces a chain of disasters. He is captured and talks, which sends the Riders to the Shire; he follows for months and, at the worst possible moment, walks two people into Shelob's lair. Every act of "I couldn't bring myself to" converts into risk carried by someone other than the person who felt merciful. Scaled up: those advocating forgiveness are usually not those absorbing the consequences. A parole, a charge not pressed, one more chance—the benefit accrues to the giver's moral feelings and the cost is distributed at random to the next victim. And the reason this book can demonstrate that mercy works is that its author arranged for it to work. In life nobody has staged a Gollum at the ending.

The strongest version of the case for is not "mercy is repaid." It is the reverse. The core of Gandalf's argument is epistemic humility, not a prediction of return: you do not know what is still coming, you cannot assess what threads this creature is still attached to across the whole web, and therefore you are not entitled to substitute your judgment and carry out something irreversible. The argument does not depend on the outcome—if Gollum had destroyed everything, "you had no way of knowing" would still be true. Pity in this novel is not a shrewd investment; it is a rule about the boundaries of human judgment. That it occasionally saves the world is incidental. Brought home: it is permissible to give up on the relative or colleague who wrecks everything repeatedly, but be clear that the reason is "I can no longer carry this," not "I have established he will never change"—the second dresses up your own limit as a verdict on someone else.

Question four
"The least important people decided history"—an insight about power, or a flattering lie told to the powerless?

The skeptical side is well armed: in reality large outcomes are settled by large forces—capital, armies, institutions, technology. Telling ordinary people that history is in their hands is rousing and tends in practice to keep them seated, because it converts a structural problem into a question of personal character. Worse, it carries a conservative comfort: it asks nobody to change how power is distributed, only that small people behave nobly at the decisive moment. And the two hobbits entrusted with the errand are a country gentleman and his gardener—the gardener calls his employer "Mr. Frodo" from first page to last, and that relation is never once questioned.

The other side's argument is not sociological but structural, and it is sturdy: when a thing's only property is domination, the only person who can hold it is a person with no plans to scale anything up. That is not a moral bonus, it is a job requirement. Gandalf, Galadriel and Aragorn are all far more capable than Frodo, and being capable is exactly what would activate the thing in their hands. Ability is a liability here. Translated into the present tense: the question is never "who is most competent to hold this access," it is "who least wants the access widened"—the root password, the final signature, the key to what an organization can decide. Handing it to the most capable and most ambitious person is generally where the trouble starts.

Tolkien's personal footnote sits here too: he remarked in his letters that Sam owes a great deal to the privates and batmen he knew in the 1914 war. A man back from the Somme writing that wars are decided by people with no names is not making an inspirational remark. It is closer to late testimony.

The recurring subject: what Tolkien kept writing

One obsession runs through everything he wrote: time moves in the direction of loss, and the right way to live is to hold the position knowing you will lose it. Galadriel calls it the long defeat—every victory the elves have won across the ages still sums to a downward curve. The ending completes the curve: Sauron falls, and the price is that the three elven rings fail with him, the elvish age ends, and Galadriel and Elrond have to leave. The content of the victory is the loss of the thing that was being defended.

The second line is death. In his long letter to a publisher and repeatedly afterward, Tolkien said the real subject of the book is not power but death and the desire for deathlessness. Middle-earth writes that into biology: elves do not die, and pay by remaining in the world through all its wearing-down until they are weary of it; Men die, and in his mythology that is called the Gift of Men—a gift nobody wants to accept. The Nazgûl are the extreme form of the theme: nine people who wanted not to die, got it, and are consequently no longer anybody.

Against his other work: The Hobbit (1937) is the same world in a light register, where the ring is a convenient prop; The Silmarillion (edited by his son Christopher, published 1977) tells an older version of the same story—Fëanor makes jewels of unbearable beauty and then drags an entire people into centuries of blood feud to keep them, which is the Ring in another key: the finer the craft, the easier it is to fall in love with what you made, and the easier to destroy everything for it. Leaf by Niggle (1945) is about a painter who never finishes his tree; Smith of Wootton Major (1967) is about a man who has to give the star back. He wrote one thing all his life: what you value most has to be released in the end, and the only variable is whether it is taken from you or you open your hand.

Criticism and controversy

  • Critical hostility. In 1956 Edmund Wilson published a famous demolition in The Nation, "Oo, Those Awful Orcs!", calling it juvenile trash. In 1978 Michael Moorcock's essay "Epic Pooh" attacked it as consoling, imagination-deadening nostalgia. When a 1997 British readers' poll named it book of the century, critics including Germaine Greer said publicly and forcefully what they thought of that.
  • Very few women. The Fellowship is entirely male; the number of women with lines is small; Arwen's story proper is in the appendices. Éowyn is the most fully drawn, and her wish to fight is written seriously (half courage, half the despair of being told to stay behind)—but her ending, laying down arms, marrying Faramir, declaring she will be a healer, has long been read as a retreat. The defense notes that the turn comes after she has struck the most important blow in the book and that she chooses it herself, and that Aragorn renounces the temptation to rule and Faramir renounces the Ring—laying down is not assigned exclusively to the woman. The argument is unresolved.
  • Race. Orcs are constructed as a species irredeemable in the aggregate; the Men of the South and East are described as darker-skinned and aligned with evil; geographically west is good and east is not. The criticism of that structure is long-standing and serious. The other half must also be stated plainly: on the record Tolkien was explicitly anti-racist—in 1938 he drafted a reply to a German publisher refusing to certify his "Aryan" descent, and in letters to his son he described Hitler in extremely unflattering terms. "The author opposed racism" and "the text's structure distributes good and evil along racialized lines" can both be true, and using either to cancel the other is lazy. He was himself aware that an irredeemable species does not work inside his own theology, and worried at it repeatedly in his letters.
  • Hard going, and too clean a moral scheme. Songs and verse in quantity, archaic syntax, an entire chapter that is a meeting, geography by the page, plus a hundred-odd pages of appendices. The charge of moral dualism is common too; the rebuttals are in the book—Boromir, Denethor, Saruman, Gríma, Gollum, and above all the protagonist's failure—but those complications are all on the human side of the line, and the orcs are indeed granted none of it.
  • Politically claimed from both ends—and his own limit. It has been read by the right as a hymn to hierarchy and restored monarchy, and adopted by the 1960s counterculture as an anti-industrial, anti-establishment banner ("Frodo Lives" was a real piece of graffiti). Tolkien himself wrote to his son that his political leanings ran toward anarchy in the philosophical sense, or unconstitutional monarchy—neither camp fits him well. In the second-edition foreword he denied outright that the book is an allegory of anything, the Ring-as-atomic-bomb reading in particular, and offered a counterfactual: had it been an allegory, the Ring would have been seized and used to rule, not destroyed. He preferred applicability to allegory: the first belongs to the reader's freedom, the second to the author's wish to dominate. Which is that book's own theme, applied to itself.
  • The films are not the book. Peter Jackson's 2001–2003 trilogy shaped most people's memory of this story, and it departs substantially: the Scouring of the Shire is cut entirely (it is the book's ethical landing point), Faramir lets them go on the spot and never marches Frodo to Osgiliath, Frodo never sends Sam away on the stairs, the Army of the Dead stops at the southern harbor and does not appear on the Pelennor, and Saruman dies at the door of Bag End rather than at his tower. Discussing the novel from the films often means discussing a different work.
  • Translation matters more than usual. Tolkien wrote a guide to the names for translators, because early foreign editions displeased him. Anyone arguing about how a particular name should be rendered is arguing about something the author himself cared about intensely.

Ten sentences to take away

1. The fuse is not Sauron forging the Ring; it is the stroke Bilbo did not make in the dark—one withheld knife that is both the first link in the catastrophe and the only way out of it.

2. The Ring's power comes in exactly one form: do as I say. What it offers each person differs; the grammar never does.

3. "Give it to someone strong" is closed off not because good people turn bad, but because good people succeed—benevolence carried through unobstructed is tyranny in effect.

4. Saruman is the sample who took that road to the end: he imitated Sauron in order to oppose Sauron more effectively, and by the time he was ready he was a smaller Sauron who still thought he was resisting.

5. Sauron loses on a single thought he cannot produce—that someone who gets it might want it gone.

6. The protagonist fails at the finish: he does not throw it, he puts it on. Six books of preparation for a sacrifice, and then the sacrifice does not happen.

7. So the victory is earned by nobody. Three acts of mercy left standing in the world catch the fall.

8. The Witch-king is killed by two people who had been told they had no business on a battlefield: a woman lands the last stroke, and a small person cuts the spell with a dagger issued three books earlier.

9. The content of this book's victory is the loss of what was defended: Sauron falls, the elvish age ends with him, Galadriel has to leave.

10. It refuses to stop at the coronation and shrinks all the way back to a kitchen, so the last line can be spoken by the one who got home—and its weight comes from the one standing beside it who did not.
