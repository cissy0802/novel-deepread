Novels, Deep-Read · Eileen Chang · No.82

Love in a Fallen City

Eileen Chang · 1943 · a novella

中文 →

Shanghai, an old family mansion. The rest of the city has set its clocks an hour ahead to save daylight; this household won't bother. They keep to the old time — their ten o'clock is everyone else's eleven.
Late at night, Fourth Master sits on the dark, shabby balcony playing the huqin, a two-stringed fiddle. Downstairs, the family has just finished tearing into the Sixth Miss. She goes upstairs, switches on the light, and goes straight to the full-length mirror. Twenty-eight, divorced seven or eight years, living on her brothers' charity after they spent her money. The face in the glass hasn't aged yet.
The fiddle keeps playing. She lifts a hand, turns, takes a few steps, the way an actress moves on an opera stage — and then she smiles. It isn't a pretty smile. It is dim and sly, the smile of someone who has just made up her mind.

This page contains complete spoilers — what Bai Liusu is after on each of her two trips to Hong Kong, why Fan Liuyuan won't marry her, what happens at the foot of that wall, and who finally settles the matter for them. The ending of this story is its title: unless you know how the city falls, you can't really argue about whether this marriage is love.

In one sentence

A divorced Shanghai woman whom her own family treats as dead weight and a rich, English-raised playboy who refuses to take anything seriously fight a long war of nerves in Hong Kong, each waiting for the other to show their hand first — until Japanese shells land on the colony and the fall of a city settles the account for them: they marry, and for one moment they actually mean it.

Coordinates

Eileen Chang (Zhang Ailing, 1920–1995) was twenty-three when she wrote this. When Japan attacked Hong Kong in December 1941 she was a student at the University of Hong Kong and lived through the eighteen-day siege. Back in occupied Shanghai in 1942, she erupted: "Aloeswood Incense," "The Golden Cangue," and Love in a Fallen City appeared in quick succession in magazines, and within a year she was the most talked-about writer in the city. The novella ran in the monthly Zazhi in the autumn of 1943 and was collected the following year in her book Romances (Chuanqi); she also adapted it for the Shanghai stage. The standard English version is Karen S. Kingsbury's translation.

Its place in literary history is odd. Most Chinese fiction of the period was about national salvation, class, awakening. Chang took a war that redrew the map of Asia and cast it as a matchmaker. The title plays on an old idiom: in a Han-dynasty song, a beauty glances back once and a city topples. Here the causation runs backwards — first the city falls, and that is what makes the woman.

The story

Act One · The Bai mansion, running on the old clock

Foreshadow ①The story opens to the sound of the fiddle. Fourth Master plays it on the balcony, creaking and wheezing, and the narrator tells us it plays a desolate tale with no end. The Bais are a decayed old-style Shanghai family: the rules are intact, the money is gone. Bai Liusu is the Sixth Miss. Seven or eight years ago she married a man who beat her; she insisted on a divorce and came home with her share of the settlement. Her brothers "invested" it, and in a few years there was nothing left. Since then she has been the mouth the household feeds for nothing.

Tonight Third Master, Fourth Master and Fourth Mistress corner her with news: her ex-husband has died. Third Master's idea is that she should go back to his family and mourn him as his widow; they might let her adopt a boy from the clan, and then she'd have someone to support her in old age. Liusu points out that they've been divorced for years — what would she even be, there? Third Master answers with the eternal verities: law changes with the times, propriety does not. Fourth Mistress joins in, and her words get uglier: you've used up our money, so why are you still here? A divorced daughter who comes home is, from start to finish, a line in the accounts.

Liusu goes upstairs to her mother and throws herself down by the old lady's bed, hoping for one fair word. Her mother only murmurs — be patient, your brothers have it hard too. Liusu understands that her mother is part of this house as well. There is no help there.

Foreshadow ②That is when she goes to the mirror. The woman in it is still young, still slim-waisted, her eyes still bright. Out on the balcony, the fiddle has reached a passage from an opera, and she follows it with stage gestures, as if performing for someone. When it's over she smiles at the glass. That smile is the moment she stops being someone things are decided for and becomes someone who is going to place a bet.

Act Two · A blind date, hijacked

The chance comes quickly. Mrs. Xu, a family acquaintance, is arranging a match for Liusu's younger sister Baoluo. The man is Fan Liuyuan, in his early thirties. His father was a famous overseas Chinese tycoon in Southeast Asia; his mother was a society girl his father met in London, and the marriage could not be acknowledged. Liuyuan grew up in England, had a hard time of it, and fought a long battle to secure his inheritance. Now he is back in China and the most sought-after son-in-law in Shanghai. While she's at it, Mrs. Xu has also kept an eye out for Liusu — a middle-aged widower trailing several children. That is the going rate for a divorced woman of twenty-eight.

On the day of the meeting nearly the whole family turns out. Fourth Mistress dresses up her own two daughters and brings them along in the hope of poaching him; Liusu is dragged along to make up the numbers. After dinner they go dancing. Baoluo, raised by the old rules, can't dance; Fourth Mistress's girls are too young. The only one who can is Liusu. Fan Liuyuan asks her for one dance, and then another.

At home, the house explodes. Baoluo sulks in silence; Fourth Mistress makes pointed remarks about how divorced women certainly know their tricks. Liusu doesn't argue. She knows she made the right move — even if nothing comes of it, everyone in that room now knows she still has a price.

Some days later Mrs. Xu comes back. She and her husband are going to Hong Kong on business; would Liusu like to come along? Tickets and hotel taken care of. Everyone understands who's paying. Liusu thinks it over all night. If she goes, she's publicly admitting she's angling for Fan Liuyuan, and her reputation is finished. If she stays, she'll slowly turn into the family's old maid. She decides to stake what little future she has left.

Act Three · Repulse Bay: a war in which no one will speak first

Fan Liuyuan meets them at the pier and installs her at the Repulse Bay Hotel, in the room next to his own. Then come days of dinners, films, dancing, walks by the sea. He is attentive, witty, considerate — and he never lays a hand on her, and never mentions marriage.

Liusu gradually works out the rules of the game. He wants her, but he doesn't want to pay the price called "marriage." She wants a marriage certificate — for a divorced woman with no income it is the only secure livelihood there is — but she cannot be seen to want it. The moment she does, she has lost, and becomes exactly the kind of woman who is after his money. Both of them are shrewd, and each is waiting for the other to blink. Meanwhile he likes to tell her she is a "real Chinese woman," that her particular talent is lowering her head, that she should always wear a qipao and never change. He grew up in England; what he wants is an imagined old China.

He makes a point of flirting, in front of her, with a woman at the hotel who calls herself an Indian princess — Saheiyini — to see if she'll get jealous. She refuses to give him the satisfaction.

Foreshadow ③One evening they walk out from the hotel and come to a wall of grey brick, tall and long, with no end in sight. Fan Liuyuan leans against it and says something unlike himself. For some reason, he says, this wall makes him think of phrases about the end of the world — when heaven grows old and the earth runs dry. Some day our whole civilization will be destroyed, burned out, bombed out, collapsed, and maybe this wall will still be standing. If we met at the foot of it then, Liusu, maybe you would be a little sincere with me. Maybe I would be a little sincere with you.

Liusu has no idea what he's talking about and takes it for another flourish. The reader is tempted to as well: a playboy at the seaside, holding forth on the death of civilization to a woman, is a bit much.

Foreshadow ④Late that night the phone in her room rings. It's Fan Liuyuan, next door. He says he loves her, and hangs up. A little later he calls again: I forgot to ask — do you love me? She doesn't answer. He calls a third time and recites a poem from the Book of Songs, the one that runs, roughly, in death or life, through meeting and parting, I hold your hand and we grow old together. It is, he says, the saddest poem he knows: life and death and separation are enormous things, and none of them is ours to decide; next to the forces outside us, people are so small. And yet we insist on saying "I'll be with you forever," as if it were up to us. Finally he asks: can you see the moon from your window?

She goes to the window. The moon is there. She cries, without knowing what she is crying about.

And in daylight, he still says nothing. Liusu does the math: she has spent more than a month in Hong Kong; the money was his, the reputation was hers. If this goes on, she'll become a woman he got without paying any bride-price at all. She decides to go back to Shanghai — to retreat in order to force his hand. Fan Liuyuan sees her politely onto the ship and doesn't say a word to keep her.

Act Four · Back to Shanghai, and back again

In Shanghai, worse talk awaits her. To the Bais, a divorced woman who went off to Hong Kong with a man for a month and came home empty-handed has simply been used and discarded. This time she has no ground to stand on. She has lost the first round, and everybody knows it.

Autumn turns to winter. A telegram arrives from Hong Kong asking her to come back. It promises nothing. Liusu knows what going means this time: not a wife but a kept woman. She goes anyway. Not because she has been persuaded, but because the family has closed off every other road.

She arrives in Hong Kong. That night he comes into her room.

Payoff ② ↑He kisses her; she loses her footing and her back meets the cold surface of the full-length mirror, and the two of them seem to tumble into the mirror, into a dim other world, cold where it is cold and burning where it burns, like the flame-red blossoms they had seen on their walks, setting her alight. That mirror in Act One was where she rehearsed alone. Here the same object returns: the play finally has a partner, but by this point she has pushed all her chips into the middle. The Shanghai mirror showed a woman deciding to bet; the Hong Kong mirror shows her having bet herself. Chang doesn't let her win here. The glass is cold.

Fan Liuyuan rents her a house on the hillside and hires servants. A week later he is to sail for England on business, return date unspecified. She understands that this is now her place: a house, a man who comes back when he comes back, and no standing of any kind. He leaves, and she is alone in a house that isn't even fully furnished.

Act Five · The eighth of December

His ship never sails. The war gets there first. On 8 December 1941, almost simultaneously with Pearl Harbor, Japanese shells begin falling on Hong Kong. Liusu is alone in the empty house on the hill, listening to the guns, with nowhere to go. Before long, Fan Liuyuan comes back — the ship can't leave, and he has come for her. They retreat together to the Repulse Bay Hotel, where British troops are posted, which makes it a target.

What follows is a dozen-odd days without daylight. The hotel takes shellfire; the guests huddle downstairs; the food runs lower each day. Nobody has any appetite left for flirtation, and nobody has anything left to bargain with. On Christmas Day, Hong Kong surrenders.

They walk back up to the house on the hill. It has been ransacked, everything strewn about and coated in dust. That night a great wind blows, moaning through the empty rooms. Liusu lies awake.

Payoff ③ ↑She thinks of the grey brick wall near Repulse Bay. He'd said that when civilization had been destroyed, maybe only the wall would remain, and then there might be a little sincerity. She had taken it for a pretty line. Now civilization really has been destroyed in front of her — money, property, everything that was meant to last forever counts for nothing; all she can rely on is the breath in her own chest and the man lying beside her. What he said at the foot of that wall wasn't a seduction line after all. It was a prophecy — only neither the speaker nor the listener believed it at the time.

Payoff ④ ↑Suddenly she crawls over and puts her arms around him through his quilt. He reaches a hand out from under the covers and takes hers. The "I hold your hand" he recited over the phone that night has become a physical act, with no poetry in it at all — just two hands meeting in a cold room. And there's a further twist in the poem itself: in the Book of Songs it is a soldier's lament, sung by a man at war, which is why it says life and parting are not ours to decide. In peacetime, Fan Liuyuan quoted a war poem to sigh that people have no say over their fate; then an actual war came and decided for them. The narrator says that in that instant they saw each other through and through — just one instant of complete understanding, but enough for them to live together in harmony for ten years, perhaps eight.

Soon after, Fan Liuyuan proposes they announce their marriage in the newspaper. The two of them return to Shanghai.

Act Six · Coda: the mosquito-coil tray

Married, Fan Liuyuan is a different man. He no longer banters with Liusu — he saves his witty remarks for other women. She feels a twinge of loss, but only a twinge. The Bais are suddenly polite to her. Later Fourth Mistress starts talking about divorcing Fourth Master, and the family mutters behind her back that Liusu has set a bad example.

Then the narrator steps back and delivers the story's cruelest line. The fall of Hong Kong had made Liusu's fortune; but in this incomprehensible world, who can say what is cause and what is effect? Perhaps a great city was toppled just so that she could be made whole. Tens of thousands of people died, tens of thousands suffered, and then came upheaval that shook heaven and earth — and Liusu doesn't feel there is anything special about her place in history. She simply gets up, smiling, and kicks the mosquito-coil tray under the table.

Payoff ① ↑In the last paragraph the fiddle starts up again: on a night of ten thousand lamps it saws back and forth, an endless desolate tale — better not to ask. The fiddle at the start was Fourth Master's, on a crumbling balcony, accompanying a woman under attack by her whole family. At the end it returns to accompany a woman who has won. Same instrument, same tune; one a loss, one a victory; and they sound like the same desolation. That framing closes the whole story inside a box: put back into that wheezing music, Liusu's triumph rings no louder than her defeat.

The fuse, in one paragraph: what actually lights this story is not Fan Liuyuan's charm, and not the Japanese shells, but that family meeting downstairs in the Bai mansion — a household that books a divorced woman as a liability and drives her to the mirror, where she decides to put herself up as her last chip. The blind date, the two trips south, the words at the wall, the midnight calls are all aftershocks of that one wager. The war simply happened to overturn the whole table just as she was about to lose. The city fell for her sake — the line is ironic, and for Liusu it is also literally true.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion one · The mirror and the fiddle

Replay it: the quarrel downstairs has just ended. Liusu goes up alone, turns on the light, looks in the mirror. On the balcony Fourth Master is playing; the fiddle tells the old operatic stories of loyalty, filial piety and chaste widowhood. She moves to the music in front of the glass, then smiles.

Why does it hit so hard? Because Chang doesn't give Liusu a single line of interior thought here. The whole preceding stretch is other people talking about her — Third Master's propriety, Fourth Mistress's bookkeeping, her mother's evasions. When it's her turn, all the author gives us is action: a look, a gesture, a smile. We have to read her mind from her body. And the soundtrack is perfect: the fiddle is playing tales of chastity and loyalty, precisely the moral code that wants her to go back and keep faith with a dead man. At first she drifts into the stage poses almost involuntarily, like someone well trained by that code. But after the smile, the narrator tells us, the fiddle plays on — and its distant tales of loyalty and virtue no longer have anything to do with her. A woman walks out of a piece of music, and does it without a word. That sly smile turns an object of pity, in one stroke, into someone slightly frightening.

Slow motion two · Three phone calls and a moon

Replay it: deep in the night, through one wall, the phone rings again and again. First he says love; then he asks about love; then he recites a poem and asks about the moon.

The power comes from the design of distance. Only a wall separates them, yet he insists on telephoning — and that is their whole relationship in miniature: he could knock, but he speaks through a wire; he could propose, but he speaks through a poem two and a half thousand years old. The three calls warm up and then pull away: "I love you" is the most direct, but he hangs up at once; "do you love me?" kicks the ball back to her; on the third call he retreats to the farthest possible place — ancient verse, life and death, the moon — and that is when he is most moving. The moment he is sincere, he can only borrow someone else's words. Liusu weeps at the window and the narrator doesn't explain why. We know that when the sun comes up, none of it will count.

Slow motion three · The windy night, through a quilt

Replay it: the siege is over, the house is empty, the wind is howling. She remembers the wall, crawls over and holds him; he reaches out and takes her hand.

Why does it work? Because it is the smallest gesture in the story, set against the largest backdrop. When Chang writes the shelling her tone is almost cool, the casualties dispatched in a phrase. Here she suddenly slows down: the wind, the quilt, a hand emerging from the covers. And right behind "complete understanding" comes a qualification so exact it's chilling: one instant, good for ten years, perhaps eight. Not a lifetime — ten years, perhaps eight. That's Chang's kind of honesty: she grants these two real feeling, but feeling has a shelf life, and she has calculated it for you. Romance and calculation in the same sentence — that is the texture of the whole book.

The book's core devices

Love in a Fallen City isn't a high-concept novel. Its signature isn't an idea; it's in structure and tone. Take it apart and you find three things.

First: a love affair written as a negotiation. For most of its length, both protagonists are doing the same thing — appraising. Liusu estimates what Fan will pay (marriage or a kept arrangement); Fan estimates what Liusu is after (him or his money). Chang's vocabulary is drawn from the market and the gambling table: stakes, chips, winning and losing, not worth it. This isn't mockery of love. It is an accurate account of the material conditions of courtship for a woman without economic independence in the 1940s. Liusu cannot "just want love," because love doesn't pay for dinner.

Second: an outside force that settles the negotiation. Two people this shrewd would stalemate forever — whoever speaks first loses. Chang needs something bigger than both of them to break the equilibrium, and she chooses the Battle of Hong Kong. Narratively it's a deus ex machina (in Greek drama, the god lowered on a crane to untangle a plot nobody else can), but she handles it honestly: the war doesn't make them better people; it just strips them, for a while, of anything to bargain with. In her 1944 essay "Writing of One's Own" she was blunt about it: the war didn't turn Liusu into a revolutionary woman or Fan into a saint; their ending was more or less healthy, and still vulgar — taken on its own terms, it could only have gone that way.

Third: a frame of desolation. A fiddle at the start, a fiddle at the end; a mansion that runs an hour slow at the start, a smiling kick at a mosquito-coil tray at the end. In the same essay Chang said she disliked the heroic and the "stirring," and preferred what she called uneven contrast — not clean black-and-white of good and evil, success and failure, but good shot through with bad, winning shot through with losing, sincerity shot through with calculation. This frame guarantees that Liusu's victory can never be read as a happy ending. She wins a marriage, and the author drops the win back into an endless desolate tune, so that you laugh and then feel a slight chill.

One more device that's easy to miss: the title is an inverted allusion. A "city-toppling" beauty is one so lovely that cities fall for her. Here cause and effect are reversed: the city falls, and that is what makes the woman. The narrator's line — perhaps a great city was toppled just so she could be made whole — is said with a straight face, putting one woman's wedding and a disaster for millions in the same sentence. It's absurd, and because it's absurd it captures something small and hard about the individual in the face of history.

What it forces you to think about

One · Liusu wants a meal ticket. Is it still love?

From beginning to end Liusu wants one thing: marriage. Not because she adores Fan Liuyuan, but because she is twenty-eight, divorced, without income, and her family is about to push her out. Every move she makes — the dances at the blind date, the trip to Hong Kong, the strategic retreat to Shanghai — is calculated.

First, the strongest case that this is a transaction, not love. Love is prized because it's supposed to be for nothing: I want you, not what you can give me. Liusu's feelings come with a price tag from the start; what she cares about is a certificate, a house, being able to hold her head up in front of the Bais. If that counts as love, the word means nothing more than "a long-term contract both parties have agreed to." Fan's own line — maybe I'd be a little sincere with you — admits it: in peacetime there was no sincerity between them.

The other side is just as hard: only people who never worry about food and shelter get to demand that love be for nothing. Liusu has no profession, no property, no legal protection; in her world a woman's entire social safety net hangs on marriage. Asking her to love "purely" is asking her to bet her life. And her calculation isn't empty of real feeling — the tears under the moon, the hand reaching across in the windy night, weren't calculated. Maybe calculation and sincerity are never either/or, only a matter of sequence, proportion, and circumstance.

Where does Chang stand? Nowhere, deliberately. She lets Liusu win and calls the win vulgar; she grants real feeling and says it lasted an instant. Bring it home: have you ever weighed a partner's income, residence status, apartment? After weighing them, do you still feel entitled to say you did it for love? Or put it another way: could you accept someone who loves you and has also done the math on you?

Two · Fan won't marry her: is refusing the contract more honest, or more selfish?

Fan Liuyuan's position is clear: he wants Liusu, but not marriage. He comes close to saying outright that he wants her to love him for himself, not for the position of Mrs. Fan.

Let's make his case as strongly as possible. A rich man spends his life being calculated upon: his father's family fought him for the money, Shanghai's matrons compete to land him as a son-in-law, and with every woman who smiles at him he has to guess what she's really after. He won't buy feeling with a marriage because once the contract is signed, he can never again know whether she means it. A relationship without a contract has to be renewed by both people's will every single day — in a sense that's a higher standard, not a lower one. Plenty of couples today choose to live together without marrying on exactly these grounds: a certificate proves nothing about love, only how the assets get divided.

The other side: going without a contract is a luxury of the strong. Without marriage Fan can walk away at any time and lose nothing; once Liusu becomes his kept woman she has lost her reputation, her fallback, everything she'll need when she's old. "No paper, just sincerity" weighs completely differently coming from a rich man than from a divorced woman. What he calls testing her sincerity is in practice shifting the entire risk onto her. And the ending gives it away: only once the war has flattened his advantages too does he offer to sign.

None of this has dated: prenups, property rights for cohabiting couples, "we don't need a piece of paper." Every time you hear that last line, it's worth asking: if this falls apart, what does the person saying it stand to lose?

Three · A city's catastrophe makes one couple: is happiness in the ruins a kind of coldness?

Hong Kong falls; the dead and wounded are beyond counting. Liusu gets married because of it, and ends the story smiling as she kicks a mosquito-coil tray. The novella never has her shed a tear for anyone else.

First, the strongest case that this is callous. One person's happiness built on thousands of deaths — even if she did nothing to cause them, accepting it calmly is a kind of moral obtuseness. Literature has a duty to see the dead; making a real massacre of a city into a matchmaker turns other people's blood into a backdrop. In a time of national catastrophe, writing that cares only about its own little affair is hard to read as anything but frivolous.

The other side: in a great upheaval, the only thing ordinary people can hold on to is their own little affair. Liusu cannot save Hong Kong, and her grief would help no one; what she can do, when nothing else holds, is hold on to the one person beside her. Chang herself lived through that siege, and in her essay "From the Ashes" she wrote about how people in the lulls between shelling ate ravenously, fell in love, got married — not because they didn't know others were dying, but precisely because they knew they could die any moment, so they held on tighter. Faulting a woman in the ruins for insufficient patriotic grief may be its own kind of heartlessness.

Bring it home: a pandemic, a wave of layoffs, a market crash — someone always finds opportunity in other people's losses: a move to a new city, a partner, a cheap apartment. Do you feel guilty about it? Should you? Chang's answer is hidden in that line about not knowing cause from effect: she neither defends Liusu nor judges her. She just makes you watch the kick.

Motifs: what Chang kept writing about

Chang's recurring obsession sits at the intersection of two relationships: women and money, and people and their times. How a woman without economic independence prices herself on the marriage market; and how history, however cataclysmic, tends to land on the individual as a meal, a dress, a wedding.

On that line, Love in a Fallen City is the rare story at the "winning" end. "The Golden Cangue," published the same year, is its photographic negative: Cao Qiqiao trades her youth for gold, the gold becomes a cangue around her neck, and she passes her misery down to her children — the hell that follows a successful calculation. In "Aloeswood Incense: The First Brazier" another Shanghai girl comes to Hong Kong — Ge Weilong — but she is softer than Liusu, is swallowed bit by bit by the luxury of her aunt's house, and ends up willingly selling herself. Later, "Red Rose, White Rose" switches to a man's point of view: a man who ruins two women between his ideal wife and his passionate lover. "Lust, Caution" gives us a woman who feels something real for a man at the last moment and pays with her life.

Seen together, Love in a Fallen City is the nearest Chang came to comedy — a rare case where she lets a woman's gamble pay off. Even here, though, she sets a desolate fiddle playing at the end to remind you that the payoff required a city to be buried alongside it.

Controversy and criticism

"Ornament over bone." The earliest and weightiest critique came in 1944 from the translator Fu Lei, writing under the pen name Xun Yu, in his essay "On Eileen Chang's Fiction." He praised "The Golden Cangue" extravagantly but had reservations about this one: roughly, that it relies too much on wit and repartee, that the duel between the two leads stays at the level of flirtation and sparring, that the characters lack deeper inner conflict, and that the story's weight can't carry so vast a backdrop. Chang replied with "Writing of One's Own," defending her "unthorough" characters and her uneven contrasts. That exchange remains one of the best ways into the novella: is its lightness a flaw, or the whole point?

The war as stage scenery. Another critique holds that the story turns a real war, with real mass death, into a catalyst for one couple, while the suffering of ordinary Hong Kong people is almost absent. Chang's defenders say that's exactly the perspective — she's writing a woman who can see only her own small patch. Critics answer that the author's view and the character's view are different things, and the author was free to see further.

Two readings of the heroine. Some see a shrewd gold-digger who lands on her feet through cunning and luck; others see a woman cornered by an old-style family who breaks out on her own, and argue that her "calculation" is the only form of resistance available to her. Both readings find support in the text.

The author's own situation. The verifiable facts: the novella was published in Japanese-occupied Shanghai. In 1944 Chang married Hu Lancheng, who at the time held office in Wang Jingwei's collaborationist government. After the war she faced some public criticism for this, and in the 1946 expanded edition of Romances she included a short preface addressed to her readers in her own defense. After moving to Hong Kong in the 1950s she wrote The Rice-Sprout Song and Naked Earth, the latter commissioned by the United States Information Service. These facts are often brought into discussions of her work; they are listed here without verdict.

Adaptations versus the original. In 1984 Ann Hui filmed it with Chow Yun-fat and Cora Miao, and there have been stage and television versions since. When discussing the novella, be careful not to mistake their additions and changes for the original — the source is only a novella, and much of it was expanded or altered in adaptation.

Ten lines to take with you

① A family living by the old clock: their ten o'clock is everyone else's eleven — the Bai mansion even runs an hour behind the world.

② A divorced daughter who comes home is, first of all, a deficit on the books.

③ The smile in the mirror is the moment someone things are done to becomes someone who places bets.

④ He wants her to love him, not his money; she wants a certificate, because love doesn't pay for dinner — both are right, so neither will speak first.

⑤ He phones through a wall and tells the truth through a poem two thousand years old: the moment he's sincere, he has to borrow someone else's mouth.

⑥ "Maybe there'd be a little sincerity," said at the foot of the wall — he meant it as a line, she heard it as a flourish, and it was a prophecy.

⑦ The war didn't make them better; it just left them, for a while, with nothing to bargain with.

⑧ One instant of complete understanding, good for ten years, perhaps eight — Chang even works out the shelf life of sincerity for you.

⑨ The city fell for her sake: it's irony, and for Liusu it's also the literal truth.

⑩ The same fiddle opens and closes the story; one a loss, one a win, and they sound like the same desolation.
