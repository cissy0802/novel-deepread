Novels, Deep-Read · Gabriel García Márquez · No.97

Love in the Time of Cholera

El amor en los tiempos del cólera · Gabriel García Márquez · 1985 · novel in six parts

中文 →

Pentecost Sunday, the rain just over. A parrot that speaks French and can recite the Mass has escaped into the mango tree in the courtyard, and Dr. Juvenal Urbino, eighty-one years old, climbs a ladder and reaches for it. The ladder slips. He falls into the mud.
That night, when the last mourners have left the wake, one man stays behind: seventy-six, in an old-fashioned black suit, hat pressed to his heart. He tells the new widow that he has waited more than half a century for this chance, to swear to her once again his eternal fidelity and everlasting love.
She tells him to get out, and not to show his face again for whatever years he has left.
It has been fifty-one years, nine months, and four days since she first turned him away.

This piece contains complete spoilers: why Fermina stopped loving him in a single glance in a crowded market, how the man she married shared half a century with her, how many women Florentino slept with while he waited, and why the last boat in the book never docks. The novel begins at the end; knowing where it goes is what lets you see each step.

In one sentence

A poor telegraph boy of about eighteen falls for a thirteen-year-old girl, is rejected, and vows to wait; he watches her marry the most respectable doctor in the city and live out more than fifty years of marriage, and the night the doctor dies he renews his vow — then, with a year of letters and one river voyage, he finally wins her when both are past seventy. It is a novel about constancy that never stops puncturing the idea of constancy, and both halves are true.

Coordinates

Gabriel García Márquez (1927–2014), Colombian, author of One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967), won the Nobel Prize in 1982. This was his first novel after the prize, published in 1985. He said openly that the young lovers' story borrows from his own parents' courtship: his father was a small-town telegraph operator, and his mother's father opposed the match and sent her off on a long journey to forget.

It holds an unusual place in his work: there is almost no magic in it — it is his most realist book. The setting is an unnamed colonial port on the Caribbean (readers see Cartagena in it), and the time runs roughly from the 1870s to the 1930s.

The story

Act One · Bitter almonds and a ladder: how an old man ended a marriage

The first sentence belongs to Dr. Urbino's mind (in substance): the scent of bitter almonds always reminded him of the fate of thwarted love. Seed ①He smells it in a photographer's studio. His chess partner, Jeremiah de Saint-Amour, a wheelchair-bound refugee from the Antilles, has killed himself overnight with the fumes of gold cyanide. The suicide letter knocks the doctor off balance twice: his dignified old friend was an escaped convict from the penal colony in French Guiana, and he had long ago resolved to die at sixty, and never be an old man. He also had a secret lover, who knew the plan, spent his last night with him, and then let him go.

Urbino is the weightiest man in the city: trained in Paris, he came home and drove cholera out with drains, running water, and quarantine; his own father died in the previous epidemic. At eighty-one he still sees patients, lectures, and attends every ceremony, his life running like a clock. That day he comes home from a student's celebratory lunch, soaked by rain, just as the parrot he personally taught to talk escapes into the mango tree. His wife, Fermina Daza, runs into the courtyard. Lying in the mud, he has time for one sentence: "Only God knows how much I loved you" (Part One). They have been married half a century.

Among the mourners that night is a seventy-six-year-old man who runs the river shipping company: Florentino Ariza. When everyone else has gone, he stays and speaks the vow from the opening. Fermina, seventy-two, does not faint and does not weep; she shakes with rage, orders him out, and adds that she hopes his remaining years are few. Then she goes to her bedroom, sleeps alone in that bed for the first time, cries herself to sleep — and wakes to find that the person she is thinking about most is not her dead husband but the man she threw out.

Payoff ① ↑Only now does the first sentence reveal whom it was about. Following the doctor's nose, we assumed "thwarted love" meant the photographer. But his love was not thwarted at all: he had a lover, an agreement, and an ending he chose himself. The love that really had been blocked for half a century was standing at the doctor's own funeral, waiting for this day. García Márquez has the unsuspecting husband open the book on behalf of his rival. And the photographer who refused to live past sixty is the novel's exact opposite: the whole book is about love after seventy.

Act Two · The Crowned Goddess: telegrams, letters, and a little park, fifty years earlier

The novel steps back more than fifty years. Florentino is illegitimate. His mother, Tránsito Ariza, runs a notions shop and quietly lends money against pawned jewelry; his father, one of the owners of the river company, never acknowledged him and died when the boy was ten. Florentino is an apprentice at the telegraph office — thin, shy, fond of love poetry, a violinist.

One day he delivers a telegram to Lorenzo Daza, a mule trader newly arrived from the interior. Crossing the house, he passes the sewing room and sees a girl teaching her aunt to read. The girl looks up at whoever is passing the window. That one glance settles the next fifty years.

After that he sits every day on a bench in the little park across from her house, pretending to read, waiting for her and her aunt to walk past on the way to school. He writes a love letter some seventy pages long, then cuts it down. He gets lovesick in the most physical way — diarrhea, green vomit, dizziness, cold sweats. Seed ②His frightened mother consults an old homeopath, who sees at once what's going on: the symptoms of love are the same as the symptoms of cholera. His mother is not worried. She's almost pleased: her son has learned to love.

Aunt Escolástica takes pity on the two children and carries their letters back and forth. At night he stands near the cemetery, upwind of her window, and plays a waltz he composed for her on the violin, Seed ③titled "The Crowned Goddess," and the wind carries it to her. He chews gardenias and drinks her cologne, just to taste what she tastes like.

At last he proposes by letter. Her answer, written on a scrap torn from a school notebook, says in substance: very well, I'll marry you, Seed ④if you promise never to make me eat eggplant.

The discovery is ordinary. A nun catches her writing a love letter in class, and she is expelled from the convent school. Her father throws out the aunt, invites Florentino to a café, starts gently, turns hard, and finally shows him a revolver. The boy says, roughly: shoot me — there is no greater glory than to die for love. The father doesn't shoot. He finds another method: he takes his daughter over the mountains to her late mother's people, a journey designed for forgetting.

It doesn't work. Telegraph operators in the mountain towns pass messages along the line, and her cousin Hildebranda covers for her. But when she returns to the city more than a year later, she is no longer the girl who looked up in the sewing room. She runs the household now — keeps her father's accounts, manages the servants, and does her own shopping in the great market.

One morning she is buying things beneath the colonnade by the market known as the Arcade of the Scribes, and Florentino slips up behind her and speaks into her ear:

"This is not the proper place for a crowned goddess."Part Two · the Arcade of the Scribes (wording approximate)

She turns and sees a frozen face, gray with fear. Payoff ③ ↑She has heard that title countless times: it was the name of the waltz drifting on the night wind, the way one boy had placed her on an altar when she was a schoolgirl. But in the noise of the market, coming from a real, thin, awkward man a step away, the same phrase suddenly becomes a paper crown that doesn't fit. She feels hollowed out — not angry, disenchanted. She says only "No, please," tells him to forget it, and that same day sends a two-line note: seeing him today, she understood that what was between them was nothing but an illusion.

Act Three · The doctor and the boy: one marries, one takes the boat

Dr. Urbino first walks into her house because someone suspects she has cholera. She doesn't — just an intestinal infection. He listens to her chest with his ear, and she finds him unbearably arrogant. But he is the city's most eligible bachelor: old family, Paris-trained, handsome, revered. Lorenzo Daza is desperate for the match; his own money comes from murky sources, and polite society won't receive him.

She first writes to refuse, then softens. The question she asks herself is not "Do I love him?" but "Can he give me a life without fear?" She is not persuaded by love; she is persuaded by order. The wedding is lavish, and the couple sail to Europe for a honeymoon, staying in Paris more than a year.

Florentino hears of the wedding and collapses. His mother asks his uncle, Leo XII Loayza — the big man at the river company — to find him a post at a remote telegraph station in the interior, and sends him up the great river. Seed ⑤For days on the boat he watches the banks: caimans sunning themselves with their jaws open, parrots and monkeys screeching in the treetops, manatees nursing their young on the surface and weeping like women. Virgin forest crowds right down to the water.

On that boat, one night, someone in the dark drags him into a cabin and takes his virginity; he never learns who it was. It changes him: he discovers that he can be loved in the body without Fermina. When the boat reaches the end of the line he doesn't go to his post; he rides the same boat back. He decides to stay in the city, earn money and standing, and wait. Wait for what? For Dr. Urbino to die.

Later Fermina returns from Europe pregnant, on her husband's arm at the cathedral door. Florentino watches from a distance, and from then on begins to record his women in a notebook.

Act Four · Two kinds of fifty years: one marriage, six hundred and twenty-two women

For the next half-century the novel tells two lives side by side.

On one side, the marriage. Fermina moves into the Urbino family mansion, with a mother-in-law who finds fault with everything and a whole code she must learn. Payoff ④ ↑The only condition she ever set for Florentino was no eggplant; married into the Urbinos, she finds herself eating eggplant at their table after all. The single condition she gave the poor boy quietly lapsed at the rich family's dinner table — a light touch, almost a household joke, that tells you exactly what kind of security she got and what she gave up for it.

The couple have a famous fight over soap. The doctor complains that there's been no soap in the bathroom for nearly a week; she insists there always has been. Two proud people, neither willing to give in: he sleeps in his study for months, and in the end he is the one who softens. García Márquez is sharpest on trivia: what wrecks a marriage is often not a catastrophe but a bar of soap.

In middle age the doctor has an affair with a patient, Miss Barbara Lynch, a Jamaican mulatta. Fermina has a habit of smelling her husband's clothes before the wash; she smells it there. He confesses, and she packs for her cousin Hildebranda's ranch and stays two years. In the end he goes to fetch her himself — and she has been waiting for him to come. They return to the house and grow old slowly together, learning to hand over a handkerchief before the other coughs. The marriage has no passion, but it is something solid, built out of daily trifles, and by old age the two of them have grown into halves of one person.

On the other side, Florentino. He starts at the bottom of his uncle's company — his business letters keep coming out as love letters — yet he climbs, and ends up as president of the river company. In his spare time he writes love letters for free for the illiterate, sitting under the Arcade of the Scribes, Seed ⑥the same colonnade where she told him to forget it. In other people's romances, he finishes again and again the letter he never got to finish.

His women form a long queue. The Widow Nazaret, who took refuge in his mother's house during a bombardment in the civil war, is his first real lover. A young wife named Olimpia Zuleta lets him paint an obscene declaration of ownership in red on her belly; her husband sees it and cuts her throat that same night. He keeps one woman, Leona Cassiani, as his true confidante; she rises from clerk to his right hand at the company, and after one failed attempt they never become lovers, but remain friends for life. By his notebooks' count, he has six hundred and twenty-two long-term lovers, not counting the fleeting ones.

And yet he is certain he has been faithful to Fermina, because his heart has never gone to anyone else. The novel neither punctures that belief nor defends it; it simply sets side by side a longing almost religious in its purity and a list of lovers frighteningly long.

When he is past seventy, his family entrusts him with a young relative from the provinces who has come to the city for school: América Vicuña, fourteen. He is her guardian. He also becomes her lover.

Act Five · After the funeral: a year of letters

Back to the night of the first act. Thrown out, Florentino does not give up. He starts writing to Fermina — not the scorched love letters of his youth but long typewritten letters about aging, death, marriage, how to get through the days. She means to burn them; then she reads them; then she waits for them. These letters don't court her. They are one old person telling another: I know what you are going through.

A year later he starts calling in the afternoons; the two old people take tea and talk on the terrace. A scandal sheet prints lurid stories about her late husband's affair and the origins of her father's fortune; she nearly breaks, and through those days Florentino's letters are what she leans on.

Her son thinks it's good for his mother to have company. Her daughter Ofelia explodes — in substance: love at our age is ridiculous, but at theirs it's revolting. Fermina throws her daughter out of the house. Half a century ago, a father stopped her love with a journey; now a daughter tries to stop it with a sentence, and this time she doesn't yield.

Meanwhile Florentino grows colder toward América Vicuña, his attention wholly fixed on an old woman.

Act Six · The New Fidelity: a yellow flag

He invites her on one of his company's boats, a trip up the great river to lift her spirits. She accepts. The boat is called the New Fidelity.

Payoff ⑤ ↑This is Florentino's second trip up this river. The first time, as a boy, he was fleeing her wedding. More than half a century later he stands beside her on the same water: the forests have been fed, stand by stand, into the boilers of the riverboats; the caimans and manatees are nearly gone; now and then a bloated corpse drifts past, from cholera or from war, no one can say. He kept one love intact; the river kept nothing — and the boilers that ate the forest belong to the company he runs. García Márquez never says so; he just lets the same river be seen once at the beginning and once at the end.

One night on deck he takes her hand for the first time; both hands are old. A few days later she kisses him. In the cabin, two people with slack skin and the smell of age make love, clumsily, unsuccessfully, and try again. He tells her: "I've remained a virgin for you" (Part Six). She doesn't believe him, but she likes the courage with which he says it.

During the voyage a telegram reaches him: América Vicuña is dead. She failed her final exams and drank a bottle of laudanum. He hides and weeps alone, and tells Fermina nothing. Then he folds it away inside himself and sails on.

The boat reaches its end point and must turn back. As they near the city, Fermina panics: the docks will be full of people who know her, and she can't bear their eyes. Florentino asks the captain whether they could go without stopping anywhere. Only, says the captain, if there were cholera on board: then regulations require a yellow flag and no one may board or leave. So they run up the yellow cholera flag.

Payoff ② ↑Fifty-some years earlier, an old homeopath looked at a boy vomiting green bile and declared that the symptoms of love were those of cholera — a funny diagnosis at the time. At the end it is redeemed word for word, as a flag: a boatful of old people can only keep their love going by declaring themselves plague-stricken. The title opens fully on the last page. "The time of cholera" is not just the era the story is set in; love itself is the epidemic — it isolates, burns with fever, and refuses to be cured.

The boat goes up and down the river and stops at no port. The captain looks at the two old people and asks how long they think they can keep up this goddamn coming and going. Florentino has had his answer ready for fifty-three years, seven months, and eleven days and nights:

"Forever."Part Six · the last line of the novel

Payoff ⑥ ↑Now look back at the Arcade of the Scribes. She told him to forget it there; he spent decades there writing other people's love letters, turning the place of his rejection into the place where he practiced his phrasing. The year of typewritten letters that finally reached her was learned under that colonnade. The conversation he lost, he won back on paper, over half a century.

If you want the fuse, it is not Dr. Urbino's death, nor the first glance. The fuse is "crowned goddess," spoken in the market: a boy lifted a girl onto an altar with the loveliest word he had, she saw in that word someone she didn't recognize, and she turned away. Everything after — her marriage of order, his six hundred stand-ins, his climb to power — is the aftershock of that miss. Only past seventy, when he finally stops calling her a goddess and writes to her instead about aging, constipation, and death, does she agree, for the first time, to go with him. What he spent fifty years learning was how to take the crown off.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · A glance from the sewing room: fate in a single sentence

Replay it: a boy with a telegram in his hand crosses a run-down house. Through a window he sees an old woman and a girl in the sewing room, the girl teaching her to read, syllable by syllable. The girl lifts her head to see who is passing. That's all.

Why does it hit? Because García Márquez writes almost nothing at this moment. No description of her beauty, no racing heart. The biggest event gets the shortest sentence, and then the narration jumps straight to his symptoms over the following months. He writes love as a disease of the body, not an elevation of the soul; we laugh first, and only later understand it is a lifelong illness. One more detail: she is teaching her aunt to read — and the whole story will unfold in letters, and begin again in letters.

Two · The turn in the market: the point of view changes hands

Replay it: a noisy morning, she is choosing goods at a stall beneath the arcade, and behind her a voice only she can hear says "crowned goddess." She turns. He is one step away; she sees his eyes, his ashen face, the fear on his lips. What rises in her is not love but a bottomless disappointment.

Why does it hit? Until now an entire part has been seen from Florentino's side, and we've believed with him that this love is fated. At this instant the narration slips into her head, and what she sees is a stranger. The novel gives no reason for her change of heart — no misunderstanding, no rival, just one close-up act of seeing. That is what makes it cruel and honest: love at a distance feeds on imagination; at one step's distance, imagination can't hold up against a real face. A few lines end a two-year romance, and the aftershock takes the rest of the book.

Three · The yellow flag: a comic ending over a tragic ground

Replay it: on the river, a yellow cholera flag goes up the mast; people on shore watch from afar, and no one dares approach. The captain with his lover, Florentino with Fermina — two couples on a quarantined boat, drifting up and down a river stripped of its trees.

Why does it hit? This ending is three things at once: a fairy tale (together at last, and "forever"); a farce (two septuagenarians posing as a plague ship to dodge gossip); an elegy (devastated forest on both banks, and the news of a fourteen-year-old's death hidden aboard). García Márquez won't choose for us. The final figure — fifty-three years, seven months, eleven days — is precise to the point of comedy, like an accountant reckoning up love, but that comic precision is exactly what makes "forever" sound not like a vow but like a calm fact, worked out long ago.

The book's core devices

This is not a premise-driven novel. Its core devices are two: a time structure that starts at the end, and a narrator who shows its own hero no mercy.

First: begin near the end, then circle back to it. Part One is close to the story's endpoint — the husband dead, the old suitor renewing his vow. Part Two drops back fifty years and works forward, catching up with that night only in Part Five; only the last part moves past it. The effect: from the start, we know where these fifty years lead, so every quarrel and reconciliation in the marriage chapters carries a shadow of foreknowledge — this couple will grow old together, and an old man is waiting across town. What holds up: we wait alongside Florentino. The cost: the middle fifty years have to be compressed into summary and lists, a built-in price of the structure.

Second: an ironic narrator telling a romance. The book borrows every prop of the nineteenth-century serialized love story: love at first sight, love letters, serenades, a tyrannical father, a vow to wait a lifetime. Yet the narrating voice stays cool and amused: lovesickness written as diarrhea, love letters turning into business correspondence, eternal fidelity set right beside six hundred and twenty-two lovers. It tells the most hackneyed love story there is while dismantling each hackneyed part in front of you. So the book has no reliable "view of love": passionate love is written as a disease, married love as habit, old love as a brave piece of foolishness — all three taken seriously, all three mocked.

What it forces you to think about

One · After the market: was she right to choose Urbino?

A young woman of about eighteen has two men before her: a poor boy who has written her hundreds of letters and would die for her, but who, at a step's distance, leaves her feeling only disappointment; and a doctor she doesn't love who can give her standing, safety, and a whole world. She chooses the second, and lives with him fifty years. If you were she — or her friend — what would you advise?

First, the strongest case that she chose wrong. Her life was arranged by three people: a father who hauled her off to the mountains, a husband who installed her in a mansion, a mother-in-law who set eggplant before her. Her marriage held infidelity, cold wars, and a family she had to learn to please. Meanwhile the boy she dropped proved over half a century that his love was no passing impulse. She traded "safety" for "being loved by one person with his whole heart", and the bill came due at seventy-two: on the first night after her husband's death, she was thinking about someone else.

The other side is just as strong. What she saw in the market was real. The boy loved the "goddess" he had written into his letters, not this woman who kept accounts, scolded servants, and haggled. Marrying someone who loves only your phantom is more dangerous than marrying someone who loves you less but can see you. And Urbino gave her far more than safety: they grew old together, and his dying words were true. Besides, the Florentino of fifty years later learned to stop treating her as a goddess precisely because she refused him. Had she married him at eighteen, they might simply have had a marriage whose disenchantment arrived later.

Where does the author stand? García Márquez hands down no verdict. He spares neither the vulgarity and damage of that marriage nor its tenderness; he gives the ending to Florentino but sets "forever" after seventy, as if to say: the two loves belong to two ages, and at each age she chose what that age called for. Bring it home: someone who makes your heart race, someone who makes your heart rest — are you choosing a relationship, or choosing a future self?

Two · Six hundred and twenty-two women: is that fidelity, or self-flattery?

For fifty years a man never forgets one woman for a single day, while sleeping with more than six hundred others, and never once feels he has betrayed her. At the end he tells her: I've remained a virgin for you. Is he lying, or telling a kind of truth we aren't used to hearing?

First, the strongest case that this is real fidelity — for the position that sounds most absurd. Body and heart are separate: plenty of people privately admit as much. Florentino never let any lover into the place in his heart kept for Fermina. He married no one, promised no one anything, and aimed the whole direction of his life at one person. If fidelity means "my heart faces only you," he is more faithful than anyone married in the book — including Urbino, with his affair. In Florentino's own dictionary, "I've remained a virgin for you" may not be a lie.

The other side: this split between body and heart is the most convenient exemption a man could ever give himself. Olimpia Zuleta was killed by her husband because of words Florentino painted on her; América Vicuña killed herself after he dropped her. His "purity" toward Fermina was propped up by other women's bodies, reputations, and lives. Perhaps what he loved was never Fermina the person but the image of himself as the eternal waiter — an image that lent every indulgence a noble excuse.

Where does the author stand? He has Fermina "not believe him, but like the courage with which he said it" — which is almost the author's own stance toward the reader: he doesn't ask you to believe Florentino, only to see how a person can be sincere and self-deceived at once. Bring it home: when someone says "it's only ever been you," which counts more — the sentence, or how they actually lived those years? And do you have an obsession of your own that you've used to forgive a great many other things?

Three · América Vicuña: can a beautiful book hold a girl's death?

A guardian past seventy has sex with a fourteen-year-old girl, then abandons her for an old woman, and the girl kills herself. He hides, weeps once, and continues his romantic voyage. This happens in a book countless readers call the most beautiful love story ever written. How should a reader treat it — as a stain on the book, or as its true center of gravity?

First, the strongest case that this remains a great love story. García Márquez doesn't prettify the relationship: the girl dies, the news crashes down in the middle of the sweetest voyage, and Florentino can do nothing but weep in secret. That stroke is exactly what keeps the ending from becoming a cheap fairy tale: two old people win happiness, and the happiness has an invisible price. A writer who dares to put something this foul inside his tenderest book is refusing to whitewash anything for us.

The other side: the problem isn't that the novel depicts it, but how. América Vicuña has almost no voice of her own; her death takes a few lines, and the narration soon drifts back to the scenery and the two old people's kisses. Florentino suffers no consequence; the story doesn't change course. When a book handles a child's destruction as its hero's private sorrow, it has structurally already forgiven him — and the reader is invited to go on being moved by that yellow flag.

Where does the author stand? The book gives no clear judgment, and that is part of the controversy; but he placed this at the most conspicuous moment, not buried in the middle-section list. Bring it home: a person you admire, a work you love, contains something you can't accept — do you cut it out, treat it as the whole, or carry it along and remember both?

Motifs: what García Márquez keeps writing about

First, waiting. In No One Writes to the Colonel (1961), an old colonel waits decades for a pension that never comes; Florentino is another version of that waiter, except that he gets what he waits for. Second, the ending announced in advance. Chronicle of a Death Foretold (1981) tells you in its first sentence that the hero will die; this book likewise begins at the end. Third, love as illness. In Of Love and Other Demons (1994), love tangles with rabies. Fourth, solitude and old age. The General in His Labyrinth (1989) sends a dying Bolívar down the same Magdalena River, aging and forgotten.

This is the gentlest, most hopeful link in that chain of obsessions: solitude isn't defeated, but two old people carry it together — even if it takes a plague flag to do it.

Controversy and criticism

América Vicuña. There's no getting around it. A guardian in his seventies having sex with his fourteen-year-old ward is, by any measure, the sexual abuse of a minor. Critics note that the narration barely lets it slow the romantic ending; and García Márquez's last novel published in his lifetime, Memories of My Melancholy Whores (2004), about a ninety-year-old man and a fourteen-year-old girl, makes the problem even harder to look past.

The male gaze. Most of the novel's women are a string of names orbiting Florentino; some die because of him, some are played for laughs. Fermina is the exception, yet some readers argue that she too ends up as a prize "won" by two men in turn.

Romance or satire. Critics have long argued over whether the book is a hymn to love or a parody of romantic love. Thomas Pynchon reviewed the English translation in The New York Times in 1988, in a well-known and admiring piece; other critics find the fifty-year middle over-summarized and slow, Florentino's love life reading like a ledger.

Translations. The English translation, by Edith Grossman, appeared in 1988. In China, several unauthorized translations circulated from the 1980s; García Márquez complained publicly about piracy there, and an authorized Chinese translation appeared only in 2012.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · Tennov's "limerence": Dorothy Tennov (1979) described a state of obsessive thinking about another person and acute dependence on their response. Young Florentino's symptoms have exactly this shape. The difference: limerence usually ebbs within months or a few years; he kept it alive in imagination for fifty, and finally turned it into something else.
  • Psychology · Sternberg's triangular theory of love: Robert Sternberg (1986) split love into intimacy, passion, and commitment. The boy's love is passion alone; the Urbinos' old age is intimacy plus commitment. The difference: the novel's final, elderly love grows all three sides from zero.
  • Psychology · Carstensen's socioemotional selectivity theory: when people sense that their time is limited, they prioritize emotionally meaningful relationships. Two septuagenarians who stop caring about gossip fit the theory. The difference: it predicts a narrowing toward familiar old ties, and they begin a new one.
  • Behavioral economics · Sunk costs: people hold on because they've already invested too much. Fifty-one years of waiting looks like an investment too large to cut. The difference: the final "forever" is about the future, not about recouping the past.
