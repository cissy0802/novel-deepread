Novels, Deep-Read · France · No.19

Madame Bovary

Provincial Manners · Gustave Flaubert · serialized in the Revue de Paris, 1856 · book edition 1857

中文 →

A hired cab drives around Rouen for an entire afternoon. The blinds are down; the driver has no idea where he is going, only a voice from inside telling him to keep moving — over the bridge, out through the gate, along the quays, back again. The horse is lathered. People stop in the street to stare at this lunatic carriage.
For that whole afternoon, nothing inside the cab is written down. Once, a bare hand comes out through the blinds and scatters a fistful of torn paper, and the scraps drift into a clover field like white butterflies.
They are the letter she had written to end the whole thing.

This page contains complete spoilers — the break-up letter delivered in a basket of apricots, how Lheureux's ledger closes around her, what Emma swallows, what the blind beggar is singing under the window, what Charles finds afterwards, and who gets the medal in the novel's last sentence. This site works on one conviction: everything worth discussing sits on the far side of the ending. If you want to keep the suspense, read the novel first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

A farmer's daughter raised on romantic novels in a convent marries a clumsy, decent, wholly unimaginative country health officer; one aristocratic ball shows her what else existence might have looked like, and she can never live inside her own life again. She takes a landowner who treats her as a diversion and then a young law clerk who will not take a risk for her — and every surge of feeling comes attached to a line of credit. When the bailiff's writ finally arrives, she goes to every person she can ask, gets nothing, and swallows arsenic in her own bedroom. What kills her is neither adultery nor romance: it is a promissory note she signed herself.

Coordinates

Gustave Flaubert (1821–1880) was from Rouen, where his father was chief surgeon of the main hospital; he grew up next door to a dissecting room. In his early twenties a seizure-like attack ended his law studies, and he spent the rest of his life at Croisset on the Seine doing essentially one thing: writing, extremely slowly. This book took him close to five years.

It was serialized in the Revue de Paris in 1856 and published as a book in 1857, with an obscenity trial in between (see the last section). Its place in the history of the novel is not really disputed: this is where the modern novel's scalpel was sharpened. Before it, novelists stood beside their characters handing the reader verdicts. From here on, the narrator withdraws behind glass, characters' private thoughts slide directly into the narration, and readers are forced to judge for themselves — and to own the judgement. Maupassant, Joyce, Hemingway and every cool, withholding novel since run on this machinery.

The story

Act one · A boy whose name is misheard, a girl reading novels in a convent

The novel's first word is "we": a class of schoolboys watching a new boy from the country come in. His cap is indescribably hideous; asked his name, he mumbles a blur of syllables that the class hears as one ridiculous word. That "we" vanishes at the end of the chapter and never returns — Flaubert uses it to pin Charles Bovary to his starting point: a man who begins as a joke and never really gets to speak.

Charles is mediocre and his mother arranges everything: medicine, one failed examination, then a pass, and the qualification of officier de santé — a second-class licence to practise within the province, not a doctor's degree. His mother also marries him to a thin, elderly widow said to have money. During that marriage he is called out to a farm, Les Bertaux, to set old Rouault's broken leg. The door is opened by Rouault's daughter, Emma. Flaubert never says she is beautiful; he gives details instead: fingernails too clean for a farm, a small movement of her lower lip when she speaks, and — finishing the last of a liqueur — the tip of her tongue going into the glass to lick the bottom of it. Seed ①

The widow dies and Charles marries Emma. The wedding is a country feast that runs sixteen hours; what Emma had wanted was a wedding at midnight by torchlight, which she had read about in the convent and never said aloud. That education is the seed of everything: sent to the nuns at thirteen, taught to pray, she absorbed instead the things the sisters brought in with them — knights and secret meetings, the portrait of Mary Stuart, passages about moonlight and deathbed farewells. What she learned was not faith but the posture of being moved. Out of the convent, this equipment has nowhere to go, so all of it is transferred to "love" and "marriage".

Then Tostes. Charles is luminous with happiness; Emma sits at the window listening to this man make noise over his soup, watching his conversation run as flat as a pavement, and understands for the first time that she has married not a person but an afternoon without end.

Act two · The ball: let in once, then sent home

The turn comes by accident. Charles lances a marquis's abscess and the couple are invited to a ball at the château of La Vaubyessard. That night is the novel's ground zero: candles, iced wine, an old duke said to have been in a queen's bed in his youth, bare shoulders, the particular way rich men talk, and a waltz in which a viscount turns her until the room blurs.

On the road home, Charles's whip strikes something in the grass. It is a green silk cigar case, embroidered with a crest and smelling of tobacco. Emma keeps it. Seed ②

After this she does three very small, very fatal things: she buys a map of Paris and walks its streets with her finger; she subscribes to Paris fashion magazines; and she marks the date of the ball every year, until the year she realizes she can no longer remember the viscount's face. That night taught her nothing except what she was missing. Then she falls ill — no diagnosis, just sleeplessness, palpitations, sudden laughing and sudden weeping — and Charles decides to move. Before leaving Tostes she burns her wedding bouquet; the dried orange blossom curls up in the grate and goes up the chimney as black moths. She preserves a cigar case dropped by a stranger and burns her own bouquet: that woman's entire logic is written in those two gestures.

They move to Yonville. Emma is pregnant and does not yet know it.

Act three · Yonville: a pharmacist, a draper, a clerk

Yonville is the specimen box Flaubert built for this book. In it: Monsieur Homais, pharmacist, disciple of Voltaire, enemy of the clergy, forever writing pieces for the Rouen paper in progressive phrases copied out of other papers; Abbé Bournisien, whose mind holds the catechism and nothing else; Binet, the tax collector, turning napkin rings on his lathe, one after another, for life; and Lheureux, the draper, who on his first visit spreads out shawls and lace and embroidery, smiles, says madame may have anything on account, no hurry at all, and mentions that he himself is never short of money. Seed ③ And Léon Dupuis, the notary's young clerk: over the first dinner he and Emma discover that they both love sunsets, the sea, and books "that break your heart". Neither of them knows they are reciting.

A daughter is born, Berthe. Emma had wanted a son — a man at least is free, a man can go somewhere; a woman always has something tied to her. The baby is put out to a wet nurse in the village; Emma visits occasionally, holds her, puts her down.

Nothing happens with Léon. Both are too timid, and the timidity keeps converting into poetry. So Emma performs an extreme version of the good wife: gentler with her husband, church, donations, her own restraint admired over and over. She is not enduring; she is starring in a novel called Endurance, and the only audience is herself. The day she cannot hold it any longer she goes to Abbé Bournisien and says she is suffering. He asks whether it is indigestion, observes that her husband is a doctor after all, and then starts on the villagers who genuinely have nothing — no firewood, no bread. She opens her mouth twice and finally says never mind. The priest offers every scrap of goodwill his understanding contains, and his understanding has no room for the idea that a woman who is warm and fed may still be unable to go on.

Léon cannot bear the stalemate and leaves to study law in Paris. Emma collapses inward and starts buying things: a scarf, a length of cloth — and Lheureux always happens to call.

Act four · Rodolphe: the agricultural show, a horse, a wooden leg, and a letter in a basket of apricots

Rodolphe Boulanger, thirty-four, owner of La Huchette nearby, with money, leisure and experience. He brings a farmhand in to be bled, sees Emma, and works it out before he is back on the street: this woman is bored to death, she will not be difficult — and afterwards, how do I get rid of her? The occasion he chooses is the agricultural show (comices agricoles), the town's big day, livestock and manure in rows, officials on a platform giving prizes. He takes Emma up to an empty room on the first floor of the town hall, and through the open window one voice is the prize list and the other is his declaration of love (the next section takes this scene apart).

His method is a professional's: withdraw, let her wait six weeks, reappear. Then the riding — he tells Charles that madame's nerves need exercise, and Charles agrees gratefully and buys her a habit. The husband personally puts his wife on that horse. In the woods, on the way back, she becomes Rodolphe's mistress. That night she looks in the mirror and says to herself:

"I have a lover!"— Emma, Part Two (quoted phrase)

The stress does not fall on "I love him" but on "I have a lover" — she has finally acquired an item she had read about. After that she is reckless: crossing the soaked fields to La Huchette before dawn with her skirts wet to the waist, and giving presents — a riding whip, a seal, a scarf, all entered in Lheureux's book. She buys her lover gifts with her husband's money, and her husband's money is in fact Lheureux's.

Then comes the episode that finishes the marriage. Homais reads about a new operation for club foot and pushes Charles into performing it on Hippolyte, the inn's stable boy — a cure would bring glory to the town, and could be written up in the paper. Charles is manoeuvred into it and does it; Emma too is seduced by the prospect and feels, for once, a flicker of warmth for her husband. Days later the leg turns black and putrid, and the surgeon Canivet arrives, curses everyone, and takes the thigh off. This is the summit of Flaubert's cruelty — he refuses to make Charles wicked; he simply lets him, on the one occasion he tries to be somebody, destroy a man's leg. Emma's remedy is perfectly in character: she pays for the handsomest artificial leg available, jointed, with a patent-leather boot. On credit, naturally.

She throws herself back at Rodolphe and starts pressing him: let us go, take the child, Italy. Rodolphe agrees aloud and goes cold inside. On the eve of the departure he sits down to write. He cannot get the thing moving, so he fetches an old biscuit tin stuffed with the trophies of years: love letters, dried flowers, hair, other women's handkerchiefs, portraits he can no longer identify. He turns them over one by one and the names are gone. Payoff ② ↑ That tin is the second appearance of the green silk cigar case. Emma kept one dropped relic for a lifetime as evidence that she deserved another kind of existence; Rodolphe's box holds dozens of the same relics, gone musty. One image, twice: on one side a reliquary, on the other a bin — and without a word of comment from the author, that woman's fate has been priced.

The letter gets written, full of "destiny" and "you will forget me", ending with the line that it is all the fault of fate. Feeling something is still missing, he drips water onto the page for tears, and sends it in a basket of apricots. Emma reads it, goes to the window; the paving is below and she is already leaning out. Downstairs Charles calls: madame, dinner. She steps back, goes down, sits at the table and eats. Even her suicide is interrupted by supper — Flaubert's deepest single act of revenge on the romantic.

That night she falls seriously ill, and stays ill for months while the medical bills stack up; during the illness she turns to religion, reads devotional books, gives things away, and enjoys her own sanctity. To cheer her, Charles borrows money and takes her to the theatre in Rouen: Lucia di Lammermoor, in which a woman is forced to marry a man she does not love, loses her mind and dies. In the interval they run into Léon, now working in Rouen.

Act five · Rouen: Thursdays, signatures, and a bill due in twenty-four hours

The second affair opens far more competently. Léon takes her to the cathedral to declare himself; a verger insists on showing them the famous tombs, and Léon, driven mad, pulls her outside, hails a cab and tells the driver to keep going. What follows is the scene this page opened with. The scraps of paper thrown from the window are the letter Emma had written to break it off.

From then on she goes to Rouen every Thursday, ostensibly for piano lessons, complete with receipts from a teacher. In a hotel room she lives the life she wants: carpet, mirrors, scent, candles — and walks back into Yonville through the mud. It is the only space she ever furnishes to her own taste, and it is rented by the hour.

Meanwhile the money closes in. Lheureux's technique deepens by stages: first goods on account; then the debt converted into a note (just a signature, madame); then, when the note falls due, help in the form of a larger new note with fees and interest added; and finally the suggestion that she obtain her husband's power of attorney (procuration) so that she need not consult him about household money at all. Charles signs without a thought. Payoff ③ ↑ The draper who called that first day and said, smiling, that he was never short of money has never been selling cloth. He sells time: you get the thing now, and you buy back your own future with signatures. His name, Lheureux, means "the happy one" in French — Flaubert gave the most lethal figure in the book that name and never once pointed at it. So the snowball rolls: Charles's father dies, Emma talks him into selling the small property he inherits, old notes become new notes, new notes roll again.

Then the writ: eight thousand francs within twenty-four hours, or seizure. The notice is posted in the town.

And so the fastest day in the novel. Lheureux, in his counting-house, says politely that there is nothing to be done, madame, he has his own creditors. The notary Guillaumin says the money is easy and slides an arm around her waist; she breaks free and runs, shaking — that they should dare. She asks Léon for three thousand francs, hinting that he might take it from the office; Léon goes white, temporizes, and refuses in effect. She even stands outside Binet's lathe-room and cannot get a word out. Finally she goes to La Huchette. Rodolphe is still tender until she names the figure; then he spreads his hands: I haven't got it. And she sees the room perfectly clearly — the guns on the wall, the carpets, the silver, every item worth more than three thousand francs. Only now does she understand that everything those men said about love and undying devotion was free, and had always been free.

She runs to Homais's pharmacy and works on Justin, the boy apprentice — a teenager who worships her in secret and has cleaned her shoes Seed ④ — saying she needs something for rats. Justin unlocks the storeroom of dangerous substances on the third floor. She pushes him aside, seizes the blue jar, plunges her hand in and stuffs a fistful of white powder into her mouth. Payoff ④ ↑ The one person in the book who loves her without wanting anything back is the child who opens the poison cupboard for her, and he has no idea what he has done. (The person weeping at her grave at night, later, is also him.)

And one more account to settle: the girl who first appeared licking the last drop out of a liqueur glass ends by putting her hand into a jar, taking everything, and putting it in her mouth. Payoff ① ↑ The same gesture, three hundred pages apart, has gone from alluring to lethal: she only ever had one posture, which was to scrape the bottom of the glass.

Charles ransacks his medical books; Canivet comes, then the celebrated Dr. Larivière from Rouen, who sees at a glance that it is hopeless, takes the pulse, says two things and is invited to lunch by Homais.

As she is going, singing comes through the window: the blind beggar who follows the Rouen coach for coins, working through his indecent little song about a girl dreaming of love in the hot sun. Seed ⑤ Emma hears it, pushes herself upright in the bed with a terrible laugh, and falls back dead. Payoff ⑤ ↑ The beggar has been on the Rouen road throughout; Homais, to show off, boasted in print that he could cure him, then wrote articles against him when he could not, and finally had him shut away in an institution. She spent her life chasing the word "love", and the man who finally sings it to her is a beggar used all along as advertising copy.

Act six · Afterwards: bills do not die with people

During the wake, Homais and Bournisien argue science against faith on either side of the body and both fall asleep. Two languages fed her whole life — the religious and the progressive — both learned by rote, and neither of them saved her. Charles insists she be buried in her wedding dress, in three coffins, oak, mahogany and lead, one inside the other: the single extravagance of his life, spent on someone already dead.

Then the creditors come and the furniture goes piece by piece. He finds Léon's letters in the attic and, later, Rodolphe's apricot-basket letter in a drawer, and so learns everything. He does not hate them; he begins to worship her instead, grows his hair long, dresses as she would have liked. One day he meets Rodolphe at the market, they have a drink, and Charles says the most unbearable line in the book: it is not your fault — it is the fault of fate. Which is precisely the formula Rodolphe used to excuse himself in the break-up letter. The man deceived from beginning to end forgives his deceiver in the deceiver's own language. Rodolphe thinks him rather comic and rather base. The next day Charles dies in a garden chair with a lock of her hair in his hand. Berthe is passed along until a poor aunt sends her to work in a cotton mill. And the last sentence of the novel belongs to Homais: he has just received the cross of the Legion of Honour.

Set the six acts side by side and the fuse turns out to be a single night: adultery did not destroy her; someone let her look inside once and then sent her home. Before La Vaubyessard she was merely dissatisfied; after it she had a specific standard to measure against, one she could never reach and never forget, and everything afterwards is aftershock. What she wanted was never a particular man — the order of Rodolphe and Léon hardly matters. What mattered is that the feeling of almost, but not quite had to be filled by somebody. And the only person willing to keep filling it, indefinitely, was Lheureux.

Three scenes in slow motion

1 · The agricultural show: seduction cut against manure

Rodolphe takes Emma up to the town hall's first floor with the window open onto the prize-giving below. Flaubert alternates the two voices line by line: Rodolphe saying that certain people recognize each other on sight, that he has been misunderstood, that he has been slandered — while below, the prizes are called out: manures, first prize. He takes her hand and says fate brought them together; below, they are announcing a champion boar.

This is the first use in fiction of what would later be called montage, half a century before cinema. Its power is not the mockery of Rodolphe, whose lines are already ridiculous enough; it is that Flaubert offers no comment at all. He simply sets two soundtracks side by side and lets the reader perform the subtraction: declarations of love and the valuation of livestock and dung are quoted in the same market.

And the final stroke of the scene is the heaviest. A prize goes to an old servant, Catherine Leroux, fifty-four years on the same farm, too frightened to move on the platform, handed a silver medal worth twenty-five francs. She holds it and murmurs that she will give it to the curé to say masses for her. Fifty-four years, twenty-five francs — while upstairs a lady is breaking her heart over the dullness of her life. Flaubert still declines to comment: he is not saying Emma's suffering is fake, he is putting a scale next to it.

2 · The cab: delete the part you most want to write

The most explicit passage about adultery in the book contains no adultery. The blinds come down and the point of view is expelled into the street; we ride with the driver in circles through Rouen — bridges, the river, the cemetery, the toll gate, the quays, a clover field — place names read out like a list, the driver thirsty, furious and baffled.

Three effects follow. First, the reader shut out is more excited than the reader shown — imagination is conscripted. Second, that absurd itinerary pins "passion" to a map: this world-shaking love is, physically, a circuit of the street grid of a provincial city, with no exit. Third, the only thing that comes out of the cab is torn paper — even her resolutions are made of breakable props. One verifiable fact: this passage was cut by the editors of the Revue de Paris during serialization, to Flaubert's fury, and restored in the book. Where the censor pressed hardest, the novel's strongest piece of technique grew.

3 · The death: romanticism on the dissecting table

A nineteenth-century heroine normally dies pale, thin, with a last word. Flaubert smashed that: Emma's death takes two full chapters of clinical detail — the taste of ink, black vomit, convulsions, sweat, blue fingernails, the breathing getting shorter. He was a surgeon's son, he knew what arsenic poisoning looked like, and he wrote what it looked like.

Crueller still are the two things he sets beside it. One is extreme unction: oil applied to eyes, ears, nose, mouth, hands, feet — precisely the organs with which she had coveted the world, so that the sacrament becomes an inventory of the senses. The other is the beggar's song outside. She laughs, and that laugh is the only moment in the book when she sees herself clearly: what she chased all her life has this for a soundtrack. Romanticism is not criticized in this novel; it is autopsied.

The device at the centre: where did the narrator go?

Free indirect discourse: the character's words smuggled into the narration

Consider a sentence built like this: Why had she married? Happiness, passion, intoxication — those enormous words out of the books; where were they in an actual life? That is not the narrator asking, it is Emma asking, but there are no quotation marks and no "she thought". This is free indirect discourse (French: style indirect libre): the character's idiom, vocabulary and logic moved straight into third-person narration.

It produces two opposite effects, and this book wants both. The first is proximity: you are not told what she thinks, you think with her mind for a moment. The second is irony: the words are hers — "intoxication", "fate", "for ever" are all second-hand goods lifted out of novels — so the reader pities her and simultaneously hears how hollow the vocabulary is. The proportions are set by the reader, not the author. Hence a century and a half of argument: some readers find a woman crushed by her century, others find a self-absorbed fool, and both readings run on the same sentences.

The author who refuses to appear

A much-quoted line from Flaubert's letters to Louise Colet runs, in substance:

"The author in his work must be like God in the universe: present everywhere and visible nowhere."— Flaubert to Louise Colet, 1852 (paraphrase of the familiar line)

That is not a pose but a set of specific prohibitions: no verdicts, no advocacy for characters, no sentences beginning "poor Emma". The cost is a book that reads inhumanly cold; the benefit is that it has not expired — works that write their conclusions into themselves go out of date with the conclusions, while this one only files evidence. It is also exactly what makes the book hated: from the outside, a refusal to take sides and an inability to feel look identical.

Bovarysme: a word the novel gave away

In the 1890s the philosopher Jules de Gaultier extracted a term from this book: bovarysme, the capacity to conceive of oneself as other than one is — not occasional daydreaming, but living by, and making demands of reality on behalf of, an imagined self. The faculty is not purely pathological: it is what gives people ambition, what makes them move, what makes them read a book from outside their own class. Emma's problem is not that she imagined another self; it is that her imagination was rented — from the torchlit wedding to the flight to Italy, every item she wanted came from a catalogue written by someone else, and even her dissatisfaction followed a template. What Flaubert dissects is not desire but mass-produced desire.

What it forces you to think about

Question 1
She only wanted more. What exactly is wrong with that?

Take her side at full strength. Emma's entire crime is refusing to be satisfied by a marriage with no conversation, no desire and no prospects, and trying to obtain something else. In her world a woman has no profession, no property of her own, and no possibility of divorce (it was abolished in France from 1816 to 1884); her lawful means of changing her life amount to zero. Her options are two: endure, or steal. She stole, and was sentenced to death for it, while of her two lovers one goes on shooting game and the other makes a good marriage. To demand that a person be satisfied with the portion she was allotted is to demand that she endorse the allocation.

Now the other side at full strength, and not via the lazy route of "adultery is immoral". The lethal charge is this: what she wanted was not freedom but scenery. Having acquired a lover, her first reaction is to think of heroines in novels; the most detailed part of her elopement plan is the luggage and the views along the way; even her suicide is composed at a window and abandoned when someone calls her to dinner. She never once asked herself what she wanted to become, only whether she looked like the sort of woman who had become it. Circumstances can strip a person of everything except the work of finding out what she actually wants — and she never did that work once.

The question is not remote today; only the props have changed. Travel photographs, weddings, other people's displayed lives — how much of our dissatisfaction is ours? The difference is not whether you want more, but whether you can say what "more" is. A longing that cannot be stated will keep looking for something to buy.

Question 2
Does Charles deserve pity, or is he an accomplice?

Defending Charles is easy: he is the only person in the book who loves without conditions. He is not clever, not impressive, not articulate, but he borrows money to take her to the theatre, buries her in three coffins and a wedding dress, learns the whole truth and feels no hatred, and grows his hair the way she liked. If love means giving whatever the other person wants and asking nothing back, he is the only one here who ever loved.

The indictment is just as sound and harder to answer: he never asks her a single question. Every judgement Charles forms about his wife comes from her body — colour, appetite, nerves; her suffering is permanently a symptom requiring a prescription. Slowness is not a sin, but never attempting to understand the person beside you is a dereliction that can run for decades; he gave her everything except being seen.

And yet one has to ask the uncomfortable reverse: treating a man's clumsiness as a charge sheet is precisely Emma's logic. If dullness in conversation constitutes a moral accusation, then everyone insufficiently interesting deserves to be betrayed. Most marriages contain no villain, only two people growing at different rates, one of whom does not know the other is leaving. Is the person who "doesn't understand you" in your debt, or simply unable to reach you? From inside the situation, those two look exactly the same.

Question 3
Was she crushed by desire, or by credit?

Read as a moral fable, the book loses its most modern layer: Emma does not die of adultery, she dies of debt. Both affairs are over or ending; what sends her running around the district and finally into the pharmacy is a writ due in twenty-four hours, and that writ was manufactured systematically. Every step Lheureux takes is legal: goods on account, notes, renewals, fees, compounding interest, and then a power of attorney that pulls her husband's solvency into the arrangement. He is not selling drapery. He is running a credit product designed for one client: a woman permitted to have longings but not money.

The strongest rebuttal is equally solid: nobody forced her to sign. Every franc became a riding whip, a scarf, a hotel room, a present for a lover; she concealed, forged receipts, tricked her husband out of his land. Casting her as a victim of the financial system cancels her agency — and she is the most active person in the novel. Saying she had no choice is dishonest: she had choices, and each time she chose whatever made the present hour easier.

The intersection of the two accounts is where most of us live. Credit means living the life before the capacity arrives, at the price of having your future priced in advance. The thing worth remembering is not "don't borrow" but Lheureux's opening line: no hurry at all, madame, I am never short of money. When the person hurrying you is not himself in a hurry, the arithmetic has already been done.

The recurring obsession in Flaubert

Line his books up and the same fixation appears: a person's inner life is made of the language they have absorbed, and most of that language is second-hand.

Sentimental Education gives Frédéric a lifelong love in which nothing happens, while a revolution passes him by. "A Simple Heart", in Three Tales, is Emma's gentler counterpart: the old servant Félicité, stripped of everything, ends by loving a stuffed parrot as the Holy Ghost — the same act of projecting a fantasy onto an unworthy object, dissected in Emma's case and saluted in Félicité's, the difference being that she never wanted more. He also compiled a Dictionary of Received Ideas, collecting the phrases the middle class kept in its mouth.

So Homais is not a side dish; he is one of the book's protagonists: a man can be composed entirely of ready-made sentences, do very well, and end up decorated. Giving him the honour is a verdict — Emma dies of fantasy and Homais is promoted for it, the only difference being that he copied the fashionable set.

Criticisms and controversies

  • The 1857 obscenity trial. After serialization, Flaubert, his publisher and his printer were prosecuted for offending public morals and religion; the imperial prosecutor was Ernest Pinard, the defence was led by Jules Senard, and the verdict was acquittal — the book edition is dedicated to Senard. The same year, the same prosecutor took Baudelaire's Les Fleurs du mal to court and won. The trial made the novel an overnight bestseller.
  • The charge of misogyny. A criticism that persists: Flaubert renders a woman's desire as pathology, gives her a maximally degrading death, and lets both male lovers walk away. The defence points out that no man escapes either — Rodolphe is squalid, Léon is a coward, Homais is a self-satisfied fool — and that what is actually convicted is provincial society entire. The argument has no settled answer.
  • "Madame Bovary, c'est moi." Enormously widespread and of doubtful provenance: it appears in none of Flaubert's known letters and survives only through a second-hand report. Anyone quoting it should say so.
  • The charge of coldness. A common contemporary verdict was that the book is technically perfect but lacking in goodness, and leaves the reader with less interest in humanity. People still say it, and it is the necessary by-product of the aesthetic described above.
  • What translation costs. The hardest thing to carry across is not vocabulary but free indirect discourse and the rhythm of the sentences: the moment a translator adds "she thought" or "she felt" for clarity, the current that holds sympathy and irony together drains away. English versions run from Eleanor Marx-Aveling's of 1886 to Lydia Davis's of 2010, each with its own trade-offs. In translation the story arrives complete and the machine arrives discounted.
  • It is also accused of having no content. Readers who dislike it complain consistently: a stale plot (adultery, ruin, suicide — the furniture of melodrama), unlovable characters, a slow pace. Factually they are right; Flaubert wanted a book with almost nothing in it, held up by style. To a reader who does not accept that style is content, this novel has little to offer.

Ten sentences to take away

1. The fuse is a single night: adultery did not ruin her — someone let her look inside a château once and then drove her home.
