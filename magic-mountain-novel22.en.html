Novel Deep-Read · Thomas Mann · No.22

The Magic Mountain

Der Zauberberg · Thomas Mann · 1924

中文 →

The train climbs, and the air thins as it climbs. The young man in the compartment has one suitcase, packed for three weeks: a few changes of clothes, a technical book about ocean liners, a box of cigars.
Up here they call everything below "the flatland," and they say it the way you would say another planet. On his first evening he is rolled into two camel-hair blankets on a balcony lounge chair, watching the snow on the far slope go from pink to grey. From the next room come laughter and other noises, and between them a cough—the cough of a person with a hole rotted into the chest.
He has booked three weeks.
He stays seven years.

This page contains complete spoilers—including why Hans never leaves, what happens between him and Clavdia on carnival night, how Joachim dies, whom Naphta shoots on the duelling ground, what Hans works out in the blizzard and how he loses it, and where the last page throws him. Nothing in this book becomes available until you know the ending: unless you know that the seven years on the mountain lead into the mud of 1914, all you can read is a sanatorium travelogue. If you want the suspense, read the novel first—this page will wait.

In one sentence

A reassuringly ordinary young German goes up to visit his soldier cousin for three weeks, is told there is a shadow on his lung, and stays; over seven years in a sealed Alpine sanatorium he is educated by fever, desire, death and two tutors fighting for his soul—one who believes in reason and progress, one who believes in terror and faith—until, alone in a blizzard, he works out a single sentence: a man must not let death rule his thoughts; then he forgets it, and in August 1914 a thunderclap empties the mountain, sends him down into the trenches, and his own author says goodbye to him in the mud and admits he does not know whether he will live.

Coordinates

Thomas Mann (1875–1955) was the second son of a grain-merchant dynasty in Lübeck, famous at twenty-six for Buddenbrooks, and awarded the Nobel Prize in 1929 (the citation named that family novel above all). He spent his life on one question: why does a respectable merchant household keep producing a son who is unfit for merchant life?

The Magic Mountain appeared in 1924, two volumes, over a thousand pages. Its origin is nearly as famous as the book: in 1912 Mann's wife Katia was a patient at a high-altitude sanatorium in Davos, Switzerland. He came to visit, stayed three weeks, picked up something bronchial himself, and the house physician suggested that he too might do well to stay for a cure. He declined, went back down the mountain, and then wrote a thousand pages about what would have happened if he hadn't. The plan had been a novella, a comic companion piece to Death in Venice; the war interrupted him for years, and by the time he picked it up again the light little thing had turned into a case history of Europe.

Its place in literary history is equally clear: it is the last great mutation of the German Bildungsroman, and also a Zeitroman—a novel of an era that makes time itself the protagonist. Lecturing on it at Princeton in 1939, Mann admitted something writers rarely admit: the book has to be read twice, because you cannot hear the music the first time.

The story

Act one · A suitcase packed for three weeks

Hans Castorp, twenty-three, from Hamburg, has just qualified as an engineer and is about to start at a shipyard. His parents and grandfather died early and he was raised by a great-uncle; he is mild, polite, unfailingly correct—the kind of young man who will neither ruin a task nor distinguish himself at it. Mann pins him to a single word on the first pages: ordinary. So ordinary that he has almost no character, which is exactly what makes him perfect raw material.

One childhood memory gets unusual space. The family owns a silver christening basin passed down through generations, with a plate whose back is engraved with the names of its owners. An adult points to the earliest of them and explains that this was your great-great-great-grandfather. The sound of all those greats booms in the child's ear like something rising out of a deep well, and he is enchanted by it—his first brush with the discovery that time can be deep enough to drown a person, and that being drowned in it is somehow lovely.seed ①

He goes up to visit his cousin Joachim Ziemssen, who wants to be an officer so badly it hurts, and whose lungs have kept him on the mountain counting off the days until a doctor lets him go. Meeting the train, Joachim's first serious remark is roughly this: your ideas about time, down there, do not apply here.

"Up here" is an international sanatorium called the Berghof. Hans learns the rules fast: five full meals a day, seven tables in the dining room, rest cure on the balcony afterwards wrapped in blankets, four temperature readings a day, seven full minutes with the thermometer in your mouth. The director, Behrens, is a large, coarse, cheerfully vulgar court physician; his assistant Dr. Krokowski gives regular lectures on love as a force conducive to illness—every bodily complaint, in his hands, traces back to some desire that was pushed under. There is a girl who can whistle with an artificial pneumothorax (air pumped into the chest cavity to rest a diseased lung), and a Mexican mother with no German who says only one thing to anyone she meets—both of them, both of them dead, meaning her two sons. Let people stop working, stop marrying, stop carrying consequences, and they grow into all sorts of shapes.

On his third day Hans is flushed and feverish and his cigar tastes wrong. Behrens takes a reading, takes a plate, and tells him there is a moist spot in his lung; it has been there a while and he simply didn't know. Three weeks won't do it. He should stay.

That sentence is the bolt on the door of the whole book. Hans writes down to his great-uncle to say that he too is ill and will be staying a while. The tone of the letter is not dismay; it is an excitement, unnoticed by the writer, that comes from relief. Nobody forces him. The shipyard post, the Hamburg marriage, the orderly life are all still waiting below. He simply puts them down.

Act two · The pencil

As a patient, Hans starts learning the language of the mountain. Nobody counts in days here; they count in months and years. The most precious personal possession is a thermometer, and he buys one and treasures it like a lover. Behrens takes him into the X-ray room and lets him look at his own hand: the ring floating in mid-air, the finger bones perfectly distinct. He has seen his own skeleton.seed ③ Death is not something ahead of him; it is inside him and always was.

Then there is Clavdia Chauchat: a Russian woman in room 7 who lets the dining-room door slam behind her every single time, who slouches when she sits, with high cheekbones and narrow eyes. Her husband is far away in Dagestan and effectively never appears. Hans's obsession makes no sense to him at all, until one day he realizes why: she looks like someone.

When he was thirteen or fourteen there was a boy at school, Pribislav Hippe, with exactly those eyes. One break, Hans worked up the nerve to walk over and ask to borrow a pencil. The boy handed him a silver propelling pencil; he used it and gave it back. The whole thing took a minute, and they never spoke again. He has been remembering that minute for ten years.seed ②

He watches her across the dining room for the better part of a year without a word. Then comes carnival night—a masked ball, the rules loosened, everyone permitted the familiar you. Hans goes over to her, and what he cannot say in German he says in French: bodies, fever, lungs, and the claim that love and death are one and the same thing. Switching languages is a way of issuing yourself a disclaimer—it is not him talking, it is the man speaking French.

And the first thing he says is to ask her for a pencil.payoff ② ↑ The gesture from that schoolyard break is performed again, unchanged: same nerve, same pretext, same narrow eyes. Mann explains nothing and lets no character point it out; he simply allows the reader to notice that the one initiative of Hans Castorp's life is the second performance of a single gesture. A borrowed pencil is at once a prop, a password, and a diagnosis of the man holding it.

"Don't forget to give me back my pencil."— Clavdia Chauchat, the last line of the "Walpurgis Night" chapter (said in French in the original)

She leaves the Berghof the next day, and her parting gift is not a photograph but her chest X-ray. He carries the glass plate in his breast pocket the way other men carry a portrait.payoff ③ ↑ Ever since the day he saw his own bones he has been taught a new way of looking: the truest part of a person is on the inside, in the lesion. So what he falls in love with is not a face but a negative—and every learned speech in this novel about love and death being one thing says less than that piece of glass in his pocket.

Act three · Two tutors, and a snowstorm

The real fight over Hans is between two men. Lodovico Settembrini is an Italian in shiny old checked trousers, living off small fees and relatives; his grandfather was a revolutionary, his father a humanist, and he himself is contributing to a great work for a progressive league—an encyclopaedia that will classify and catalogue every form of human suffering, because he genuinely believes that indexing pain is the first step toward abolishing it. He believes in reason, literature, the republic; he distrusts everything that turns a person inward, music included. He sees at once that Hans has come up here to play with fire, urges him on their first meeting to go back down, and gives him a title—

"life's problem child"— Settembrini's standing name for Hans Castorp

Meaning: you are not wicked, you are just the sort of boy who will find something nice to say about dangerous things.

Then Leo Naphta arrives and turns the education into a war. A Galician Jew whose father worked as a ritual slaughterer and was killed in a pogrom, taken up and trained by a Jesuit, now a scholar and a believer who teaches Latin below and lives up here for his lungs; his rooms are lined with silk and hold a carved pietà, which fits nothing about the ascetic in black who occupies them. Settembrini calls him a terrorist of the spirit. Naphta's case is that the entire package handed down from the Renaissance—the individual, freedom, reason, progress, business—is a fraud: its freedom is the freedom of people with money, its peace merely hides violence inside contracts and procedures, and what human beings actually need is not liberation but discipline. Every sentence he says is hard to refute, because he aims exactly at the soft places in the other man's creed.

Hans enjoys all of it enormously, nods at both, commits to neither; his excuse is the Latin tag Settembrini likes—it pleases one to experiment. That is the most dangerous thing in the book: a man without strong convictions will collect every extreme position as an interesting exhibit.

The answer, when it comes, comes not from either tutor but from a snowstorm. That winter Hans learns to ski and goes off alone to the empty heights. A blizzard drops on him, visibility goes to nothing, he cannot tell up from down, and after following the wind for a long time he discovers he has circled back to the same hut. He props himself against its wall, takes a swallow from his flask, and half freezes into unconsciousness.

He dreams, and the dream has two halves. The first: a southern sea, sunlight, meadows, a company of beautiful people moving among trees and horses and children, courteous to one another with a gentleness he has never seen in any human being. He follows them to a temple, whose doorway is black, and he goes in. The second: inside, two shapeless hags are hunched over a basin, tearing a small child apart and eating it, cursing obscenely while they do.

He wakes still lying in the snow, not far from freezing to death, and half-conscious he works something out: the two hags are underneath the sunlit people—the reason those people are so gentle and so courteous is precisely that they know what is inside the temple, and are good to one another anyway. He compresses this into one sentence, the high point of the book:

"For the sake of goodness and love, man shall grant death no dominion over his thoughts."— the sentence Hans Castorp arrives at in the blizzard ("Snow," chapter 6; the only sentence Mann sets in italics)

Notice its architecture: it does not deny death, it only refuses death the throne. This is neither Settembrini's optimism (death as a backwardness to be swept away) nor Naphta's infatuation (death as the only reality) but Hans's own third road.seed ④ Then he gets up, skis back to the Berghof, and is in time for dinner. Mann adds the quietest knife in the novel: by evening the sentence has already begun to fade in him.

Act four · Joachim comes back, and dies

The cousin's patience runs out. The doctors keep extending him; finally he packs without permission and goes down—the book calls it a wild departure. He really does become an officer, really does ride, really does have a few months of the life he wanted.

Then he is carried back up. The disease climbs into his larynx; he cannot speak and writes on slips of paper. In his last days a beard comes in and turns him overnight from a young man into a stranger, grave and unfamiliar. He dies very quietly, with something like a smile, as if he had finally been handed the pass he spent three years waiting for. The book's verdict on him is four words: as a soldier, and brave. He is the only person who refuses the magic mountain from beginning to end and insists on going back to duty, and he is the only major character who dies, and he did nothing wrong—there is no moral bookkeeping in this novel, only biology.

With Joachim dead, Hans has no remaining reason to leave. Now time accelerates: Mann stops writing day by day and starts sliding through stretches of years. There is a soup that arrives the same every single day, so that you cannot tell one from another—the book names it everlasting soup. Nobody asks the date any more; they ask how many months you've been up.

The deep well he heard as a child in all those greats is now something he has fallen into entirely.payoff ① ↑ Back then the booming remoteness merely sounded lovely; now he knows what it was—the noise time makes when nobody is measuring it. A boy with a taste for the bottomless, dropped into a place with no calendar, is not going to want to leave. Mann laid his temperament on the table in chapter one, and readers filed it under family background.

Two things happen in the long stall. First, Pieter Peeperkorn arrives: a Dutch coffee planter from Java, white hair like a crown, incapable of finishing a sentence—he begins, raises a hand, makes a magnificent gesture, and the sentence dissolves. Yet everyone is subdued by him, and the two inexhaustible tutors look beside him like a pair of squabbling birds. The woman he brings with him is Clavdia, returned; and Hans, instead of being jealous, becomes the old man's friend—he has finally met a personality that dominates a room on sheer vital force rather than on opinions. When the old tropical fever has drained him, Peeperkorn injects himself with venom from a small device modelled on a snake's tooth.

Second, the sanatorium buys a gramophone. Hans appoints himself keeper of the records and picks out five favourites, one of them Schubert's Der Lindenbaum, from Winterreise: the linden tree by the well at the gate, its leaves rustling as if calling a man who has walked away to come back, telling him there is rest for him here.seed ⑤ Here Mann does something he almost never does and says it straight out: the song is beautiful, but what it beckons toward is death, and a man who loves it all the way down has taken death's side.

Act five · The great irritability, a helmet nobody recognizes, a thunderclap

In the final stretch the air changes. Everybody starts picking fights: at table, in the corridors, over remarks of no consequence, until faces are red and the sedatives run short. The book calls it the great irritability—the most prophetic passage in it, Europe in 1913 moved into a single building.

Krokowski's interests slide from psychology into the occult. He convenes séances with a young girl as medium. The circle sits, the girl sweats and twitches and moans for a long time, and then there is a figure in the chair: Joachim, beard and all, looking better than he did at the end; and on his head is a helmet nobody in the room has ever seenseed ⑥, of no army's issue in that year. Hans says nothing at all. He gets up, turns on the electric light, and walks out.

At about the same time the tutors' argument runs out of language and Naphta demands a duel. Settembrini, whose life has been a campaign against exactly this barbarism, has to accept—which is the sharpest irony the novel plays on his faith that persuasion solves things. On the ground he fires into the air; Naphta shouts a word about cowardice at him, turns the pistol on his own head, and fires. The verdict on several hundred pages of debate is brutally short: the man who preached terror executes it first upon himself, while the humanist, standing in a ritual he spent his life denouncing, preserves his principle with a shot at the sky and saves nobody.

Then the thunderclap. In August 1914 the news comes up the mountain and the Berghof scatters overnight: passports, telegrams, luggage, farewells, people who have not gone down in years fighting for train tickets. Hans goes too.payoff ⑥ ↑ And the unrecognizable helmet from the séance is explained: not a ghost's odd costume but a thing that will be mass-produced a few years later, the next war worn early on behalf of everyone. Mann takes the one supernatural event in this vast realist novel and uses it as physical evidence mailed back from the future.

The last page is a muddy plain, rain, shellfire, a line of raw infantry pushing forward. One of them is in his twenties and we know the face under the helmet rim. He falls, gets up, and he is humming something—the linden tree by the gate.payoff ⑤ ↑ That record was chosen in a warm room to the applause of fellow patients, and Mann warned us then: whoever loves this song has taken death's side. The warning is now redeemed. He was not dragged off the mountain by the war; he walked in singing.

And here Mann does something few novelists dare: he says goodbye to his protagonist directly, in the second person—farewell, life's problem child; we told your story not because you matter, you do not particularly; whether you survive we do not know, and the odds are poor.payoff ④ ↑ The sentence worked out in the snow and fading by dinner is finally taken to its real examination hall: a man who cannot keep hold of his own insight is thrown into a machine designed to let death rule everybody's thoughts. The book's final sentence is a question—out of this worldwide festival of death, will love one day rise? Mann does not answer. He leaves the question mark standing in the rain.

Put the five acts together and the fuse is not an event but a negative: nobody ever makes Hans Castorp do anything. Staying, not leaving, spending seven years on fevers, debates, records and a plate of glass—all his own. And what finally takes him off the mountain is not a single thing he learned there but a noise that has nothing to do with him. That is the genuinely frightening part: a man can be thoroughly educated, opened, heightened—and still have an external event decide for him.

Three slow-motion replays

1 · "Snow": putting the book's answer inside an unconscious man

The technical choice is close to reckless: the novel's highest truth is arrived at by a man half-conscious and freezing to death. Why stage it that way? Because two characters have already spent hundreds of pages arguing in the open, and the reader has learned by then to distrust anyone making a case—Settembrini states, Naphta dismantles, and any proposition that arrives in the form of an argument goes straight into that mincer. So Mann changes the physical state of the answer: not a proposition, an image. You cannot refute a dream.

The order is crueller still: beauty first, so you believe it; then horror, so you are sick; then the discovery that they are one world. Reverse the order and the chapter is cheap consolation. And the last screw, tightened by the author's own hand—letting Hans forget—is what fixes the book's rank. Had he remembered and lived differently ever after, The Magic Mountain would be an ordinary novel of education. He forgets, and the book becomes a colder question: can a human being hold on to his own best five minutes?

2 · Walpurgis Night: saying seven years' worth in a foreign language

The novel is written in German; in this one scene the hero speaks French, and not especially good French. A masked ball is already the one night a year when transgression is licensed—put on a mask and you need not be yourself—and a foreign language is a second mask on top of the first. More to the point, "I love your illness, your fever, your lung" cannot be said in a mother tongue; German would rule it a foul instantly, whereas a foreign language installs a pane of glass between the man and the sentence. And German readers without French are shut out for a stretch—you get the sensation of eavesdropping on someone's privacy, which is exactly the effect Mann is after.

The pivot of the whole scene is the pencil. Mann does not have Hans say "you remind me of someone." He has him perform an action. A line of dialogue can only carry information; a repeated gesture carries fate. In that one second the reader understands two things that were never written down: Hans's desire was fixed in adolescence, and every initiative of his life runs along one very narrow rail.

3 · The last page: the author drops his hero in front of you

The ending is extreme. The narrator turns from teller into someone watching a man walk away, addresses Hans in the second person, admits he does not know what becomes of him, and casually belittles the person he has spent a thousand pages on. Why does this work? Because it punctures the membrane between the novel and history. For a thousand pages the Berghof was a sealed laboratory where time could be stretched and compressed and characters argued over at leisure; the moment the war arrives the walls come down and the author loses his privileges—he cannot follow his protagonist into the trench, because there are several million people in there just as important, or just as unimportant, as Hans. The failure of the narration is the measure of the war.

Then look at what stands after the full stop: not a conclusion but a question, and a question about love—very nearly naive at the end of a novel in which two thinkers have just strangled each other. But that is the last echo of the sentence in the snow: refusing death the throne, in practice, means daring to ask something about love at the least plausible moment. The question goes unanswered, as German readers in 1924 could tell, and as Thomas Mann in 1939 knew better than anyone.

The book's central device: time

The Magic Mountain has no hard speculative premise. Its signature device is harder to build: it does not write about time, it manufactures time and makes you undergo it. There are four parts.

1 · Telling-time and told-time deliberately uncoupled

The most easily felt and least easily described fact about this book is that the further in you go, the faster time moves. Hans's first day takes dozens of pages—what was at breakfast, how the blankets are wrapped, how his face burns. The first three weeks take a large fraction of the novel. The last two or three years go by in a few dozen pages.

This is not an author running out of steam. It is the physical length of a novel used as an instrument: while everything is new, a day outweighs a year; once it is habit, a year does not outweigh a day. Anyone who has moved house, changed jobs, been hospitalized or shut in one place for a long time recognizes the curve. If the opening feels unbearably slow and the end slips through your fingers, that is not a flaw; that is the content.

2 · The units of measurement stop working

The mountain runs a whole apparatus for putting time out of true. Seven minutes with a thermometer stretch enormously, because you are waiting; a month counts for nothing, and a doctor saying "another six months" sounds like "another drink." There is the identical everlasting soup. When every graduation is void, what remains is pure duration—which is the magic of this mountain: it does not make you happy, it makes you unable to say how long it has been.

3 · Illness as a privilege

People up here do not work, do not support families, do not carry consequences, because they are ill. Illness issues them a sick note, and a sick note seven years long becomes an identity. Disease here is not suffering, it is exemption—an expensive pass out of the flatland's obligations, usually paid for by relatives below; Hans lives seven years on his family's income. Mann never hides it: the Berghof is a utopia maintained jointly by money and lungs.

4 · "Heightening" in a sealed retort

Mann's own account of the book was alchemical: seal an ordinary material in a vessel, heat it, hold it, let it alter and refine—his word was Steigerung, heightening. Hans is the material, and the first condition of heightening is separation from the world. Honestly, though: the model in which isolation produces growth is a literary device, not life advice—in reality most people isolated for seven years have simply been isolated for seven years. Mann knew that better than anyone, which is why he never lets Hans graduate. He blows up the classroom with a shell instead.

What it makes you think about

question 1
Two men both more articulate than you—one wants to save you with reason, the other with an iron hand. On what basis do you choose?

The strongest case for Settembrini is not "progress is good." It is this: every compulsion exercised in the name of final justice ends up concentrated in one person who decides who is to be liquidated. That is not a historical possibility, it is the historical norm. So even if reason, parliaments and slow reform look sluggish and hypocritical, they keep one thing available: the possibility of reversing the last decision. Naphta's scheme has no such slot. And note the physical evidence the novel supplies: the first person Naphta uses terror on is himself.

The strongest case for Naphta is not "order above freedom" either. It is a splinter that is very hard to pull out: who exactly is paying for that humanitarian peace? It offers free speech, but the opportunity to speak is distributed with property; it opposes violence, while the order it runs on rests on other people's lives; it compiles suffering into an encyclopaedia whose editors sit in heated rooms and whose subjects are down the mine. Hiding violence inside procedure is not the abolition of violence; it only makes it invisible and harder to charge to anyone's account.

The novel's own move is to have Hans choose neither, and not pretend he can reconcile them: the sentence in the snow is a simultaneous refusal of both tutors—I will let neither of you rule my thoughts. But is that a position or an evasion? A man who listens to everything and believes nothing looks open-minded, right up until the thunder, at which point he goes where the crowd goes. Every public argument today is full of Hans Castorps: both sides out-argue our judgement, so we adopt "I can see merit on both sides" as an intellectual virtue. Mann does not say this is wrong. He just shows you where such a person ends up.

question 2
Is sympathy with death depth, or a slow form of suicide?

Mann had a name for the novel's disease: Sympathie mit dem Tode, sympathy with death. Hans has it; German culture has it; the linden tree is its loveliest form.

Put the case for it at full strength: a civilization that refuses to look at death is shallow. The healthy people below send the sick up the mountain to be out of sight, and the Berghof itself carries its corpses out by a back route so as not to disturb the other patients' appetites; their optimism is bought by not looking. And soon after arriving, Hans does the most moving thing in the book: he starts visiting the dying nobody visits, bringing flowers, sitting by the bed and listening. It is the one unambiguously good act of his life, and its source is precisely his unhealthy interest in death. Being able to sit beside a dying person is part of what depth means, and it cannot be bought or taught.

Now the other side, at its most damning: people who aestheticize death end up walking into the abattoir voluntarily. In 1914 educated young Europeans who had read the Romantics went to the trains singing. They did not think they were going to die; they thought they were going toward something solemn and at last meaningful. The last page demonstrates the mechanism exactly: the man humming the linden tree as he stumbles through the mud learned to love that song in a warm room a few years earlier. Loving death does not mean wanting to die—but on the day someone needs you to die, you will resist a little less than the next man.

Bring it up to now: depression styled as temperament, overwork worn as a medal, self-destruction packaged as clear-sightedness, suffering treated as proof of seriousness. All modern variations on the same linden tree. They are indeed closer to the truth than cheap positivity, which is exactly what makes them dangerous. Mann's sentence marks out a very narrow middle line: you may look straight at death; you may not let it govern. The hard part is not understanding that. The hard part is keeping hold of it, which Hans could not do.

question 3
A thunderclap cancels every private perplexity at once. Is that a release?

When war breaks out, the reaction at the Berghof is not fear but exhilaration: people who have not gone down in years pack overnight and turn brisk and purposeful. What nobody could decide for Hans in seven years is decided for him by a news bulletin.

The strongest case for calling it release: for a person drowning in unlimited possibility and unlimited debate, the worst part is not the absence of an answer but the absence of a demand. War arrives, and for the first time somebody needs him by name; he has a position that requires no justification—where to go, what to do, whom to stand with, all solved. The hunger for certainty runs much deeper than the hunger for happiness, and people who say they felt most alive during a crisis are telling the truth.

The strongest case against: that is not release, it is outsourcing your choices to a mincing machine. Hans goes down without knowing what he is going to do; he only knows he no longer has to think—and not having to think is precisely what Naphta was offering: discipline, enrolment, relief from the burden of freedom. So the cruelty of the ending is not that he may die. It is that he is resolved by the war rather than by his education: a thousand pages of intellectual training lose to one mobilization that requires no thought at all.

The same structure occurs weekly on a small scale. Work is stuck in an unanswerable tangle, an emergency lands, and you feel unaccountably lighter; a relationship rots along undecided until some external event ends it for you, and you grieve and exhale at the same time. The question worth asking is how much of that lightness came from the problem being solved, and how much from your no longer having to solve it. The book's reminder is cold: external events never solve problems, they only remove the person who was asking.

Motifs: what Mann kept writing

Line his books up and the obsession is visible at once: why does a decent, capable, well-run world keep having to pay out one son who is unfit to live in it?

Buddenbrooks tracks four generations of a grain-merchant family downward: the business gets worse while the people get finer, more musical, more sensitive, until the last boy burns with fever over music and dies of typhus—refinement and viability, in Mann, are a single see-saw. Death in Venice takes a writer who disciplined himself for a lifetime and destroys him in a plague city with the beauty of a boy. Doctor Faustus gives a composer twenty-four years of genius in exchange for syphilis, while Germany completes the national version of the same bargain. Illness in Mann is never only illness; it is a cheque drawn against depth, and cheques get presented. The Magic Mountain is the one time the protagonist does not die inside the book—which is not a pardon but a deferral: he is sent to a larger, collective lesion instead.

There is also Mann's own political turn, and this novel sits exactly on the hinge. During the First World War he wrote a thick book, Reflections of a Nonpolitical Man, defending German "culture" against the Western democratic men of letters represented by his brother Heinrich; in 1922 he gave a public speech endorsing the Weimar Republic; after 1933 he went into exile, first Switzerland, then the United States. Settembrini carries traces of the figure Mann used to mock, and Naphta carries the abyss Mann himself had once found fascinating. The book gives no prize to either side because its author had stood on both.

Criticism and controversy

Without flattery, several charges against this book are well founded:

  • It talks too much. The tutors' debates run to hundreds of pages, and many readers—professional ones included—think they long ago exceeded any plausible length for dialogue and became the author stuffing his reading notes into two mouths. For a stretch the second half has no events at all, only positions. Readers who want plot abandon it here, and that is not entirely their fault.
  • The Naphta problem. One model for him is generally taken to be the Hungarian Marxist György Lukács, whom Mann met in the early 1920s. The trouble is the recipe: Jewish birth plus Jesuit training plus advocacy of terror plus morbid asceticism, which a substantial body of criticism reads as carrying the antisemitic stereotypes current at the time. Mann's own record is not clean—an early story of his used unmistakable Jewish caricature and he withdrew it himself after writing it—while his wife Katia came from a Jewish family and he became one of the most implacable exiled opponents of the Nazis. These facts do not cancel one another.
  • The women are thin. Clavdia has almost no interior; she is an object of looking—narrow eyes, the slammed door, a slackness and formlessness the novel calls Asiatic. She is written as the East incarnate, set against West European discipline, and that opposition reads badly now. Defenders point out that the male characters are also vehicles for ideas; the difference is that each man owns an entire worldview and the women own none.
  • Is the ending a dodge? A thousand-page novel of education whose hero grows into nothing and is then collected by a world war. Critics say the author had written himself into a corner: he could not send Hans down to an ordinary life (that would negate the book) and could not grant him real enlightenment (too cheap), so he ended it with history. Defenders say that is the whole point—a generation whose personal endings were replaced by history.
  • The accuracy of the cure. Alpine treatment was already medically contested when Mann was writing, and antibiotics later voided the entire system. It is easy to read the Berghof as exotica; it was an expensive business of dubious efficacy that separated patients from society for years.
  • Translation. The long-standard English version was Helen Lowe-Porter's of 1927, much disputed for errors and style; John E. Woods's 1995 translation is generally considered more accurate. The book depends heavily on German tone and irony, and irony is precisely the thinnest layer, the one that evaporates in translation—not the translator's fault but the nature of the thing.

Ten lines to take away

1 · A man books three weeks and stays seven years—the question is never why he stayed, but what makes us so sure we wouldn't.

2 · Illness on the mountain is not suffering but exemption: a very expensive sick note that buys you out of responsibility.

3 · What he falls in love with is not a face but a chest X-ray—the mountain has taught him that the truest part of a person is inside, in the lesion.

4 · A borrowed pencil is more accurate than any declaration: the one initiative of his life is the second performance of a gesture from ten years earlier.

5 · "For the sake of goodness and love, man shall grant death no dominion over his thoughts"—the book's highest sentence, arrived at by a man close to freezing, and already fading by dinner.

6 · Two eloquent tutors argue for hundreds of pages; the verdict is one line: the man who preaches terror executes it first on himself.

7 · The only character who insists on going back down to duty is the one who dies. There is no moral bookkeeping here, only biology.

8 · Magnifying glass at the front, fast-forward at the back—your impatience while reading is the experience the book intends to give you.

9 · The unrecognizable helmet at the séance is the novel's single supernatural event, used as physical evidence mailed back from the future.

10 · In the end the war does not drag him off the mountain. He walks in humming a song about a linden tree.
