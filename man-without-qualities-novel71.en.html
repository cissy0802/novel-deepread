Novels, Deep-Read · Robert Musil · No.71

The Man Without Qualities

Robert Musil · vol. 1 1930 / vol. 2 1932 / posthumous volume 1943 · unfinished · roughly 1,700 pages

中文 →

Vienna, August 1913. A lorry knocks a pedestrian down in the street. A crowd forms; somebody says an ambulance should be called, somebody else checks the time. A gentleman passing by explains the matter to the lady on his arm: according to American statistics, this many people die in such accidents every year, and this is the relation between braking distance and speed. He is entirely accurate. The lady listens, feels something unpleasant in the pit of her stomach, and then the feeling passes. The stretcher is carried off and the street closes over the gap.
That is the novel's first scene. The injured man never appears again in the next seventeen hundred pages.
This is Musil's subject: something happens, is instantly translated into numbers and procedure, and therefore might as well not have happened.

This page contains complete spoilers — what a year of committee meetings produces, how Agathe forges their father's will, how Clarisse loses her mind, how far the brother and sister go, and the most important fact about the book: it has no ending, because its author died in the middle of it. Ulrich takes a year's leave from life in August 1913, which means the leave expires in August 1914 — and that page was never written. Without knowing that, there is nothing to discuss.

In one sentence

A thirty-two-year-old Austrian who has been a cavalry officer, an engineer and a mathematician — good at all three, finished with all three — takes a year's leave from life in August 1913 to find out what a man trained in precision is supposed to do with himself. During that year he becomes honorary secretary of a patriotic committee charged with discovering a Great Austrian Idea for the emperor's seventieth jubilee, which meets for twelve months and finds nothing; he becomes obsessed with a carpenter who murdered a prostitute; and at his father's funeral he meets the sister he had almost forgotten, with whom he decides to withdraw from the world into a kind of life that has no name yet. His leave expires in August 1914, and his author did not live to write that page.

Coordinates

Robert Musil (1880–1942) was Austrian. He trained as a mechanical engineer, worked as an assistant at a technical college, built an instrument for colour-perception experiments, then switched to philosophy and psychology and took a doctorate in 1908 with a dissertation on the physicist Ernst Mach. In 1906 he made his name with a boarding-school novel, The Confusions of Young Master Törless, and then spent more than thirty years on the book he never finished: volume one in 1930, volume two in 1932, and in 1938 he pulled twenty already-typeset chapters back from the printer to rewrite them. That same year Austria was annexed by Germany; Musil went into exile in Switzerland and died in Geneva in April 1942, almost unnoticed. The book only began to be read after Adolf Frisé's edition of 1952.

Its place in literary history is clear enough: it belongs on the shelf with Ulysses, In Search of Lost Time and The Magic Mountain, but it is doing something else. Joyce rebuilt perception, Proust rebuilt memory; Musil rebuilt argument — he made the essay the basic unit of the novel and asked thinking to do the work of plot. The price is visible: half the reason this book is unfinished is the author's death, and the other half is the nature of the machine itself.

The story

Act one · August 1913: a man gives himself a year off

The accident is written, the ambulance leaves, and the narrator walks away with it without once looking back at the man on the ground. Two chapters later we meet the protagonist: Ulrich, thirty-two, whose family name the narrator explicitly declines to give us — a surname would make him a member of some family, some clan, and that is precisely what this book is dismantling. He has made three attempts at becoming a man of importance: cavalry officer, where he discovered that the weight a uniform gives a man is borrowed; engineer, where he discovered that his colleagues were precise only on the drawing board; mathematician, where he genuinely had the gift.

What stops him is a newspaper. A racehorse is described in print as a genius.seed ① If the word can be attached to a horse, what exactly is a man spending half his life trying to be called? So he grants himself a year's leave — a holiday from life, as he puts it. He rents a small château with a garden and sets out to furnish it according to his own personality, only to find he cannot produce one: he can argue for every style and finds all of them faintly ridiculous, and ends by handing the list to the suppliers to work out themselves. A man without qualities is not a man with no character; he is a man who refuses to pretend that the available characters are his own.

Three people stand near him. Bonadea: a judge's wife whose carriage stops after three thugs beat Ulrich in the street, who takes him home and becomes his mistress; her nickname is borrowed from the Roman goddess of chastity, and she herself admits to having two lives. Walter and Clarisse: a friend from his youth and that friend's wife. Walter falls just short at everything and ends up a civil servant; Clarisse is strung too tight, and the engagement present Walter gave her was the complete works of Nietzsche.seed ②

Part One closes with a letter from Ulrich's father, a jurist of some standing, urging his son to take a proper position and complaining in passing about the scholarly feud he has been waging for years: he and a colleague cannot agree on a point of criminal law — whether a man can be held partly responsible for what he does, and how that sentence should be worded in the statute. Neither of them knows that the dullest argument in the book will decide a man's life several hundred pages later.seed ③

Act two · The Parallel Campaign: an empire shopping for a slogan

First the country. Musil's nickname for Austria-Hungary is Kakania (Kakanien), taken from the official prefix k.k. — kaiserlich-königlich, imperial-royal — a state that needed two adjectives to say its own name, and a word that also sounds like a child's term for excrement. His tone toward it is peculiar: the mockery carries affection, as though he were writing about an old man who has stopped believing in himself but still has excellent manners.

The engine of the plot is the Parallel Campaign (Parallelaktion). 1918 will mark seventy years of Emperor Franz Joseph's reign, while the Germans will be celebrating thirty years of Wilhelm II in the same year. Seventy against thirty should be an easy win — but with what content? So the old aristocrat Count Leinsdorf founds a committee whose task is not to arrange a ceremony but first to discover a Great Idea adequate to Austria, the ceremony being merely its shell. Ulrich becomes honorary secretary through his father's connections, and is the only person on the committee who expects nothing.

The venue is the drawing room of Diotima — the wife of Section Chief Tuzzi of the Foreign Ministry, nicknamed by Ulrich after the priestess who taught Socrates what love is. She sincerely believes the age requires a soul; her practical diplomat of a husband mainly wants to establish which ministry these people fall under. The brightest object in the room is Paul Arnheim: a Prussian industrialist who also writes books reconciling the soul with economics, a genuine celebrity (his acknowledged model is the German industrialist and author Walther Rathenau). The whole room is besotted with him, Diotima falls in love with him, and his real reason for being in Vienna is the Galician oil fields.

A newspaper calling a horse a genius looks like a passing joke; here the joke acquires a body. Arnheim discourses on the soul and on civilisation, is taken by an entire capital for the voice of the age, and buys oil fields — he is the man with qualities, Ulrich's opposite. Once the word genius has been devalued, the remaining business is selling it. What makes the move elegant is that Musil refuses to let him be a fraud: Arnheim believes every word he writes, which is what makes him impossible to dismiss.payoff ① ↑

Two more people to remember. General Stumm von Bordwehr, the army's liaison, whose real errand is an artillery appropriation, but who sincerely wants to help these disorderly civilians by drawing up an order of battle for all the ideas of the age (see the next section). And Hans Sepp, a young Germanic-nationalist student full of blood, soil and the true German spirit, an anti-Semite, who is courting the daughter of a Jewish bank director. The thread carries little weight and sits in the most important place in the book: the Great Idea the committee cannot find in a year, Hans Sepp and his circle already have — it requires no discussion and no content, only blood. Musil wrote this character in around 1930, before Germany turned. The committee's harvest for the year: a stack of minutes, a name still undecided, and a fatigue everyone has begun to feel and nobody can describe.

Act three · Moosbrugger: a murder becomes a formula

Running parallel to all this, the newspapers are covering a trial. Moosbrugger, an itinerant carpenter, a big man with big hands, killed a prostitute who followed him one night and mutilated the body. His account is deranged, logical and internally consistent: the woman would not leave him alone, he felt surrounded, he had no choice. He does not think he is mad; he thinks he is a craftsman who kept the rules all his life and was once backed into a corner.

What the court is arguing about is not whether he did it — he did, everyone knows it — but whether he is responsible, and how much. The psychiatric assessments contradict each other, one finding illness and the other lucidity, and the statute offers nothing between fully responsible and not responsible at all. So the entire courtroom begins debating the wording of a phrase.

The father's dreary lawsuit comes due here. What he spent half his life fighting over is whether and how criminal law should write down diminished responsibility — whether a man can be partly insane. In Part One this was an old scholar's hobby-horse, the paragraph a son skips when reading a letter; here it is a knot around a living man's neck.payoff ③ ↑ Musil never resolves the thread, and that is the answer: nobody is responsible because responsibility has been successfully distributed among phrasings. The reader saw the same procedure on page one — the man knocked down by the lorry was also digested by a correct explanation; this time the procedure is called law.

The strangest thing in these chapters is Moosbrugger himself. He is pleased by the dignity of the proceedings and enjoys being taken seriously by so many respectable people; but the moment anyone describes his case slightly wrong, he corrects them. He would rather be treated as a man who did this thing than as a patient who need not answer for it — the one thing he still holds is ownership of his own act. Ulrich is transfixed by him, and thinks something to this effect:

"If mankind could dream collectively, it would dream Moosbrugger."— Part One, Ulrich on Moosbrugger (in substance)

Clarisse is gripped by the same man from the other end. She has been reading Nietzsche for a decade and has read him into an assignment: one must become who one is, one must prise this slack world open from the inside. In her hands the engagement present is not a book but a set of instructions.payoff ② ↑ She decides first that she must bear a redeemer, then that Moosbrugger, whom the whole world has written off, is the one who truly needs her, and gets her doctor brother to arrange a way into the asylum to see him. Once someone decides to live by a book, the better the book, the harder the fall.

Act four · The forgotten sister

Volume two changes temperature. The father dies; Ulrich travels back to the provincial house to deal with the funeral and meets, inside it, a person he had nearly forgotten existed: his sister Agathe. They come upon each other having both just changed into loose lounging clothes, almost identically dressed, and somebody says the line about being Siamese twins (that scene is in the next section). For the next several hundred pages, the centre of the book moves out of the drawing room and into the space between these two.

Their father left detailed written instructions for his own laying-out: how the body should lie, which decorations go where. They follow them. When it is done, Agathe quietly slips one of her garters into the coffin.seed ④ She cannot say why, except that a man who lived his whole life by the statute ought to take one thing with him that he never approved.

Agathe's second husband is Hagauer, a pedagogue: progressive, diligent, methodical about everything, a man you cannot fault and cannot look at twice. She has decided not to go back, and carries a capsule of poison — not to use, but to know the option exists. Then she does it: their father's will contains a codicil in Hagauer's favour, and she alters it, in front of her brother, making no secret of it. Ulrich knows perfectly well that this is forgery; he argues briefly, does not stop her, and does not report it. The title of that part of volume two is Into the Millennium (The Criminals).

The garter pays off here. Beside her father's body she inserted something private and irregular into a rite executed strictly to instruction, and it looked like nothing more than a sentimental gesture; a few chapters later she makes the same gesture with a legal document as its object. The same hand: tenderness the first time, crime the second, and she herself can barely tell the two apart. What makes the planting so good is that Musil got the reader to accept the small gesture first, so that the forged will does not provoke condemnation as a first reaction.payoff ④ ↑

Brother and sister move in together in Vienna and begin the experiment they call the Other Condition (der andere Zustand): a life that runs on neither possession nor purpose nor action, in which the boundary between a person and the world loosens and love stops pointing at a particular object. There is unmistakable erotic charge between them, which the book never denies and never acts on (some of the surviving drafts do). These hundreds of pages are the most beautiful and the hardest to read in the novel: almost nothing happens, only two people talking and the light between their sentences. Outside, the world keeps idling — Arnheim does not marry Diotima and gets his oil fields; the Parallel Campaign still has no Idea. In this stretch Agathe meets Lindner, a pious schoolmaster whose timetable and whose prayers are scheduled to the minute; he resolves to save her soul and she half-plays along — the cruellest mirror the book holds up to the Other Condition: he too is living a highly spiritual life, except that his soul runs on a timetable.

Act five · No ending

Here the book stops. Musil went into exile in Switzerland and died in Geneva in April 1942, still revising a chapter of the siblings' conversation in his last days. The drafts show roughly where it was going: Clarisse goes thoroughly mad; the Other Condition runs out and is shown to be unsustainable inside the world; and Kakania is about to receive the Great Idea it finally found — the war. He did not merely fail to finish; the thing cannot be finished: his method requires every proposition to be met with an equally strong opposite, and a method like that has no terminus.

Put the five acts together and the fuse in this book is not an event. It is the calendar. Ulrich grants himself a year in August 1913, and the whole novel is that year: an empire spends it in meetings looking for its own meaning, a court spends it arguing over the wording of a phrase, a brother and sister spend it trying out a new way to live, and not one line reaches a conclusion. The last month of that year is August 1914. Every question the book cannot answer is cancelled simultaneously, off the page, by a mobilisation order — not solved, cancelled. Which is why this unfinished novel does not read like a fragment: its ending is not in the author's hands but in history's, and every reader knows what is written there.

Three scenes in slow motion

One · Page one: two ways of saying the same thing

The weather report that opens the book rewards a slow read. Musil uses the language of the meteorological office: isobars, a barometric low, monthly mean temperatures, the irregular movement of water vapour — every word correct, not one of them any help with the question of what the day was like. Then he closes it off in plain, slightly old-fashioned speech:

"It was a fine day in August 1913."— Part One, chapter 1 (original, very short sentence)

This is not a joke at the expense of science. It is a demonstration: there are two languages for the same fact, they do not translate into each other, and a person can only live in the second one. The choice of vantage is harsher still: the opening pages belong to nobody, they belong to a city — traffic, voices, deaths in the statistical sense. We walk through a world with no protagonist for two chapters before being led to Ulrich, by which point the lesson has landed: in this place, one person does not matter. The accident that follows is the same joke with a body in it — the gentleman is entirely right, and the more right he is, the less the man on the ground exists.

Two · A general draws up an order of battle for the ideas of the age

This is the funniest scene in the book and also the least funny. General Stumm concludes that what ails the Parallel Campaign is a lack of order, and resolves to help in the army's way: inventory every important idea of the age, rank them, identify the greatest idea in the world, and present it to the committee. He takes an adjutant into the Imperial Library, calls up the catalogue, and sets to work.

The comedy comes in three layers. First, the ideas run into the tens of thousands, and every great idea is paired with an equally great opposite — the ranking fails at step one. Second, he meets a library attendant who knows the holdings exhaustively and never reads, because opening any one book would cost him his grasp of the whole — the most accurate portrait of modern knowledge in the novel. Third, he is the only character in the book who actually completes his assignment: he comes back with a firm conclusion, namely that it cannot be done.

The technique is taking a metaphor literally: other people call ideas a battlefield as a figure of speech, so Musil sends a general to draw the map. But the scene is more than satire, it is the book's self-portrait — a novel attempting to inventory the ideas of an age dispatches a stout general to the library to announce, on its behalf, that the job is impossible.

Three · The siblings meet: from the long satirical lens to two faces

The meeting that opens volume two is the book's largest change of key. Ulrich pushes open the door of his father's house and sees a woman in loose house clothes; she has just changed into the same kind of thing. They stand almost mirrored, first awkward, then obscurely familiar.

Three technical points. The information is withheld and then released: for a thousand pages we barely know he has a sister — she is mentioned as an item of funeral business — and then she is standing there, filling the frame. The eroticism is carried by the clothes, not the sentences: nothing suggestive is said, and the two identical garments do all of it. And then the tempo: from this scene on the sentences lengthen, slow and turn inward, and the satire visibly loses its edge. Many readers give up here, and that modulation is precisely the structural event of the novel — in volume one Ulrich could express the way out he wanted only by refusing things; now he has found someone to refuse them with, so the book is obliged to move from mockery to attempt, and mockery is what Musil is best at, while nobody is good at the attempt.

The machine inside this book

One · Qualities (Eigenschaften): the attributes are off the rack, and they are not yours

Neither "qualities" nor "characteristics" quite renders the German: Eigenschaft is closer to a property, something a thing can be described as having. Ulrich's discovery is that enormous quantities of attributes are in circulation — courage, hypocrisy, pragmatism, profundity — and they are ready-made, public and interchangeable, not grown out of any particular person. Hence there are qualities without a man, and a person is merely the place where some of them happen to intersect. He is not short of them; he refuses to pretend they belong to him. None of this was invented out of nothing: his doctorate was on Ernst Mach, and Mach's position that the ego is nothing but a bundle of sensations and is unsalvageable is this novel's philosophical foundation. That part holds. It is not a figure of speech but a real philosophical claim that has grown flesh inside a novel.

Two · Sense of possibility and essayism: the form is the subject

The title of chapter four says, in substance, that if there is a sense of reality there must also be a sense of possibility (Möglichkeitssinn). A man with a sense of reality asks how things are; a man with a sense of possibility asks how they might equally well have been, and sees every accomplished fact as one option that happened to win a lottery — which is exactly why Kakania is funny, since it treats a pile of pure accidents as the order of nature. But Musil settles the account honestly: a man of possibility cannot act, because acting means cutting away every possibility but one.

The way of life he derives from this he calls essayism (Essayismus): an essay claims no universal truth, it holds only from its own angle, and the next one may overturn it — a hypothetical life. He then built the novel on the same principle: more than a hundred short chapters, many of them genuine essays, each advancing a proposition that the following chapter frequently knocks back with another one just as reasonable. This is the book's boldest move and the reason it was never going to end — an ending would mean one of the hypotheses winning, and the whole point of the method is to let none of them win. So "unfinished" is not entirely the stroke's doing; the thousands of pages of variants are this structure's medical record.

Three · The Other Condition and "precision and soul": the only exit, and the one load-bearing wall that fails

The book floats a half-joking proposal: a General Secretariat for Precision and Soul. Musil's lifelong problem is compressed into that joke, and so is his own CV — an engineer who built instruments and a man who believed the soul has real content. The modern world has separated the two: the precise half handles facts and may not discuss meaning, the soulful half handles meaning and may not be asked for evidence. The result is that a society can combine an extremely high level of technique with an extremely low capacity for understanding itself. The Other Condition the siblings pursue is an attempt to close that seam, and Musil takes it seriously; he read widely in the literature of mystical experience and regarded those accounts as testimony rather than lyricism.

But this should be marked honestly: it is the one load-bearing wall in the book that does not hold. The conditions it requires are extraordinarily special — two people with private incomes, no need to work, no children, nobody depending on them, and who happen to be siblings, so that the other person is very nearly oneself. It is not a route anyone could generalise, and Musil himself never managed to send the two of them back into the world: in some drafts they cross the line, in others they separate, in others the war cancels everything. The number of times an author rewrites the same passage is a reading of how much weight it can take.

What it forces you to think about

Question one
Keeping your possibilities permanently unspent: honesty, or evasion?

Take Ulrich's side at full strength first. Every identity he refuses — officer, engineer, celebrity, husband — would require him to treat a set of ready-made attributes as his own and to talk thereafter in their voice. He knows that is false: the same man in another country, another decade, grows an entirely different "character". In a world whose contingency you have already seen through, solemnly becoming something is a form of lying. Arnheim supplies his strongest evidence: the man who has made a brand of his own soul is the highest form of having qualities, and he uses that soul to talk about oil.

Now take the other side, harder than Ulrich's admirers ever put it. A possibility that is never spent costs nothing and is therefore worth nothing. He can afford this year because he has an income he does not work for, a small château with a garden, no children and nobody depending on him — somebody else has paid for his lucidity. The timing matters more. He spends the year confirming that there is nothing he wants to be, while in the same city Hans Sepp's circle is organising a crude, bloody, wholly unreserved answer into a movement. The trouble with the sense of possibility is not that it is wrong but that it is slow — in an age where somebody is willing to supply an answer immediately, whoever declines to answer vacates the field. The book was written around 1930, and its author knew how that arithmetic comes out.

Today the question is simply a life situation: the person at thirty-five still saying there are other things they could do, the person who keeps every choice filed under "later". The comfort is real, and Ulrich is its most distinguished version. The one difference is that he set a deadline — one year. Most people don't.

Question two
Is "partly responsible" a true statement or an administrative one — can a man be partly insane?

The case for the binary is much stronger than it usually sounds. Responsibility is not a measurable natural fact; it is an institution. We need a line, even if where it falls is somewhat arbitrary, because the moment responsibility is conceded to be continuous, the decision migrates from the court to the experts — from then on a defendant's life turns on which of two contradictory assessments is better written. The binary is crude, but it keeps judgement in a public, accountable place; a continuum lets everybody carry a fraction of the responsibility, which ends with nobody carrying any.

The case for the continuum is just as hard. Everything we know about brains, trauma, addiction and psychosis points one way: capacity for responsibility comes in degrees, and forcing it into two boxes is not prudence but administrative convenience. Part of what Moosbrugger did was genuinely outside his control and part of it he chose; the law demands a yes or a no, so it must lie about one half. And a system that lies in order to remain operable always lies in the same direction — against the people who cannot draft an appeal.

Where Musil stands is visible but never stated: he lets the father fight over the wording for half a lifetime and die without settling it, lets the court spin on a single word, and lets Moosbrugger demand to be treated as a man who answers in full — the only person in the novel who unambiguously says "I am guilty" is the man who killed someone. The contemporary version is not abstract at all: risk-scoring algorithms, "he wasn't well at the time", layer upon layer of corporate delegation with nobody answerable. Every time we hand an unbearable judgement over to a phrasing, Moosbrugger's courtroom opens again.

Question three
Does a society need one great unifying idea?

For: a community that cannot say what it exists for cannot act — it cannot allocate sacrifice, cannot establish what is worth enduring, cannot explain why these rules should be kept. Kakania's trouble is not too many ideas but that it has stopped believing its own state has a reason to exist; a polity that cannot settle on a slogan for a jubilee did in fact cease to exist a few years later, and that is not a coincidence. Meaning that is not shared is merely taste, and only shared meaning gets people to do long, unrewarded things for the sake of tomorrow.

Against, put as strongly as it goes, which is uncomfortable: the ideas with the power to unify a society are all of the dangerous kind. The Campaign searched for a year and failed, and what it was looking for was duly found by somebody else: Hans Sepp's blood-and-soil formula has unifying power because it needs no argument, and the mobilisation order of August 1914 has unifying power because it needs no discussion. The more a society craves one great unifying idea, the more readily it accepts the first one handed to it. Musil spent a thousand pages putting both in the same drawing room — respectable people who cannot find an idea, young men who already have one — and history settled which was more efficient.

The everyday version: the company mission statement, the team's "what are we actually doing here". "We need a vision" is usually said not because a vision is missing, but because nobody knows which thing to do tomorrow.

Question four
Two people withdrawing from the world together: an exit, or a desertion?

The siblings' side is strong, and theirs is the only part of the book with any happiness in it. What they find is real: in that condition a person is, for once, not calculating, not performing, not fobbing others off with ready-made attributes; it is the only place in the novel where precision and soul actually close over each other. Nor are they hypocrites: they convene no committees, publish no views on the age, pretend to be saving nothing. Measured against the whole of Vienna, these two are honest.

The other side has to be named plainly. This is a secession for two, and the conditions for it are paid by other people. Private means, no work, no children, nobody dependent on them, and the other party conveniently a blood relative — the route requires you first to have someone who is very nearly yourself. It looks like the limit case of love; structurally it is closer to narcissism with a partner. And there is the will: the first step into the Millennium is using a forged document to cut an irritating man out of an inheritance. People who withdraw from the world spiritually have usually arranged it materially first. The verdict is finally delivered by the form — Musil worked on this for a decade and never managed either to return these two to the human world or to let them stay where they were.

This one is close to home as well: the couple who opt out of the competition, the two people who move to the mountains, anyone who uses one relationship to keep the world outside the door. The relief is real and so is the cost — a world that only two people can maintain, and an understanding that depends on shared blood, both break as soon as a third person walks in.

The motif: what Musil kept writing

Line his work up and the same obsession appears throughout: what happens when a man trained in precision collides with something that cannot be made precise.

In The Confusions of Young Master Törless (1906), a boarding-school boy watches his classmates torment another boy and discovers for the first time that the world has two layers: the daylight one that follows reason, and another with no name and no language. What shakes him most is a mathematics lesson on imaginary numbers — a number that plainly does not exist, yet you calculate with it and arrive at the right answer. That thin early book already contains every component of the enormous late one. The three stories of Three Women (1924) stage the same structure three times: a methodical man meets something he cannot explain, his entire apparatus fails, and what he gains is worth more than what he loses. His play The Enthusiasts (1921) is about people who cannot be accommodated by any available form; his essay "Helpless Europe" (1922) says it outright: we have acquired the technique for handling everything and lost the ability to say what is good.

Criticism and controversy

Without flattery: the strongest criticisms of this book are all true.

  • It has no plot, and volume two has almost no narrative. Volume one is carried by satirical momentum; once the siblings' conversation begins, events essentially stop. The commonest reader experience is not incomprehension but not knowing where one is reading towards.
  • The Other Condition is criticised as an evasion. A thousand pages establish that every language in this society has failed, and the exit offered is a mystical state that cannot be spoken, cannot be generalised and cannot be realised by anyone who has a job — with the ambiguity between a brother and a sister at its centre. The defence is that this is precisely Musil's honesty: he refused to invent a usable answer.
  • The women. The three principal female characters each occupy an old type: Diotima is the "soul woman", rendered ridiculous; Bonadea is sexual appetite incarnate, her name an irony; Clarisse is the madwoman. Agathe has real interiority, but structurally that interiority mostly serves her brother's experiment. The defence notes that the male characters are vehicles for ideas too — the argument has never been settled.
  • The murdered woman is barely a person. In the novel Moosbrugger's crime functions mainly as an intellectual occasion, used to discuss responsibility, reason and the unconscious of an age, while the dead woman gets almost no space and no biography. Turning the killing of a woman into philosophical material is not far from the very procedure the book itself condemns: translating people into concepts.
  • Whether you have "finished it" depends on the editor. Volumes one and two were settled by the author; everything after is posthumous. His widow assembled a volume in 1943, Frisé's 1952 edition established the standard shape, and a heavily revised edition followed in 1978, with changes to which chapters are included and in what order. There are two English translations, which do not even render the title of Part Two the same way. Strictly speaking, different readers are not discussing quite the same book.
  • The author's own record is contested. Musil served as an Austro-Hungarian officer in the First World War and was decorated; around 1916 he edited an army newspaper and wrote propaganda pieces with a nationalist colouring. Twenty years later he was a committed anti-Nazi, his work was banned in Germany, and he went into exile in 1938. Separately, at the 1935 Paris international writers' congress for the defence of culture, he spoke in defence of the individual and declined the collective-front line the left expected, disappointing many in the hall. These are verifiable facts, stated here without a verdict.

Ten sentences that stay

① The title does not say the man has no character. It says character is public property, ready-made and interchangeable, and belongs to nobody.

② The first two pages demonstrate the whole book: one entirely correct weather report, and one injured man digested by a correct explanation.

③ The Parallel Campaign met for a year and never found the Great Austrian Idea — while the people who had found it were in another room, saying blood.

④ The father spent half his life arguing over whether a man can be partly insane, and died unsatisfied; hundreds of pages later that clause decides a life.
