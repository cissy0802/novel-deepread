Novels, Deep-Read · Mikhail Bulgakov · No.30

The Master and Margarita

Master i Margarita · written 1928–1940 · censored publication 1966–67 · full text 1973

中文 →

One evening in May, Moscow is unseasonably hot. There is nobody at all on the avenue by Patriarch's Ponds. Two literary men sit on a bench drinking lukewarm apricot soda and discussing a piece of business: how to prove that Jesus never existed.
Then a man comes up the avenue, improbably tall, one eye black and one eye green. He sits down politely at the end of the bench and asks a question: if there is no God, then who is arranging a man's life?
Forty minutes later, one of the two has his head cut off by a tram.

This page contains complete spoilers — how Berlioz dies, how the manuscript the Master burned comes back, whom Margarita spends her one wish on at Satan's ball, and what Pilate is finally given after two thousand years on his stone platform. If you want to keep the suspense, read the novel first; this page will wait.

In one sentence

The devil arrives in Moscow in the 1930s with a small retinue and, over four days, enters the city's greed, informing and hypocrisy into the public record; a writer who has been destroyed by a review campaign, has burned his own novel and committed himself to an asylum (he will answer only to "the Master"), and a married woman who becomes a witch and hostesses Satan's ball to get him back, are granted an ending that does not belong to this world — and the novel the Master burned is about Pontius Pilate, a governor who signed a death warrant he knew was unjust and has not slept for two thousand years because of ten seconds of cowardice.

Coordinates

Mikhail Bulgakov (1891–1940) was born in Kyiv, the son of a professor at the Kyiv Theological Academy — which matters: when he writes Jerusalem he is not improvising from an encyclopedia. He trained as a doctor, served as a country physician, and turned to writing in his twenties. Through the 1920s he lived on feuilletons — the short, sharp satirical newspaper columns of the period — wrote the novellas The Heart of a Dog and The Fatal Eggs, and got into the Moscow Art Theatre on the strength of his plays.

Then everything was switched off. By 1929 his plays had been pulled from every stage and his prose was unpublishable. In March 1930 he burned the first draft of this novel and wrote to the Soviet government asking either to be let out of the country or given work. Stalin telephoned him personally; he got a job at the theatre, and never left the country. He worked on this book from 1928 until his death in 1940, dictating the final revisions to his wife Elena when he had gone blind.

Its place in literary history is unusual: a finished novel that lay in a drawer for twenty-six years. A censored version appeared in the magazine Moskva across two issues, November 1966 and January 1967; the excised passages immediately began circulating as typescript (samizdat, "self-published" underground copies). The complete Russian text was not published in the USSR until 1973. It has since become the most quoted Russian novel of its century.

The story

Act one · Patriarch's Ponds: a foreigner joins an atheist conversation

Berlioz edits a fat literary journal and chairs MASSOLIT — the writers' official body in the novel, an organization that hands out apartments, country houses and restaurant privileges. This evening he has summoned the young poet Ivan Bezdomny (the pen name means "Homeless") to discuss a commission. Ivan has written a long anti-religious poem and Berlioz is not satisfied: the Jesus in it came out too alive, like a real man. The brief was not "attack him." The brief was "prove he never existed."

He is explaining how the saviours of every mythology were invented when a stranger sits down beside them. The man says he is a foreign specialist, invited to Moscow to examine some newly discovered ancient manuscripts — the devil's official occupation in this book is manuscript expert, and the joke is not paid off until the last act. He asks an impolite question: if there is no God, who is arranging a man's life? Man himself, says Berlioz. Then, says the stranger, consider that a man cannot even guarantee what he will do this evening. You, for example — today your head will be cut off, and it will be done by a young woman of the Komsomol, because seed ①

"Annushka has already spilled the oil."— Woland at Patriarch's Ponds (quoted, Part One, ch. 1)

They decide he is insane. Then he says a third thing: Jesus did exist, no proof is required, because — he lowers his voice — he was personally present. And he begins to tell a story, starting with Pontius Pilate. When the story is over Berlioz runs off to telephone about a suspicious foreigner; running for the tram rails, his foot goes out from under him — sunflower oil is spread across the turn, spilled a few minutes earlier by a woman named Annushka who broke her bottle there. A tram driven by a young woman in a red kerchief goes over him. payoff ① ↑Notice who carries out the sentence: fate in this book is never abstract. It is always executed by a specific, unimportant person who is preoccupied with something else. Ivan chases the gang across Moscow — the tall foreigner, a lanky creature in a cracked pince-nez, an enormous black cat that walks like a man — and ends up bursting into the writers' club restaurant in his underwear, from which he is taken to a psychiatric clinic.

Act two · Jerusalem: Pontius Pilate's ten seconds

At dawn on the fourteenth of the month of Nisan, in a white cloak with a blood-red lining, the procurator of Judea, Pontius Pilate, hears a case in the colonnade of Herod's palace. He has a migraine, cannot bear the smell of rose oil, hates the city and hates the job. The prisoner is Yeshua Ha-Nozri, a wandering philosopher of about twenty-seven, charged with inciting the destruction of the temple. The interrogation goes off the rails almost at once: Yeshua sees the headache, says a few sentences, and the headache lifts. He calls everyone "good man," including the centurion who has just flogged him. He says there are no evil people in the world, only unhappy ones. He says he has no disciples, only a former tax collector, Matthew Levi, who follows him writing on a goatskin — and that nothing written there is anything he ever said.

Pilate is on the point of letting him go. What ruins it is one line in the file: Yeshua told a young man named Judas of Kerioth that all power is violence over people, and that a time will come when there will be no power of the Caesars at all. Judas had invited him home, steered the conversation precisely there, and hidden witnesses in the room. Once that is on paper the case is no longer a religious squabble; it is treason against Tiberius. Pilate tries one more thing — the Passover custom permits the release of one condemned man — and works on the high priest Caiaphas for a long time in the sun, but Caiaphas will have Bar-Rabban, the genuine killer, released instead. So Pilate mounts the platform and reads the sentence. He was not deceived, and he was not confused. He did the arithmetic and then signed: the deposition existed, and whoever spoke for such a man would lose his post and his life.

On Bald Mountain three men hang most of the day. Matthew Levi arrives too late and can only sit on a rock cursing a God who will not let a good man die quickly; when the storm breaks he takes the body down and carries it away. That night Pilate summons Aphranius, head of his secret service, and says: I have heard that Judas will be killed tonight, I am extremely worried, please protect him. Aphranius listens with his head down and asks nothing further. It is the coldest passage on power in the book — not a word of an order is given, and the thing is done cleanly. A woman named Niza lures Judas into the garden of Gethsemane; the thirty coins are thrown back into the high priest's courtyard.

Then Pilate has a dream. He is walking up a road made of moonlight beside the wandering philosopher, the two of them arguing all the way and neither convincing the other — and the execution never took place, which is the important sentence in the dream. seed ②He wakes and knows that it did. He strokes his dog, Banga — the only thing this man still loves.

Act three · A city audited in public

Back in Moscow. Woland's party move into apartment No. 50 at 302-bis Sadovaya Street — "the bad apartment," where over the past two years tenants have been vanishing one after another without explanation. (The novel never spells it out; the reader knows what decade this is.) The retinue is now complete: Koroviev, lanky, checked jacket, cracked pince-nez, able to talk anyone into anything; Behemoth, a black cat the size of a hog who walks on his hind legs, drinks vodka and pays his fare on the tram; Azazello, short, red-haired, fanged, who does the physical work; and Hella, a red-haired vampire.

Everything they do obeys one rule, and understanding it is understanding the book's satire: they never tempt anyone into anything. They simply leave a person standing for a moment in front of what he already wanted. The director of the Variety wakes from a hangover and is on a beach in Yalta seconds later. The chairman of the tenants' association takes a wad of bribe money and pushes it into the ventilation shaft; a few minutes later the men who come to search pull foreign currency out of the same shaft. The most famous set-piece is the black magic séance at the Variety Theatre: banknotes come down from the ceiling and the hall fights for them; a Paris fashion boutique opens on stage and the women queue to exchange their old clothes; the master of ceremonies demands a public exposure of the trick, and Behemoth twists his head off. The audience laughs first, then somebody shouts to put the head back for God's sake. From his box Woland delivers a verdict: they are the same as ever, ordinary people, only the housing problem has spoiled them. Afterwards the money turns to worthless paper, the new clothes vanish, and several hundred women are standing in the street at midnight in their underwear.

Take it apart and the devil has done nothing wrong at all: they grabbed the money themselves, they changed their own clothes, the audience asked for the head to be replaced. Woland is not a tempter; he is an auditor. The only thing he does in Moscow is turn up the brightness on what the city already contains.

Act four · The Master: how a man is killed by a review campaign

Room 118 of the clinic, at night. The balcony door is pushed open from outside and a man of about thirty-eight comes in. He is here voluntarily. Ivan asks who he is and he says: I am a master. He will not give a name; he says he no longer has one.

He used to be a historian and reads five languages. One day he found a state bond in a basket of dirty laundry and won a hundred thousand roubles. He resigned, rented two basement rooms in a lane off the Arbat — the windows show only the feet of passers-by, and there are lilacs and a lime tree in the yard — and began a novel about Pontius Pilate. Halfway through it he met a woman in the street carrying a bunch of what he calls repulsive yellow flowers, walking entirely alone; what stopped him, he says, was not the flowers but the loneliness in her eyes. This is Margarita: thirty, childless, married to a capable young man who treats her well, living in a large detached house, wanting for nothing. She said later that she had gone out with those flowers hoping something would happen, and that otherwise she would have drowned herself.

After that she came to the basement every day, cooked, and read every page as it was written; it is she who called him the Master. The novel was finished. Then the other face of the world turned toward them. No journal would print it; a fragment was published grudgingly, and the reviews began. The reviews did not discuss whether the book was any good. They discussed an author attempting to smuggle "Pilatism" into Soviet literature, and their headlines were each more regular than the last. One of the signatures belonged to a critic named Latunsky.

And then the part the Master had not anticipated: he fell ill. Not arrested, not starved — frightened. He began to be afraid of the dark and of people, and to feel an octopus reaching out of the wall. On a bitterly cold night he fed the manuscript into the stove a handful of pages at a time. seed ③Margarita arrived and pulled the last charred sheaf out of the fire, and left saying she would come back tomorrow and tell her husband everything. He was gone from the basement that night — with one small event in between: the neighbour Mogarych denounced him for keeping illegal literature, wanting the two rooms. He went into the clinic and never contacted Margarita again, on the grounds that he had nothing left to give her. Ivan listens to all this and says: I am never going to write poetry again. seed ④The Master calls him his pupil and asks him to write the continuation one day.

Act five · Margarita: from a jar of cream to a ball

The Master has been missing for six months. One spring afternoon Margarita is sitting on a bench under the Kremlin wall when a short red-haired man sits down beside her, recites a passage from the Pilate novel, hands her a gold box and asks her to rub the cream over her whole body at half past eight tonight. She does: thirty-year-old skin goes back to twenty, and her body loses its weight. She takes a broom, goes out of the window, and flies over the roofs of Moscow, invisible and naked. Her first errand is personal: she gets into Latunsky's flat and destroys it completely, then works down the building smashing windows — until she sees a boy of four or five standing beside his bed, woken by the noise. She stops, soothes him while remaining invisible, and puts him back to sleep. That boy is a valve Bulgakov installed on purpose: she may wreck a critic's home, but she may not frighten a child. It is the entire basis on which she will shortly be trusted with Satan's ball.

Koroviev explains that Woland is giving his annual spring ball of the full moon tonight and needs a hostess, and that by the rules the hostess must be named Margarita and must be of royal blood — they have searched the whole city and she is the only one who qualifies. She agrees and asks for nothing. The ball is held in apartment No. 50, which has been stretched into a palace. Naked, wearing a heavy diamond chain, she stands at the head of the stairs for hours receiving the guests as they come up out of the fireplace: poisoners, procurers, executioners and coiners of every century, growing flesh out of the ashes and kissing her knee. The knee swells; her arm will not lift; Koroviev keeps repeating in her ear: treat them all alike, no one better and no one worse.

One young woman in the line is called Frieda. seed ⑤She was a waitress in a café, bore a child, took him into the woods, stopped his mouth with a handkerchief and buried him. For thirty years that handkerchief has appeared in her room every morning — blue-hemmed, clean, folded. She has burned it and thrown it in the river, and the next morning it is on the bedside table. She kneels and clutches at Margarita's dress; Margarita, already being pulled away, has time only to say: I give you hope.

At the end of the ball Woland is brought a cup made from a human skull — Berlioz's — and a baron who has come as an informer is shot dead by Azazello, his blood running into the cup, which Margarita drinks. Then everything disappears and the room is an ordinary bedroom. Woland's people sit around mending clothes, playing chess and drinking, and no one mentions payment. Margarita realises she is going to get nothing, and rises to say goodbye — she does not intend to ask for anything, because she remembers the rule:

"Never ask for anything, above all from those who are stronger than you. They will offer it themselves."— Woland to Margarita (quoted, Part Two)

Precisely because she asks for nothing, Woland grants her a wish. She uses it. She does not use it on the Master. She asks that they stop bringing Frieda the handkerchief.payoff ⑤ ↑This is exactly the rule Koroviev drilled into her on the staircase: no guest is to be treated better than another. She breaks it in front of the devil, and breaks it with the only chance she has. Woland is not angry; he remarks that mercy has a way of creeping in through the cracks, sends Azazello to deal with Frieda, and says: now let us talk about your own business.

She says: I want my lover, the Master, back, right now. He is brought in unshaven, in hospital clothes, still frightened. Margarita asks for the basement back and the novel back. But the novel was burned. Woland says to Behemoth: give it here. The cat pulls a thick manuscript out from under a cushion — the burned book, not a page missing.

"Manuscripts don't burn."— Woland (quoted, Part Two)payoff ③ ↑

This is the most quoted line in the book, and not because it is consoling. It is a direct reply to the stove in act four: the reader has just knelt at a fire with a man while he burned ten years of his life, and two hundred pages later a cat pulls it back out from under the sofa intact. Something destroyed, returned exactly as it was — in a novel about disappearance, it is the single reversal.

Act six · The two lines close: peace, and the road of moonlight

They are back in the basement. But it cannot end there. On a rooftop, Matthew Levi comes to Woland with a message: he has read the Master's work and asks you to take him with you and give him peace. Woland first asks his famous question — what would your good do if evil did not exist, and what would the earth look like if shadows disappeared from it? Levi will not take the bait; he finishes the message:

"He has not earned light; he has earned peace."— the message relayed by Matthew Levi (quoted, Part Two)

Azazello brings a bottle of wine down to the basement. The two of them drink and fall dead; in the same minute Margarita collapses in her house on the other side of the city, and the patient in room 118 of the clinic stops breathing. Their Moscow account is settled by Moscow's own rules: two bodies, two records. Then Azazello calls them up again, the basement burns, and they ride out of the city. On the way the retinue take off their make-up: the clownish Koroviev becomes a sombre knight in dark violet, who once made an unsuccessful joke about light and darkness and has been paying for it in motley for several centuries — tonight the debt comes due. The funniest characters in the book turn out to have been serving sentences all along.

They stop on a mountain top. In the moonlight there is a stone platform, a man in a white cloak in an armchair, and a sharp-eared dog beside him. Woland says: he has been sitting here about two thousand years and cannot sleep; at every full moon he suffers. He once had a chance to speak and did not take it. And Woland says to the Master: you have written this man all your life — now end your novel with one sentence. The Master shouts into the abyss: You are free! He is waiting for you!

The platform splits, a road of moonlight runs from the summit up to the moon, the dog goes up it first, and the man in the white cloak runs after a young man already walking ahead of him, arguing about something as he goes. payoff ② ↑This is Pilate's dream from act two, exactly: up the moonlight, arguing all the way, and the execution never happened. What the novel gives him is not a pardon. It is his own dream, made good once. The only thing the man wanted across two thousand years was to finish the conversation he cut off that day.

The Master and Margarita are given an eternal house: a Venetian window, vines climbing to the roof, candles, a quill. He will never need to write again, which is both the reward and the sentence — peace means your work is over.

Epilogue. The official finding is mass hypnosis. Ivan becomes a professor at the Institute of History and Philosophy and writes no more poetry. payoff ④ ↑The line he spoke in that hospital room is honoured by a whole epilogue: the man once commissioned to prove that Jesus never existed spends his life on history instead. He is the only Muscovite in the book who is actually changed. But every spring at the full moon he goes and sits by Patriarch's Ponds, and has the same dream — a road of moonlight, a man in a white cloak and a young man, a moment in which everything is explained. He wakes weeping; his wife gives him an injection; he quiets down and sleeps until the next full moon.

Put the six acts together and the fuse of this book is not in the plot. It is in that conversation on page one: a literary official briefing a young poet — not to attack Jesus, but to prove he never existed. Everything afterwards is the answer. The man who claims the authority to delete a thing from existence has his head taken off within the hour; a manuscript burned to ash comes back complete; the man executed two thousand years ago is still waiting on the moon road to finish his sentence; and the devil's stated profession is manuscript expert. Deletion is the one technology in this book that does not work.

Three slow-motion replays

1 · The sunflower oil: suspense converted into a countdown

The standard move is to let the reader be surprised alongside the character. Bulgakov inverts it — in chapter one he hands over the manner of death, the instrument and the sex of the executioner, withholding one variable only: where the oil is. The reader is no longer Berlioz's companion but Woland's accomplice, watching a man hold forth against an ending that is already written. The more eloquent he is, the worse you feel.

2 · The head in the theatre: satire does not invent, it registers

What makes this scene work is that the tone never once changes: the head torn off, the head put back, the rain of money, the boutique — all of it reported in the voice of a write-up of an ordinary evening's entertainment. The horror does not come from the description. It comes from the genre — a talking severed head inside a light theatre review.

Harder still is the moral shape of the scene. The hall laughs, then grabs, then somebody calls for mercy. Woland's verdict is not "they are rotten" but "they are just as they were, only the housing problem has spoiled them." It is the most precise diagnosis of the decade in the whole book, and it is delivered in a forgiving tone: these are not demons, they are ordinary people shaped by shortage, by fear, and by a room that somebody else allocates. Once satire concedes that, it stops looking down and starts confessing.

3 · Frieda's handkerchief: a hundred pages of the grotesque compressed into one request

The ball goes on at enormous length: blood, severed heads, nakedness, a swelling knee, the dead growing flesh out of ashes. By the second half the reader's senses are close to numb. That numbness is what Bulgakov is after — he is clearing space for one quiet sentence. The engineering is explicit: the size of the contrast sets the size of the effect. The same request on an ordinary afternoon is merely kind; placed after she has drunk a man's blood and knelt half the night on a swollen knee, it is the hinge of the book. And note the order: the author has her lose her only chance first, and only then does Woland offer a second. If she had expected compensation, the gesture would be worth nothing.

The book's central device: a novel narrated by other people

The technical core of The Master and Margarita is not the magic; it is the way the inner novel is suspended. Pilate's story runs to four chapters, and not one of them is told by the novel's own narrator: the first is narrated aloud by Woland, a self-declared eyewitness; the execution chapter is Ivan's dream in the asylum; the last two are Margarita reading the resurrected manuscript.

This solves a problem that looks unsolvable: how do you write a novel about Jesus, in a country where you may not write about Jesus, without producing either a sermon or a monograph? You turn it into physical evidence. It is not the author's opinion; it is a document circulating between characters — written, burned, part-rescued, restored, read aloud, and finally given its last sentence by its author on a mountain top.

The two lines are also deliberately split at the level of prose. Moscow is written in feuilleton voice: short sentences, exclamation marks, street slang, a narrator who keeps butting in. Jerusalem is as cold as an archaeological report — exact hours, wind direction, the temperature of the stone, almost no metaphor, and across four chapters not a single joke. The absurd works in Moscow and stops working the moment you cross into Jerusalem, which is itself the book's strongest argument: the thing that actually happened is beyond the reach of any satire. The seams are beautiful too. On the charred page Margarita pulls from the fire there is a sentence about darkness coming in from the Mediterranean and covering the city the procurator hated; the reader meets that half-sentence in her hands, and later it opens a complete chapter — which is "manuscripts don't burn" demonstrated in form rather than asserted in dialogue.

Finally, Woland himself. He tempts nobody in this book and collects not one soul. Every person he punishes has walked into it; everything he wrecks was already rotten; and on the matter outside his authority — granting the Master "light" — he says plainly that it is not his department. The novel's epigraph is the devil's self-introduction from Goethe's Faust: "I am part of that power which eternally wills evil and eternally works good." The devil here is an instrument. The subject was never a contest between good and evil; the subject is that some things cannot be deleted.

What it makes you think about

Question 1
Is cowardice really the worst of the vices — when the price is your family?

Take Pilate's side first, and put it harder than his sympathisers do. He governs a powder keg at Passover with the city full of pilgrims. In the file, in writing, is a statement of treason against the emperor, with an informer and witnesses attached. The consequence of releasing this man is not criticism; it is Pilate recalled to Rome, his household destroyed with him, and Judea handed to a governor with fewer scruples. He did try. He did what any competent administrator does — minimise the damage inside the constraints he actually has. What you are asking of him is not courage; it is buying his own moral comfort with other people's lives.

Now the other side. Cowardice is the worst of the vices precisely because it has a sufficient reason every single time. Greed, cruelty and envy have to manufacture an excuse. Cowardice never does — its excuse is true, and is usually the correct cost calculation. Which makes it the one vice that can be used an unlimited number of times without the user's self-image dropping. The novel's verdict is not "Pilate got a man killed"; it is that he does not accept the excuse himself. In two thousand years no one has accused him. He is the one who cannot sleep. You can get a verdict out of every court there is and still lose to the part of yourself that wakes at the full moon.

One more turn of the knife: Bulgakov had standing to write this, because he had sat at the same desk. He wrote to the government; he took Stalin's telephone call; in 1939 he wrote Batum, a play about Stalin's youth, which was stopped before it opened. The man who pushed "cowardice is the most terrible vice" to its limit was not a man who had never bowed his head. He was a man doing that arithmetic daily. And everyone has been Pilate at small scale: the sentence not said in the meeting, the document signed without believing it. The book does not offer you "the system made me" as an exit. It just takes your ten seconds of silence and turns them into two thousand years of insomnia.

Question 2
"Manuscripts don't burn" — a fact, or something a survivor says?

Take the case for it first; it is almost eerie. The first piece of evidence is this book. The author burned the draft himself in 1930, rewrote it, worked on it until he went blind, and after his death the manuscript sat in his wife's drawer for twenty-six years, came out censored, had its cut passages circulated on typewriters, and was finally published whole and absorbed into a country's public memory. A line written inside the book was carried out by the book's own fate. And it points at something real: banning a work is frequently its most effective distribution, and power can destroy paper without destroying the fact that something was once here.

Now the case against. It is only true of the manuscripts we happen to have. Among Russian writers of the same generation there are papers that were confiscated and never seen again: Isaac Babel was arrested in 1939 and shot in 1940, and his seized manuscripts have never been recovered. Mandelstam's poems survived because his wife memorised them. They did not refuse to burn. People carried them at the cost of their lives. Treating the line as a law credits the fire-resistance of paper with work that human beings did.

And notice how Bulgakov himself handles it: he does not have the manuscript fly back out of the ashes. He has the devil pull it out from under a sofa. Inside the novel it takes a supernatural agent — which is to say, the author knew perfectly well that it does not hold in the world. He wrote it as a miracle, not as a rule. That may be its real weight: not a judgement about reality, but a thing a man said to himself at a moment when he had no reason at all for optimism. We all use the domestic version — "it won't have been for nothing." It is useful, because without it you cannot keep working. It is also worth remembering that it is a sentence spoken beside a fire, not a conclusion drawn in an archive.

Question 3
Why is Woland's kind of justice so satisfying, and what is wrong with that?

First, why it is satisfying. An outsider with absolute power arrives who cannot be bribed, does not take sides and never mistakes his man: the embezzler is stripped, the informer is shot, the liar is exposed in public, and the real victims are found, understood and taken away. His justice even has proportion — the master of ceremonies gets his head back, because the audience asked. Satire needs a fixed point, or all it can produce is despair. Woland is that point: he makes "somebody is keeping the accounts" true again in a decade when every ledger has been burned.

Now the objection, at full strength. Almost everyone in this Moscow is guilty — bribe-takers, money-grabbers, informers, apartment-snatchers — with one exception: the institution that actually takes people away at night appears mainly as a few bumbling investigators. So the book's justice arrives from outside and from above. The Muscovites do not stand up; a superior power passes through and sorts the wicked out for them. And the way the writer in the book is saved is that his work is read and approved by a supreme being — "he has read the Master's work."

Put those two together and an uncomfortable reading appears, one that has long had defenders in Russian criticism: the book's largest wish-fulfilment is exactly the only wish that generation of intellectuals was permitted — not that the system will change, but that the man at the top will read what I wrote, will understand, and will see me right. This is not an outsider's slur; it was Bulgakov's actual mode of life. He wrote to the government, he got his telephone call, and he lived on that call for ten years. You can also turn it over: because this was the only such experience he ever had, he wrote it all the way down, absurdity included — you need a devil in order to get one fair reading. And our appetite for someone to come and sort things out has not shrunk. The book lets you enjoy that fantasy thoroughly, and prints the price on the title page: the party you have invited is not a judge, he is the devil.

Recurring obsessions: what Bulgakov kept writing

Line his work up and one scene keeps recurring: a man with talent sits across a table from a man with power, and they negotiate.

In The Heart of a Dog, an eminent professor turns a stray dog into a man, and the resulting Sharikov immediately learns to write denunciations, demand a residence permit and claim living space, until the professor can only turn him back into a dog — the first collision of power and talent, and the paperwork wins. In the Molière play A Cabal of Hypocrites, a dramatist's entire fate hangs on the king's mood; the play itself was pulled from the Moscow Art Theatre after seven performances, which is its own footnote to the text. By The Master and Margarita, the two sides of the table have become Pilate and Yeshua — and this time it is the one with the power who is on trial.

The other recurring object is the house. In The White Guard, the apartment with cream-coloured blinds is the last small piece of order in a civil war. Here the house becomes three things: the basement (happiness), apartment No. 50 (terror), and finally the eternal home with the vines (peace). "The housing problem has spoiled them" is not a wisecrack; it is this writer's central politics: in a society where somewhere to live is allocated, a conscience has a market price.

And there is the doctor underneath. The interrogation of Yeshua takes place inside a migraine; the first thing to give out in the hostess of Satan's ball is a knee; the barman is told his death date down to the organ. When Bulgakov writes the supernatural, he always supplies fluids, pain and a timetable. The devil need not explain himself, but the knee has to genuinely hurt.

Criticism, honestly

  • It is a book with no final text. The author died in 1940 and the last revisions were dictated; several details do not line up, and Russian editions differ from one another. Any version you read includes an editor's choices. It is a little unfair to attack its structure on that basis — and equally overgenerous to claim every detail is deliberate design.
  • The middle sags. The long stretch around the theatre performance is a chain of separate comic turns with the cast rotating and the jokes repeating, and it advances slowly. Admirers call it feuilleton rhythm; detractors call it an author staying too long on one gag.
  • The argument about Margarita. The critique: the only woman in the book with agency spends all of it on a man and his book; her powers are a gift from the devil and are used partly to smash furniture; her reward is a house. The defence is equally strong: she is the moral summit of the novel, and it is she, not the Master, who makes the decisive choice. The argument is unresolved.
  • Theological offence. Yeshua is written as a gentle philosopher with no divinity: no resurrection, no miracles beyond one headache, a single follower, and everything that follower wrote down is wrong. Believing readers have reasonably felt this is a gospel dictated by the devil — since Woland is literally the one who begins it. Defenders reply that it is not a gospel at all but a novel written by a character. Adaptations of the book have more than once become the subject of public dispute in Russia in recent years.
  • The "good tsar" solution. Some critics hold that the book's redemptive structure descends from outside and above, closely mirroring the author's own dependence on favour from the very top — so that the sharpest protest of its era is simultaneously its deepest accommodation. It is a strong reading, and how far it holds depends on whether you take Woland for a saviour or an auditor.
  • Whether "peace" is a reward at all. The Master is explicitly judged not to have earned light. Some read that as mercy (his suffering bought him rest), some as a demotion (an eternal holiday is eternal unemployment), and some as a purgatorial middle state. The author does not explain.

Ten sentences that stay

1 · This is not a story about a devil who comes to wreck things. It is about a devil who comes to reconcile the accounts — he tempts no one; he only turns up the brightness on what the city already holds.

2 · The fuse is the brief on page one: not attack Jesus, prove he never existed. Everything afterwards is the reply.

3 · Berlioz's death is frightening because it is not sudden. The reader knows the ending forty minutes before he does, and only wonders where the oil is.

4 · Pilate was not deceived. He did the arithmetic and then signed — which is exactly why cowardice is the worst vice: it always has a sufficient reason.

5 · Nobody has accused Pilate in two thousand years. He is the one who cannot sleep at the full moon. Conscience gets redefined right there.

6 · Margarita spends her only wish on a child-killer she has never met — and in that moment proves there is still something in her the devil cannot buy.

7 · "Manuscripts don't burn" is a miracle, not a law: inside the book it takes a devil reaching under a sofa to make it true.

8 · Not one of the four Jerusalem chapters is told by the narrator. The burned novel survives by being passed from character to character.

9 · "The housing problem has spoiled them" is the book's most forgiving and most brutal diagnosis of an era: not demons, just ordinary people with a price.

10 · What stays is not the ball. It is the road of moonlight on the mountain — a man who wanted only one thing for two thousand years: to finish the sentence he cut off that day.
