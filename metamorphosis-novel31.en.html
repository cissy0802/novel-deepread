Novels, Deep-Read · Franz Kafka · No.31

The Metamorphosis

Die Verwandlung · Franz Kafka · written autumn 1912 · published 1915

中文 →

Rain is striking the metal of the window ledge. A travelling salesman in cloth samples wakes in his own bed to find his back turned into armour plate, his belly divided into stiff brown arches, the blanket sliding off the dome of it, and a crowd of pitifully thin legs waving in front of his eyes.
He tries to turn over. He can't.
Then he looks at the alarm clock on the bedside table: half past six. The five o'clock train left long ago.
His first thought is whether he can still make the seven.

This page contains complete spoilers — the apple that lodges in his back and is never taken out, the sentence his sister Grete pronounces in front of their parents, how Gregor dies, and the look the parents exchange on the tram in the last paragraph of the book. This site's conviction is that everything worth discussing lies past the ending; without the ending you can only discuss atmosphere. If you want to keep the suspense, read the novella first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

A cloth salesman who has been carrying his family's debt wakes one morning as an enormous insect nobody in the book will name; he is shut in his room for months and crawls out of that door three times, each time driven back harder than the last; and when he has stopped being "the one who can't earn for now" and become pure burden, the sister who has been feeding him all along announces in front of their parents that the thing is not her brother and has to go — he agrees, and stops breathing before dawn; that same afternoon the three survivors each write a note excusing themselves from work, take a tram into the country, and notice in the sunshine that their daughter has grown into a good-looking young woman for whom it is time to find a husband.

Coordinates

Franz Kafka (1883–1924), a German-speaking Jew from Prague, spent his days at the Workers' Accident Insurance Institute for the Kingdom of Bohemia, working on industrial-injury claims — what a worker's finger is worth once a machine has taken it off was decided by men in his office. He wrote at night. The Metamorphosis was written in November and December 1912, printed in a magazine in 1915 and as a small book later the same year.

One thing has to be cleared up first, because the myth travels further than the fact: this is one of the few works Kafka published himself, in his lifetime. The famous story — he asked his friend Max Brod to burn his papers, and Brod refused — concerns the unfinished novels, The Trial and The Castle. This one he owned, and he even fought his publisher over the cover.

Its slot in literary history is unambiguous: it is the earliest and cleanest specimen of the twentieth century turning "the absurd" from a figure of speech into a structure. Every move the later absurdists made — the unexplained premise, the clerical precision of the narration, the way the tone gets more frightening the more earnest it becomes — is already assembled in these fifty-odd pages.

The story

Act one · A door, and a man who is late

The room is a normal human room, only rather small. On the table lie his cloth samples — he sells fabric on the road. On the wall hangs a picture he cut out of an illustrated magazine not long ago and framed himself in gilt: a lady in a fur hat and fur boa, sitting upright, holding out toward the viewer a heavy fur muff into which her whole forearm has vanished.Seed ①

He lies there. He does not scream, does not look for a mirror, does not ask why. He thinks about his job: the trains, the four-o'clock alarms, the bad food eaten in transit, acquaintances who are always passing through; a boss who sits up high and has to be shouted at from below. If it weren't for his parents he'd have quit long ago — his plan was to keep at it another five or six years, clear the debt his parents owe the firm, and then walk up to that desk and say everything he has been saving up.Seed ②He considers claiming illness and drops the idea: the company doctor recognises no sick people, only shirkers in good health. The man who wrote that sentence spent his working days deciding, for an insurance institute, who was really injured and who was trying it on.

The knocking begins: his mother calls softly, his father hammers with a fist, his sister Grete asks through the door whether he isn't well. He answers, and to his own ear the words are perfectly clear — but underneath them comes an irrepressible squeaking that tears every syllable apart. That is the first cruel detail in the book: he still thinks he is speaking human.

Just after seven the firm's chief clerk arrives in person. That a salesman missing one train brings out the chief clerk is already the letterhead of a verdict. Through the door he is polite, then not, and finally insinuates: performance has been unsatisfactory lately, and there is also the small matter of the cash he has been collecting. Inside, the man of the house is wrestling with a lock — he has no hands, so he grips the key in his jaws and turns, at the cost of a brown fluid running out of his mouth. The lock gives.

The door opens, and the living room is nothing but reactions; the creature is never described. The chief clerk backs away with his hand over his mouth; the mother throws out her arms and collapses against the table, the big coffee pot goes over and coffee streams onto the carpet while she stares at it; the father makes a fist, then covers his face and weeps. Kafka never lets the reader see the insect. He only lets the reader see everyone's face. On the living-room wall hangs a photograph of Gregor from his army days: a lieutenant, hand on his sword, smiling without a care, his bearing demanding respect for the uniform.Seed ③

He has a perfectly lucid plan: stop the chief clerk. As long as the man is still in the building, everything can be salvaged. He inches forward; the clerk bolts down the stairs. So the father goes to work: the clerk's abandoned cane in one hand, a newspaper in the other, stamping his feet and hissing like a savage, driving his son back into the room. The doorway is too narrow, he wedges, one flank is scraped raw. His father shoves from behind — he flies in bleeding, the door is slammed shut with the cane, and then there is complete silence.

Act two · The family had money after all, and the father can still stand up

At dusk his sister brings a bowl of milk with white bread in it, once his favourite thing. He puts his mouth in and pulls it straight out: not only unappetising, actively repellent.Seed ④The next day she tries a different method — an old newspaper laid down and a whole spread on it: half-rotten vegetables, bones from last night's dinner in congealed white sauce, a piece of cheese he had declared inedible two days earlier. He goes straight for the cheese and the rotten vegetables and won't touch anything fresh. She sweeps the rest, and anything he has touched, into a bucket with a broom, puts a wooden lid on it and carries it out — she does not use her hands. He learns to hide too: four solid hours spent dragging a sheet onto the couch to make a curtain, so that she need not see him when she brings the food.

The gap under the door becomes his only source of information, and through it he hears the family's real accounts: when his father's business collapsed five years ago it was not to the last penny — a small capital survived, and interest has grown it a little; and not all of the money Gregor has been handing over was spent, some of it was put by. It is enough for three people to live on for a year or two.Payoff ② ↑What makes this the coldest stroke in the book is that it arrives through a closed door in a level voice. In act one, "another five or six years and I'm free" reads like an exhausted man talking himself round. Now the reader knows: the debt that made him indispensable never made him quite that indispensable. His irreplaceability was a story he needed. For the first few years he laid the money on the table to delight and gratitude; then it became routine, the delight stopped, and he went on selling his life at the delight's original price.

Then come the arrangements: the father will go back out to work; the mother, who is asthmatic, will sew fine underwear at home; seventeen-year-old Grete will take a job as a shopgirl and study shorthand and French in the evenings. Behind the door he burns with shame, because he had a different plan: on Christmas Eve he was going to announce that he was sending his sister to the conservatory to study violin, at his expense. It was a sentence he had been saving up to give as a present.Seed ⑤

His body is learning things at the same time. He finds he likes crawling on the walls and hanging from the ceiling, where a nearly happy absent-mindedness comes over him. Grete proposes clearing the furniture out so he has room; the mother objects, and her reason is both light and heavy — emptying the room means admitting he will never change back, whereas if his things stay he will find everything as it was when he returns. They do it anyway, first the chest of drawers, then the writing desk he did his schoolwork at as a boy. Listening to his life being carried out piece by piece, he suddenly cannot bear it, breaks out, races up the wall and presses his whole body flat against the glass of the picture: nobody is taking that.Payoff ① ↑

What makes the move beautiful is the choice. The thing he risks everything for is a cheap picture cut out of a magazine, connected to nobody — not his mother's photograph, not the desk, but a bit of private contraband a travelling salesman kept for himself. In act one it is a piece of wall decoration and the reader slides right past it; only when he covers it with his body does it light up: what he saves is not property, it is the last small patch of himself.

His mother turns, sees the shape on the wall and faints; Grete runs for medicine and in the confusion shouts his name — the first time since the change that anyone has addressed him, rather than the door, as Gregor. That evening the father comes home in a stiff blue uniform with gold buttons and a high collar: the outfit of a bank messenger. The old man who had not worked in five years, who walked with a stick and spent an hour over the newspaper at breakfast, is gone.Payoff ③ ↑

A uniform appears twice in this book. The first time it hangs on the living-room wall, a lieutenant in a photograph — the son's past tense. The second time it is the father's present tense, and he refuses to take it off, sleeping in it in his chair as if permanently on call. Father and son sit on a seesaw: through the five years the son earned, the father sank; the day the son stops, the father stands up.

That night the father throws apples at him — grabbing them from the fruit bowl, filling his pockets, the first ones rolling across the floor as if electrified, and then one striking home and sinking into his back. Nobody takes that apple out. It rots there and becomes a fixture of the household. The injury does remind the father that this is, after all, a member of the family: from then on the living-room door is left open for an hour or two each evening, and he lies in the dark watching the three of them under the lamp. From this point too, everything about the room is Grete's to decide — what he is fed, when the door opens, who may go in. That authority slowly turns into something else: on the question of what Gregor now is, she is the expert.Seed ⑥

Act three · A violin, and the sentence "it has to go"

The household slides. The servant girl is let go and replaced by a bony old widow who is afraid of nothing; she pushes the door open a crack and calls him, cheerfully, an old dung beetle. The only naming of the creature in the entire book comes from a charwoman who can't be bothered to be frightened, and she is plainly just talking. A room is let to three intimidatingly orderly gentlemen; to make space, Gregor's room becomes the junk store, and the ash bin and the rubbish bin are pushed in with him. He has stopped eating.

One evening the lodgers hear a violin from the direction of the kitchen and politely ask Grete to play in the living room. They had expected to be entertained; within minutes they are bored and have retreated to the window to smoke. The door to his room stands open, and as soon as the music starts he crawls out — coated in dust, dragging threads and hairs, carrying a rotting apple in his back. A question rises in him: was he an animal, that music could move him so?Payoff ④ ↑

Set that beside the bowl of milk at the start of act two and you see how exact the move is. The first evidence that he was turning into an animal was a matter of taste, and for two whole acts his body has been sliding that way. And precisely at the moment when he is most animal, and the family has finished classifying him as one, he is hit by a violin. That is not lyricism, it is an argument: it puts the line between "human" and "vermin" in front of the reader and then lets the reader discover that the line cannot be drawn.

He wants to go further: to bring his sister into his room — nobody else in this house deserves to hear her play — and tell her the thing he never said, that he had been going to send her to the conservatory.Payoff ⑤ ↑He even imagines keeping her in there and never letting her out. The sentence he had saved up for so long to give as a present surfaces one last time inside an insect, and it has a trace of possession in it.

The middle lodger sees him, points, and announces that in view of the conditions in this household he is giving notice on the spot, will pay nothing, and reserves the right to make a claim. The father sways; the mother coughs. And Grete says it. What she says is not "I can't do this any more":

"I will not say my brother's name in front of this monster… it has to go."— Grete, part three (my rendering of the German)

Her argument has two steps. Step one: this is not Gregor. Step two: if it were Gregor, he would have gone away of his own accord long ago. Step two is the harshest line in the book, because it translates "does he deserve to be alive" into "can he take a hint" — a real family member would have had the decency to disappear on the day he became a burden.

Gregor understands, and agrees. He turns and crawls back, banging into the walls; once he is inside, his sister locks the door behind him. Lying in the dark he thinks of his family with tenderness and without resentment, convinced that he must disappear — a conviction, the book says, even firmer than his sister's. The clock tower strikes three. As the window begins to whiten, his head sinks to the floor and the last faint breath goes out of his nostrils.

In the morning the charwoman finds him and calls the family in to look: it's croaked. Mr Samsa says, "Now we can thank God." The three of them cross themselves. The mother smiles a little sadly. The lodgers come in demanding breakfast and are pointed at the staircase; they go meekly.

That same day the father writes to his bank, the mother to her employer, Grete to her manager — three notes of excuse written together — and the three of them take a tram out of the city. Sunlight fills the carriage. For the first time they talk about the future: three decent jobs, all quite promising; time to move to a smaller, cheaper flat, since this one was chosen by Gregor and is too big. And as they talk, both parents notice almost at the same moment that their daughter, pale as she has been lately, has blossomed into a good-looking young woman with a fine figure. They fall silent and, without a word, agree by glance that it will soon be time to find her a husband.Payoff ⑥ ↑At the end of the ride she is the first on her feet, stretching her young body. The novella stops there.

Put the three acts together and you find there is no miracle in this book, only a ledger. The fuse is not the transformation — the transformation merely turns the ledger face up. The real fuse is the act in act one that never gets written down: Gregor stops earning. Everything after that is fallout: the clerk auditing the accounts, the father digging out a uniform, the furniture going, and at last the person closest to him striking him off the roll of humanity. And the tram at the end is a photocopy of the first page: the one the family valued as income is gone, and the person now being appraised and priced in the sunshine is the sister he had wanted to send to music school. This family does not need Gregor. It needs somebody in the position marked "the one you can lean on."

Three slow-motion replays

1 · The first sentence: the miracle in the past tense, the invoice in the present

The opening sentence disposes of the most impossible event in the book: waking one morning from uneasy dreams, he found himself changed in his bed into an enormous vermin. Not "he felt", not "it was as if", not "he dreamed" — a completed fact, with no cause, no process and no witness. The second sentence turns to the armour-hard back, the third to the blanket about to slide off, and then come the rain, the alarm clock, the train.The impossible gets one sentence; the possible gets thirty pages.

Fantasy normally spends its energy persuading you that the thing happened. Kafka deletes the persuading and uses something stronger — a narrative voice that is not remotely surprised. With no surprise in the tone, the reader has nowhere to put their own, and ends up doing sums about trains and debts instead. The absurd here is not produced by description; it is forced out by the refusal to describe.

2 · That door: an hour of lock-picking staged as a battle

The climax of act one is, in substance, a man opening a door, and Kafka gives it several pages: he hangs his whole body from the key and swings around the lock as it turns. The technical achievement is converting horror into labour — the emotion you had prepared was fear, and what you get is effort. You strain on his behalf; you exhale when the lock shifts. By the time the door is actually open, you are standing on the inside of it. And once it opens, he does not describe the insect, only what the three people outside do: he leaves the thing you most want to see empty, and lets everyone's reaction fill it in.

3 · The apple: the weapon comes out of the fruit bowl

Notice the weapon. Not a stick, not a blade — the fruit from the supper table. The whole scene is domestic: the father in his uniform, reaching into the bowl, pacing the room as he throws. The violence is never allowed to escalate into drama; it stays at the volume of an ordinary evening, which is exactly what makes it terrible. Then comes the best detail in the book: nobody removes the apple. The wound is not an event, it is a piece of furniture; the pain is not a climax, it is background noise. Harm inside a family rarely comes from the one explosion. It comes from everyone afterwards agreeing that it can stay where it is.

More precise still is the turn that follows. Because of this serious injury the father remembers that this is family after all, and starts leaving the door open in the evenings. The one who compensates him is the one who maimed him, and the compensation is a gap in a door. The warmth in this house is real, and so is its exact weight: one open door, one hour a night.

The book's central device: a metaphor that refuses to be explained, kept as a set of accounts

Literary fiction has "worldbuilding" too; it simply lives in the writing rather than in the physics. This machine has four parts, and it collapses without any of them.

One: take the metaphor literally, and never let it turn back into a metaphor. "He lives like vermin" is a dead figure of speech; nobody who says it means it. Kafka means it, in force from half past six that morning. The book's whole engine is here — a dead metaphor suddenly demanding that you pay its full real-world costs: it has to be fed, it takes up a room, it smells, it loses you your lodgers.

Two: refuse to name it. The German word is Ungeziefer — vermin, an unclean creature, and in its etymology an animal unfit to be offered in sacrifice: not even good enough for a god. The word deliberately specifies no species. When the publisher was preparing the cover in 1915, Kafka wrote specifically to insist that the insect must not be drawn — it cannot be depicted, not even glimpsed at a distance. The cover that appeared shows a man in a dressing gown, hands over his face, staggering away from an open door with darkness behind it. The author left the centre of his own book as a hole — and every later illustration or adaptation that lets you see the creature betrays it on precisely this point.

Three: weld the point of view to Gregor. The book runs on free indirect discourse — the narrator never says "I", but the judgements and calculations in the sentences are all Gregor's, so we grade this family from inside the victim's head, and that head is extraordinarily forgiving. Every coldness has to be caught by the reader unaided. Then comes the coldest stroke of all: the moment he stops breathing, the point of view lets go. For three pages the narration floats free, follows the three of them onto the tram and into the sunshine, and appraises the daughter's figure in the same level voice. The one shift of viewpoint in the whole book happens to the one person who no longer needs one.

Four: three sorties. The novella has three parts, and each part climaxes on the same action — he comes out of his room, reaches the living room, and is sent back. The cost rises each time, and so does the identity of whoever sends him back.

Under all four parts lies one more thing: the precision of a case file. The book is exact about money, hours, doors and where the furniture stands, in the manner of an accident report. The absurd here does not run on atmosphere; it runs on bookkeeping. A man who spent his days reading injury claims imported the genre wholesale: state the facts, list the consequences, pass no comment.

What it makes you think about

Question 1
Does the duty to care for someone have a limit? If it does, which step is Grete's mistake?

Take Grete's side first, and put it more strongly than her sympathisers do. Three people are left with an open-ended burden: the income is gone, the flat is too large to give up, the one paying lodger has walked out and refuses to pay, the mother is asthmatic, the father is in his sixties working as a bank messenger — and she is seventeen, on her feet in a shop all day and learning shorthand at night. For months she is the only person in the household willing to open that door, and nobody has taken a single shift from her. To demand that she care indefinitely is to demand that a seventeen-year-old spend her life buying someone else an extension — and that demand is usually made by people who will not be carrying it out. What is more, she may be telling the truth: the thing behind the door has not eaten, spoken or answered to anything for months.

Now take the other side, at full strength. There is a substitution in her sentence, and it is the same substitution every act of abandonment relies on: she does not say "we can't do this any more", she says "this isn't him". The first admits a limit; the responsibility stays with her and it hurts. The second revokes the other party's standing, and the obligation vanishes along with the pain, because obligations are owed to people and not to "it". The moment she says it, the family is visibly lighter — and that lightness is not relief, it is exemption. Her evidence, meanwhile, is circular: a real family member would have removed himself once he became a burden; he hasn't, so he isn't one. The reasoning makes "knowing when to go" the entry requirement for being human, and the height of the bar is set by people who never have to clear it.

None of this is remote: parents with advanced dementia, a partner paralysed for years, a child who will need care for life. The limits of care are real and so is the collapse of the people providing it, and nobody outside has standing to sneer. The only thing worth watching is that substitution — "I can't carry this" and "he isn't himself any more" can both be forgiven, but they are not the same sentence. In the first, the person being let go is still a person. In the second, he is expelled from the species first and abandoned afterwards. The second is popular precisely because it leaves no guilt behind.

Question 2
If someone is loved for being useful, is whatever remains once the use is gone still love?

Speak for the Samsas first, and speak fully. They are not monsters: the sister fed him for months and tried everything; the mother begged again and again to be let in and wept like a child when she was stopped; the father was in tears the day after he attacked him. They shut him away, but they did not throw him out. A love that erodes over months of horror is still love, and to demand that love not erode is to demand a thing human beings do not have. There is a less flattering layer as well: Gregor's own giving was not pure either. He chose a flat too large for the family (it is the one they mean to give up at the end). He was not only supporting them; he was buying, with money, the position of the man this family could not do without. When the payments stop, what he loses is not their love but the position he had been paying off. Seen that way, the sunlit tram at the end has a cruel fairness to it: they are better off, because they have finally been allowed to walk on their own.

The other way round, that defence has one fatal hole: if love can only be verified while it is useful, it has never been verified at all. The experimental design of this book is extremely clean — it does one thing, sets the variable "usefulness" to zero, and looks at what is left. What is left is a gap in a door, a broom nobody will replace with a hand, and the sentence "now we can thank God." Erosion is understandable; erosion that permits an afternoon in the country on the same day tells you how thin the thing was.

Turned on yourself, the question gets very concrete: if tomorrow I could no longer earn, no longer care for anyone, no longer provide anything, who am I at this table? That is not answerable by confidence, only by observation — of how the tone in a household shifts over the months after someone falls ill, loses a job, loses capacity. Kafka is not offering a conclusion. He is offering an instrument. You may dispute the reading; it is hard to argue the instrument is out of calibration.

Question 3
Is the transformation a punishment, or the only strike he was ever able to call?

The "liberation" reading is genuinely persuasive. The man hates his job and has wanted to quit for years, held in place by a debt. Then one morning his body makes the decision his will could not: no more travelling, no more clients, no more collections, no more office. He lies down; he crawls on the ceiling; for the first time he experiences something like happy distraction. On this reading the insect is not a catastrophe but an honesty — he had already been living as a thing that gets used, and his body simply filed the paperwork. And the strike works: the household discovers at once that all of them can work.

Now the rebuttal, and it has to be hard. If this is a strike, the striker never issues a single demand. His first thought after the change is the next train; his last is that he must disappear so as not to inconvenience the family. In between he lies behind a door worrying about his father's debt and his sister's prospects — he changed bodies and went on being the man who thinks of everyone else, never taking his own side for a second. So it is not liberation; it is the same obedience in a different shell. He loses the income and keeps the algorithm — "I should be less trouble for them" — and it is the algorithm that finally kills him. He is not starved to death and not beaten to death: he is talked round. The last person to agree that it has to go is Gregor.

The question has a very familiar modern shape: you are only allowed to rest once your body gives out. Plenty of people hold on until a doctor's note makes stopping legitimate. The note does deliver rest, and it also confirms something worse: your will was not enough to stop you, only your body was. Gregor's ceiling is not freedom. It is a smaller cell, with a rotting apple in it.

Question 4
The author explains nothing. Is that generosity toward the reader, or a dodge?

The "dodge" case is easy to make: a text that can support every interpretation has paid the price of none. The author supplies one astonishing image and leaves the entire job to the reader.

The other case is just as strong: refusing to explain is not saying nothing; it may be a harder kind of precision. Try adding one line of explanation. Write "he broke down under years of exploitation by his family" and the whole book shrinks to a case history; write "this is the alienation of labour under capitalism" and it shrinks to an example in an essay. Any meaning that gets stated drives out the meanings that didn't. What Kafka left is not a blank but a very precisely shaped absence — precise enough that you can fill it with any kind of suffering and every one of them fits.

Brought back to ordinary life, this turns out to be the sharpest question of the four: real suffering mostly arrives without an instruction manual either. Someone goes quietly depressed; a relationship cools; a relative stops being the person they were — and our first move is almost always to find an explanation. Once we have one it feels much better, but the person it feels better for is usually not the sufferer, it is the explainer: once there is a reason, you no longer have to keep looking at it. This book is uncomfortable because it never hands you the manual, and so it makes you keep looking.

Recurring obsessions: what Kafka kept writing

Line his work up and the same fixation keeps surfacing: an authority that will not explain itself, and a body that carries out its sentences.

In The Trial, Josef K. is charged and nobody will tell him with what; he is finally taken to a quarry by two men and killed. In The Castle, the land surveyor K. never gets into the castle and never establishes whether he was hired. Verdicts come without reasons: that is the first law of Kafka's world. The second is that the sentence is executed through the body, and the condemned tends to cooperate. In The Judgment — written in the same autumn as this story — a father tells his son "I sentence you to death by drowning", and the son goes out and does it; in In the Penal Colony the machine's function is to inscribe the sentence, stroke by stroke, into the prisoner's flesh. The father in The Metamorphosis doesn't need to speak; he has a bowl of fruit. And Gregor, in the end, consents to his own removal.

There is a finer thread as well: eating. The performer in A Hunger Artist admits on his deathbed that he could fast not out of discipline but because he never found a food he liked. Gregor is revolted by milk, prefers rot, and finally stops eating altogether; the hunger artist starves for a lifetime — two ends of one obsession. Kafka almost always begins a person's relationship with the world at the mouth.

Criticism, honestly

  • The interpretation industry. Psychoanalysis, alienated labour, assimilation, existentialism, the author's own family — every school can make its case on this text, and the upshot is that people reading the book are often reading somebody's reading of it. Its power and its suspiciousness have the same source.
  • The women. Grete is the only character with a full arc, and that arc terminates in "grown into a marriageable body", with the narration itself doing the appraising. Some readers take the discomfort as deliberate — turning the daughter from carer into the family's next asset is an exact photocopy of act one. Others point out that the mother and the charwoman are equally functions, and that no woman in the book is given an interior. Both readings have text behind them.
  • Translation. Ungeziefer refuses to specify a species; most translations decide for the reader anyway, with insect, cockroach or vermin. Every version that names a particular bug works against the author's own instruction that it must not be drawn. The same goes for the first sentence: unruhigen Träumen means uneasy dreams, and rendering it as nightmares changes the pressure — nightmares have an emotion in them, whereas an uneasy night merely means you slept badly.
  • An unbalanced structure. Many readers find part three slacker than the first two: the farcical handling of the lodgers doesn't quite mesh with the coldness before it. That is an aesthetic judgement, but it is the most frequently made complaint about the book.
  • Nabokov's reading. Vladimir Nabokov championed the story in his lectures while insisting that Gregor is a winged beetle — there are wings under that hard shell, he could have flown, he simply never found out. It is a wonderful reading, and it quietly converts the book into a story about an escape he missed, filling in the very centre the author left blank. As a brilliant marginal note it is right; as a conclusion it is too light.
  • The standard biographical misreading. The consonantal echo between Samsa and Kafka has been used endlessly to read the novella as family history. That echo was found by readers, not marked by the author; used as a key it loses the colder half of the book, which is not about one father and son but about a way of keeping accounts.

Ten sentences that stay

1 · The transformation happens in the first sentence, in the past tense; the remaining fifty pages are the invoice.

2 · His first thought on waking is not "what has happened to me" but "can I still make the next train" — that line finishes the portrait on its own.

3 · The debt that made him indispensable was sitting next to savings he never knew about. Irreplaceability was a story he needed.
