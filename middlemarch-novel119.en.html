Middlemarch

Middlemarch: A Study of Provincial Life · George Eliot · 1871–72 · eight books

中文 →

Rome, a rented apartment on the Via Sistina. A nineteen-year-old English bride sits alone in an inner room, five weeks married, crying.
She could not tell you why. Her husband left early for the Vatican library; he is unfailingly courteous to her, the way one is courteous to a visitor. The red Christmas drapery she saw hung through St Peter's that day keeps spreading across her sight like a disease of the retina.
Outside is the greatest ruin in the world, and she feels she has married into a corridor with no door at the end.

Full spoilers ahead: how all four marriages end, what Casaubon's codicil says, what the banker has been hiding for thirty years, whom Dorothea finally marries and what she gives up for it. The weight of this book sits on its last page: it is not the story of a heroine who changes the world, but of a woman who wanted to, and whose ardour soaked into a provincial town where no one could see it. The question is whether that counts as failure.

In one sentence

In a Midlands town between 1829 and 1832, a girl who wants to do something great marries a dry old clergyman she takes for a great scholar; a young doctor who wants to reform medicine marries the prettiest girl in town; a charming idler waits for an inheritance that never comes; and a pious banker is dragged back to a thirty-year-old crime by a scrap of paper someone used to wedge a brandy flask. None of the four stories can see the others, yet they hang from the same web, and everyone ends up paying for choices somebody else made.

Coordinates

George Eliot (1819–1880) was born Mary Ann Evans, the daughter of a Warwickshire land agent. She began as a translator and editor: she translated Strauss's Life of Jesus and Feuerbach's Essence of Christianity, and effectively ran the Westminster Review. She then lived openly for over twenty years with the critic George Henry Lewes, who was married and could not divorce, and was shut out of respectable society for it. She turned to fiction at thirty-seven, under a man's name.

Middlemarch is her sixth novel. It began as two separate projects, one about a provincial doctor and one called "Miss Brooke", which she stitched together; it came out in eight parts from late 1871 to late 1872. Its place in literary history is the summit of Victorian realism. Virginia Woolf, in 1919, called it "one of the few English novels written for grown-up people" (Woolf, "George Eliot").

The story

Act I · A girl who wanted to do something great

Dorothea Brooke is nineteen, orphaned, living with her sister Celia and their amiably muddled uncle Mr Brooke at Tipton Grange. She dresses plainly, thinks of giving up riding because she enjoys it too much, and wants a life that is useful and large without being able to say what that would be. The Prelude tells of Saint Theresa, the little Spanish girl who set out with her brother to seek martyrdom among the Moors, and then observes that many later Theresas were born who found no epic to fit them, foreshadowing ①so that their ardour scattered itself on things no one remembers. It reads like a general sigh. It is in fact the novel's thesis.

The only great work to hand is drawing plans for better cottages on her uncle's estate. Sir James Chettam, a neighbouring baronet, is courting her; she assumes he is courting Celia. The man she chooses is the Reverend Edward Casaubon: about forty-five, sallow, talking as if reading out footnotes, at work on a vast Key to All Mythologies that will prove every myth on earth a broken fragment of one original revelation. foreshadowing ②To Dorothea, marrying him is marrying a great cause: she will learn Greek and Latin, read to him, copy for him, as Milton's daughters read to their blind father. The rector's wife, Mrs Cadwallader, remarks that someone put a drop of his blood under a magnifying glass and found nothing but semicolons and parentheses.

She marries him, and the honeymoon is the opening scene. Casaubon politely declines every offer of help; when she asks when he will actually begin to write, he flinches as if she had touched a wound. In Rome she meets his young cousin, Will Ladislaw, whose grandmother was cut off by the family for marrying a poor Pole, and whom Casaubon supports out of a desiccated sense of duty. Will is dabbling in painting, in everything. He says, almost in passing, something that stops her: Casaubon reads no German, and the German scholars have already done his kind of work and moved beyond it. He is researching, in a windowless room, a world that has been carried away.

They return to England in January. Dorothea enters the small sitting-room Casaubon has given her, where a miniature of Will's grandmother, Aunt Julia, hangs on the wall. Outside, the avenue and the fields lie under snow, foreshadowing ③and the whole world seems to have shrunk, the furniture too, the clouds hanging low in one even whiteness. She feels that the life ahead of her is the piece of world this window frames.

Act II · A young man who wanted to reform medicine

That same winter a new doctor arrives in Middlemarch: Tertius Lydgate, twenty-seven, poor relation of a baronet, trained in London, Edinburgh and Paris. He brings a stethoscope, a refusal to make his money by selling drugs, and a scientific ambition. The French anatomist Bichat had shown the body to be made of distinct "tissues"; Lydgate wants to go one step further and find the primitive tissue they all share. The older doctors hate him on sight.

The narrator pauses to tell a story from his Paris years. He fell for an actress named Laure who played a wife who stabs her husband, opposite her real husband; one night the stage knife went in and the man died. The court called it an accident, and Lydgate followed her to Avignon to propose. She told him calmly that her foot had indeed slipped, but foreshadowing ④"I meant to do it": her husband bored her. Lydgate came to his senses and decided he was cured; from now on he would choose a wife by reason. The narrator adds that his weakness lay in his "spots of commonness": distinguished in his science, as vulgar as any man in town about furniture and women.

The banker Nicholas Bulstrode has funded a new fever hospital and needs a modern doctor to run it. He is an Evangelical, thin, always invoking Providence, respected and feared. The hospital must elect a chaplain: Bulstrode's man Tyke, or the Reverend Farebrother, whom Lydgate genuinely likes, a kindly man who supplements his stipend by winning at whist and keeps a study full of insects. Lydgate knows Farebrother is better, but he needs the hospital. He votes for Tyke. It is the first time he bends.

Rosamond Vincy, the mayor's daughter, is the prettiest girl in town. She wants Lydgate because he has titled relatives; marrying him would mean escaping Middlemarch's vulgarity. He means only to flirt. One day he calls with a message, finds her alone, and she lets her work fall; he stoops to pick it up and, looking up, sees tears in her eyes. Within minutes they are engaged. Each believes they are choosing a person. Each is choosing a life they have imagined.

Act III · Wills

Rosamond's brother Fred is the most likeable wastrel in the book. He has run up debts, and his one hope is his rich old uncle Peter Featherstone of Stone Court, whose relatives are all waiting downstairs for him to die. Fred gets the upright land agent Caleb Garth to guarantee a bill, then can't pay it; the Garths have to spend their son's apprenticeship money, and Mary Garth hands over the ninety-two pounds she had saved. Fred has loved Mary since childhood and she loves him, but she is clear: she will not marry a man who lives on expectations, nor one who goes into the Church without believing.

Mary is nursing at Stone Court. On the night he dies, old Featherstone tells her to open his iron chest, take out one of his two wills and burn it, and help herself to the money there. foreshadowing ⑤She refuses: she will not handle a dying man's will alone in the middle of the night, when anyone could later say she tampered with it. Before dawn he dies with the keys in his hand. When the will is read, Stone Court goes to an illegitimate son nobody has heard of, Joshua Rigg. Only later does it come out that the will he wanted burned was the later one, and that under the earlier one Fred would have had ten thousand pounds. Mary knows her refusal left him with nothing.

A minor figure passes through this act. Rigg has a stepfather, Raffles, a sodden old rogue, who comes to cadge money from him. As he is sent off, he picks up a stray sheet of paper and folds it into the leather case to stop his brandy flask rattling. foreshadowing ⑥He glances at the signature, a name he knows: Nicholas Bulstrode. Rigg has just sold Stone Court to the banker. The narrator moves on.

Meanwhile Casaubon has heart disease, and Lydgate tells him plainly that he might have fifteen years or might go at any moment. He is jealous of Will. On the last night of his life he asks Dorothea to promise that if he dies she will do what he wishes, and he will not say what that is. She knows: to work his notebooks into a book and give her life to them. payoff ② ↑In Act I she married him to help copy out a great book. Now, at last, she sees what the book is: shattered mummies that will never fit together, and she is being asked to spend her life sorting a tomb. The very thing she married for is the thing that threatens to bury her. She asks to give her answer in the morning. At dawn she goes out to the summer-house to tell him yes, and finds him slumped over the stone table, dead.

He never got his yes, but he left a codicil: if Dorothea marries Will Ladislaw, she forfeits everything he left her. In effect it announces to the town that the dead man suspected an affair. She had never thought she loved Will; in that moment a dead man thought it for her. She looks up at Aunt Julia's miniature on the sitting-room wall. The face is very like Will's. Alive, Casaubon could not hold her; dead, he locked her in with a piece of paper. The book is titled "The Dead Hand".

Act IV · Bills

The Lydgates' marriage is crushed one piece of furniture at a time. A good house, good silver; Lydgate never does the sums until the creditors arrive. He tries to retrench: let the house, live more simply. Rosamond neither argues nor cries; she simply, sweetly, does not comply. Behind his back she gets the agent to drop the letting. Behind his back she writes to his baronet uncle for money and is coldly refused. Pregnant, and forbidden to ride, she rides anyway with his visiting cousin, an officer; the horse is startled by trees being felled, she falls, and loses the baby. She never thinks herself in the wrong.

payoff ④ ↑The illness Lydgate thought Paris had cured has come back. Laure did what she wanted with a real knife and calmly owned it. Rosamond never owns anything; she unpicks his plans one by one with silence and a smile. He swore never again to let a woman's tears decide his fate, and his fate was decided by tears he saw while stooping to pick something up. Nor does the narrator let him off: he married her, partly, as one buys a handsome piece of furniture.

Mr Brooke, meanwhile, has decided to stand for Parliament on Reform, buys a local paper, and makes Will its editor. Will stays, half for politics, half to be near Dorothea, but he is poor and would rather die than be thought to be after her money. At the hustings someone holds up an effigy of Brooke that parrots his catchphrases, an egg hits him, and he withdraws.

Act V · The banker's old account

Raffles returns, this time to Bulstrode, and the past opens. Over thirty years ago in London, Bulstrode was clerk in a pawnbroking business that quietly fenced stolen goods. When the owner died, he married the widow, who wanted to find her runaway daughter and leave her the fortune. Bulstrode found the daughter, kept it from the mother, paid Raffles to stay quiet, and in the end took the money himself, coming to Middlemarch as a devout banker. He told himself Providence meant him to use it for greater good. The hidden daughter had married the son of a Polish exile and had a son: Will Ladislaw.

Bulstrode offers Will a quiet settlement. Will hears where the money came from and refuses it, though it leaves him too poor to speak to Dorothea.

Raffles collapses on the road and Caleb Garth brings him to Stone Court, where in his delirium he tells Caleb everything. Caleb asks no questions and gives up managing Bulstrode's land. Lydgate attends Raffles and gives strict orders: no alcohol, and opium only up to a certain dose. Lydgate had recently asked Bulstrode for a loan and been refused; that very day, the banker offers him a thousand pounds.

That night Bulstrode hands the opium to the housekeeper and "forgets" to tell her when to stop. Later she comes to ask: the patient is begging for brandy, may she give him some? Bulstrode lies awake a long time, then passes the key to the wine-cooler out through the door. By morning Raffles is dead. He did nothing. He simply stayed silent at the two moments when he should have spoken.

But before he died, Raffles had told the story to a horse-dealer at a fair in the next town. payoff ⑥ ↑The paper wedged around the brandy flask in Act III was the beginning: Raffles had no idea where the old clerk had gone until that signature told him. The book's great secret is not uncovered by a detective. It is picked up by a drunk who wanted to stop his flask rattling. Eliot hands fate to the most insignificant object in the room, which is exactly how she thinks the world works.

When the rumour breaks, the town is meeting about a new burial ground for the coming cholera. The lawyer Mr Hawley publicly calls on Bulstrode to withdraw from public life. Bulstrode turns white and cannot stand, and Lydgate instinctively gives him his arm out of the room. Everyone sees it: the doctor who took a thousand pounds, supporting the banker suspected of murder. He will never be able to explain.

payoff ⑤ ↑Before the Bulstrodes leave Middlemarch, his wife Harriet, the mayor's sister, asks one thing of him: let Fred manage Stone Court, under Caleb Garth. The land Mary refused to touch that night comes back to Fred by a long road, through a banker's ruin. Not as an inheritance, but as work he will have to earn with his own sweat. Mary's refusal cost him ten thousand pounds and gave him a life worthy of her.

Act VI · The road outside the window

Dorothea is the only person in town who does not doubt Lydgate. She lends him a thousand pounds on the spot so he can repay Bulstrode, and goes herself to tell Rosamond that her husband is innocent.

She opens the drawing-room door and sees Will there, Rosamond in tears, Will holding both her hands. Dorothea leaves her letter and goes, perfectly polite. Will turns on Rosamond in fury: the only woman he has ever loved is someone else.

Dorothea does not sleep. She lies on the cold floor of her sitting-room and admits, for the first time, that she loves Will. Near dawn she gets up and draws back the curtains. payoff ③ ↑The same window. In Act I it showed her a shrunken, frozen world. This morning she sees, on the road beyond the gate, a man with a bundle on his back and a woman carrying a baby, and in the field a shepherd with his dog. She thinks of the largeness of the world, of people waking to labour and endurance, and of herself as part of that involuntary, palpitating life; she cannot hide inside her own pain and look on as a spectator. The window that once shrank the world now puts her back into it.

She changes her dress and goes to Rosamond a second time, speaking only of how much Lydgate needs his wife's trust. For the first time, someone stronger treats Rosamond as a person who can be moved, and she does the one generous thing she does in the whole book: she tells Dorothea that Will has just told her he loves only Dorothea.

Days later Will comes to Lowick to say goodbye. A storm breaks; lightning flashes; they find themselves holding hands. He says he is poor. She says she does not want the money; her own seven hundred a year will do. She gives up Casaubon's whole estate and marries him.

In the Finale, Fred and Mary live out their lives at Stone Court and each writes a little book, his on green crops and cattle-feeding, hers on great men from Plutarch for children; the town is convinced each was written by the other. Lydgate goes to London, treats gout, prospers, and dies at fifty, having called Rosamond his basil plant: the plant that flourished so wonderfully on a murdered man's brains. payoff ④ ↑Laure killed her husband with a knife. Rosamond killed no one; she merely bloomed very well on a man's head. After his death she marries an elderly, wealthy physician.

Dorothea becomes a politician's wife, builds nothing remarkable, and no one writes her life. payoff ① ↑The Prelude's "many Theresas" comes to earth here. Her effect on those around her, the narrator says, was incalculably diffusive, for "the growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts"; that things are not so ill with you and me is half owing to those who lived faithfully a hidden life and rest "in unvisited tombs." (Finale, last sentence.) The book's last word is "tombs". Eliot gives Dorothea a grave no one visits and insists it is not a punishment.

The fuse

Looking back, the fuse was not lit in 1829 but more than thirty years earlier in London, when a pawnbroker's clerk decided not to tell a mother that her daughter had been found. That one silence made Will poor from birth, and so dependent on Casaubon, and so in Rome when Dorothea came; it gave Bulstrode the money for a hospital, which brought Lydgate, and gave him a thousand pounds to lend on the worst possible day; it put a hold in Raffles's hand, which brought him to Stone Court and that scrap of paper. The four stories look independent, but they all grow out of one concealed daughter, and almost no one in the book ever knows it. They just feel that their lives have not gone quite right.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion I · The room in Rome: a woman crying, and a narrator who turns away

The opening scene is Chapter 20. Eliot shows us the tears first and only then goes back for the cause, compressing the honeymoon into a few pages of retrospect. The reader stands where Dorothea stands: we feel something is wrong before we know what.

Then comes one of the most famous passages in the book. A young bride's tears are nothing unusual, Eliot says; if we had a keen sense of all ordinary human life it would be like hearing the grass grow and the squirrel's heart beat, and we should die of "that roar which lies on the other side of silence." Luckily, the quickest of us walk about well wadded with stupidity. The lens pulls back from one woman's tears to everyone's, then admits we could not bear to see them all: sympathy must widen, and it has limits. Nine chapters later the narrator interrupts herself mid-sentence ("but why always Dorothea?") and turns to Casaubon's fear. That dry man, too, has a heart terrified of being seen through. That turn is why the book has no villain.

Slow motion II · "Look up, Nicholas."

When Harriet Bulstrode learns everything, she shuts herself in her room for a long time. She takes off her ornaments and puts on her plainest black dress and a plain bonnet: mourning, for the respectable life she had. Then she goes to her husband, who sits with his head bowed, waiting for her verdict. She lays her hand on his shoulder and says, "Look up, Nicholas." (Chapter 74.) He weeps, and so does she. She asks nothing; he confesses nothing. He cannot say "I am innocent", and she cannot ask "Is it true?"; they accept each other in silence.

The power is in the restraint. By now the reader can barely sympathise with Bulstrode, and Eliot chooses precisely his least defensible moment to send in an ordinary, not especially clever woman with an unconditional loyalty. The change of dress says it all. She is not forgiving the sin. She is choosing to go down with the sinner.

Slow motion III · Behind the curtain, at dawn

That night stays indoors for page after page; there is no outside. Then she draws the curtains and the space suddenly opens: the road, the fields, the distant figures, a pearly light.

Other people's hardship does not make her pain smaller; it shows her that her pain is one part of a vast life, and so she can walk out of it. The first slow-motion scene says sympathy has limits; this one says sympathy is a way out. The novel's most important moral turn has no dialogue at all, only a window and a few strangers.

The core device: a web, and a narrator who keeps turning round

Middlemarch has no protagonist. Its device is the web. Eliot uses the word herself: she has so much to do unravelling certain human lots and seeing how they were woven and interwoven that all the light she can command must be concentrated on "this particular web" (Chapter 15). It works in three ways:

  • Parallel marriages, run like a controlled experiment. Dorothea and Lydgate are mirror images: one marries an imagined learning, the other an imagined ornament. Fred and Mary are the control group: they have seen each other clearly since childhood, so theirs is the one marriage that works. The Bulstrodes are a fourth case: a marriage built on a lie that becomes real for the first time once the lie is exposed.
  • The narrator is a microscope that keeps changing focus. Scientific metaphors run all through it. Introducing the meddling Mrs Cadwallader, the narrator says that under a weak lens you might think a tiny creature is actively hunting its prey; a stronger lens shows minute hairs making whirlpools that draw the victims in. Small-town matchmaking and gossip work the same way. Most famous is the pier-glass parable: hold a candle to a polished surface covered in random scratches, and the scratches seem to arrange themselves in circles round the flame. The scratches are events; the candle is anyone's ego (Chapter 27). Everyone is the centre of their own mirror, and the narrator's job is to keep moving the candle.
  • A gap in time. The story runs from 1829 to 1832: the railway is just arriving (labourers go after the surveyors with hayforks), cholera is on its way, the Reform Bill has not yet passed. Eliot was writing forty years later. The future the characters discuss with hope or dread is the one her readers already lived in. Everyone thinks they are standing at history's door; they are standing at an ordinary crossroads.

To be honest about it: this narrator is not neutral. She stops at key moments to judge, to explain, to generalise into "we". To some readers that is the soul of the book; to others it is a Victorian habit of preaching.

What it forces you to think about

I · A dying man asks you to burn his will. Do you?

Featherstone is not asking Mary to do wrong. The will is his; he is entitled to change his mind; burning it would give the man she loves ten thousand pounds. All she has to do is reach out.

The strongest case for burning it: rules exist to protect people's wishes, and this man's wish could not be clearer. He spends his last strength saying what he wants, and you refuse because you are afraid someone might suspect you later. What you are protecting is not justice but your own clean hands. Keeping your hands clean is sometimes just passing the cost to someone else.

The strongest case against: night, no witnesses, a sickbed, a box of money. This is the textbook scene of every inheritance fraud. She cannot know whether he is lucid or whether he would change his mind again tomorrow. Rules require witnesses precisely because in such moments no one can tell a real wish from sick delirium. If she reaches out, she will never be able to explain herself.

Eliot's position shows: she lets the refusal turn, in the end, into Fred's growing up. No money, so he has to learn to work. But she does not spare Mary the guilt. Has a manager ever told you quietly to skip a procedure, they'll take responsibility, and you could see that skipping it would be better for everyone?

II · Should you promise a dying spouse to finish their life's work?

Dorothea is ready to say yes. She already knows the book is worthless, but she pities the man: it is the only thing he has.

The strongest case for promising: the deepest love in a marriage sometimes means taking on the part of the other person you don't believe in. Casaubon is not wicked; he is a man afraid his life has been for nothing. A real, particular person who needs her is worthier of her devotion than any abstract greatness. Would freedom bought by refusing a dying man's last request not feel a little cold for the rest of her life?

The strongest case against: no one has the right to use their death to occupy someone else's remaining life. He won't name the terms precisely because she would refuse them. It is not a request, it is a trap, and the codicil proves he wanted control. To agree in the name of love is to live as a footnote to a dead man.

Eliot lets Casaubon die before the answer comes, which is evasion and mercy at once. Keeping on a family shop you never wanted, fulfilling a dead relative's wish you don't share: would you make that promise?

III · How many times will you bend to get the good thing done?

Lydgate first bends by voting for Tyke, sacrificing a good man to keep the hospital. Then he takes Bulstrode's thousand pounds without asking why the banker has suddenly turned generous. Each time the reasons are good; each time he slips a little lower.

The strongest case for him: no one who refuses to bend gets anything done. Without Bulstrode's money there is no hospital; without the thousand pounds he goes bankrupt. Demanding that someone working in the real world stay as clean as a saint is demanding that they never get on the field at all.

The strongest case against: corruption is never one decision; it is a chain of perfectly reasonable concessions. Every step he took was right, and together they were wrong. It was those spots of commonness that made him take the comfortable side at every turn. Blaming it all on Rosamond lets him off far too lightly.

Eliot plainly sympathises with him, but she doesn't acquit him. The first time you said at work "just this once", did you wonder what the tenth time would look like?

IV · Is a good life that nobody remembers a failure?

Dorothea's ardour went into an emergency loan, a timely visit, a few words that let someone believe in themselves again. The Finale says this is part of how the world gets better.

The strongest case that it is failure: a brilliant woman was flattened by her era, and her author stuck a label on the flattening that says "unhistoric good". Eliot herself translated, edited, defied convention and became one of the most famous writers in Europe, yet she sends the heroine most like herself back into a wife's role and tells us that is fine too. Calling constraint a virtue is how constraint survives.

The strongest case that it isn't: people in history books are a tiny part of the world; most of the good is done by the nameless. That loan and those words changed the lives of particular people, which is more real than a great unread book. Measuring a life by whether it is remembered is itself Casaubon's vanity.

Eliot leaves both voices in the book. The Finale consoles, but the Prelude grieves: those Theresas "found no epic". If your own life will never be remembered by anyone, how much of that can you accept?

Motifs: what Eliot kept writing about

Eliot returns to three things. First, the tug-of-war between egoism and sympathy. She was a fervent Evangelical as a girl and lost her faith; from Feuerbach she took the idea that our love of God is our love of each other projected onto the sky, and should be brought back down to the people beside us. Every one of her novels trains the reader to step out from the centre of their own mirror.

Second, renunciation. In The Mill on the Floss Maggie Tulliver turns back halfway through running off with the man she loves, and accepts disgrace; Dorothea giving up the estate and Mary refusing the iron chest are the same gesture. Third, the hidden past that comes back. In Silas Marner the squire's eldest son hides a secret wife and child; in Felix Holt Mrs Transome hides who her son's father is; Daniel Deronda learns only at the end that he is Jewish. Bulstrode is the darkest figure on this line.

Middlemarch is where that line is widest: for the first time she writes not one person or one family but a whole town in its connections, and admits that no one can really see the whole.

Controversy and criticism

"A treasure-house of details, but an indifferent whole." That was Henry James's verdict in his 1873 review. He admired the characters, especially Lydgate, but felt the strands were loosely joined.

Will Ladislaw is too thin. This is close to a consensus: he reads like a required correct answer rather than a person. The usual line is that Dorothea deserved a better man, or no man at all.

Dorothea's ending. Feminist critics keep asking why an author who broke every convention sends her heroine back into domesticity. Others reply that the ending is an indictment of an age that left women no road. One verifiable detail: the first edition's Finale had a sentence blaming society for Dorothea's first marriage, for smiling on a sickly man's proposal to a girl less than half his age; it was removed in the 1874 revision, and why remains unsettled.

The preaching narrator, and the harshness toward Rosamond. F. R. Leavis, in The Great Tradition (1948), placed Eliot at the heart of English fiction but faulted her partiality for Dorothea, whom he saw as an idealised version of the author herself; to readers who dislike it, the narrator's frequent pauses to comment are sermons. And then Rosamond. Almost everyone gets the narrator's sympathetic turn except her: she remains a pretty, cold plant to the last, and the basil line is merciless. Even Eliot's fairness has limits.

Crossovers

  • Social psychology · naive realism: Lee Ross's term for the default belief that we see the world as it is, so anyone who disagrees must be ignorant or biased. The pier-glass is the same shape: the scratches have no centre until a candle arranges them. The difference is that psychology treats it as a stubborn default, while Eliot thinks imagination can move the candle.
  • Sociology · Granovetter's weak ties: in 1973 Mark Granovetter showed that new information tends to reach us through acquaintances rather than close friends. Bulstrode's secret leaks exactly that way, from a drunk to a horse-dealer at a fair in the next town. The difference is that he was tracing how opportunity travels; here, what travels is ruin.
  • Philosophy · moral luck: Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel asked why our moral judgments so often hinge on outcomes the agent could not control. Had Raffles lived, the man who handed over the key was merely careless; he died, so he is a murderer. The difference is that the philosophers ask what fair judgment would be, and Middlemarch looks only at the result.
  • Buddhism · Indra's net: the Huayan school pictured a net with a jewel at every knot, each reflecting all the others, to show that everything interpenetrates. Middlemarch works the same way: touch one knot and the whole net trembles. The difference is that Indra's net has no centre and no before or after, while Eliot's web has causes, time, and a starting point thirty years back.
