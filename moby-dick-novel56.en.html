Moby-Dick

Moby-Dick; or, The Whale · Herman Melville · 1851

In one sentence

A whaling captain whose leg was bitten off by a white sperm whale uses money, ritual and sheer personal force to bind an entire crew of wage-earning men to his private grudge, hunts the animal for more than a year across half the world, corners it in the Pacific for three days, and loses thirty-odd lives, his ship and himself; the only survivor is Ishmael, the sailor whose whole function was to watch and to tell, and he stays afloat for a day and a night on a coffin that had been built for somebody else.

Coordinates

Herman Melville (1819–1891), a New Yorker from a family that had come down in the world, shipped out at twenty-one on the whaler Acushnet, jumped ship in the South Pacific and spent time in a village in the Marquesas. He turned the episode into Typee and Omoo and became famous at once — he arrived as an author of South Seas adventure, and what his readers wanted was more of it.

Moby-Dick was his sixth novel, published in London in October 1851 as The Whale in three volumes, then in New York that November under its full title, dedicated to Nathaniel Hawthorne. It takes the shape of a sea adventure and fills it with something else: dozens of chapters of cetology, soliloquies set out like a playscript, a sermon, stretches of prose poetry. His contemporaries would not have it; sales were dismal; he effectively left literary life, spent nineteen years as a customs inspector in New York, and died in obscurity. The book's standing as a classic was conferred retroactively in the 1920s, when scholars dug him out around the centenary of his birth. Its position in American literature is therefore an odd one: not a masterpiece chosen by its own age, but one returned by that age and claimed by the next.

The story

Act one · A man who wants to die, and a cannibal selling heads

The narrator calls himself Ishmael, after the son cast out into the wilderness in Genesis. He is candid about why he goes: his purse is empty, nothing on shore holds him, and every November a drizzle starts up in his soul and he begins wanting to knock people's hats off. Some men in that condition shoot themselves; he goes to sea.

"Call me Ishmael."— the novel's first sentence (original text)

He walks to New Bedford, then the whaling capital of the world, a town where every other house was paid for in whale oil. On a snowy night he squeezes into a cheap inn with one bed left, which he must share with a harpooneer who is out peddling shrunken heads. The man comes back at midnight: tattooed everywhere, face a dark purple, a genuine embalmed head in his hand. He says his prayers to a small wooden idol and climbs in with his pipe still lit.

In the morning Ishmael wakes in the arms of Queequeg, held like a bride. What follows is the warmest thing in the book: the son of a South Sea island chief who stowed away on a whaler to see how white men live, learned every Christian manner and decided not to become one; and a white man out of options who ends up burning an offering to the idol beside him.

Before sailing they visit the Whaleman's Chapel, where marble tablets are set into the wall for men lost at sea with no bodies behind them. Father Mapple climbs a pulpit built like a ship's bow, hauls the rope ladder up after himself, and preaches Jonah — what it costs to be sent and to refuse. An hour later everyone in that chapel is going off to hunt whales, and not one of them thinks he is Jonah.

At Nantucket they choose the Pequod: bulwarks trimmed with whales' teeth, a tiller carved from a whale's jaw, and a name taken from a Massachusetts tribe already exterminated.seed ①Her owners are two Quakers, one of whom prays while shaving the crew's share. Papers signed, a ragged man on the wharf who goes by Elijah stops them with vague questions: have you seen the captain? Heard about the leg he lost? They shrug it off. The ship sails on Christmas Day.

Act two · The quarter-deck: one gold coin buys thirty lives

Captain Ahab — named for the ruined king in the Book of Kings — does not appear for days. A livid whitish scar runs from his crown to his heel, and nobody knows whether he was born with it or struck by lightning; the ivory leg holds him on deck as if he had been pinned there.

One noon he calls all hands, produces an Ecuadorian gold doubloon and has the mate nail it to the mainmast: whoever first sights the white whale takes it.seed ②Then comes a ceremony — the three mates cross their lances, the three harpooneers unscrew the iron heads of their harpoons to serve as cups, the liquor goes round, and the whole ship swears an oath against one animal. A production unit of a joint-stock enterprise has just been converted into a religious order.

Only the first mate, Starbuck, does not drink. A Nantucket Quaker, cool, devout, good with numbers, he says it to the captain's face:

"Vengeance on a dumb brute!"— Starbuck on the quarter-deck (original text, ch. 36)

His reasoning is exact: the whale bit you out of blind instinct, it meant nothing by it, and to rage at a thing without intention is blasphemy; besides, we came out here for oil, and the owners' money is not yours to spend settling a private account. Ahab's answer is the intellectual core of the book — everything a man can see, he says, is a sheet of paper pasted over something:

"All visible objects, man, are but as pasteboard masks."— Ahab's reply (original text, ch. 36)

Behind the mask there may be an unknowable thing pushing, or there may be nothing at all; but a man who means to strike can only strike through the mask. The white whale, for him, is not a fish. It is every unearned injury the world has done to one man, finally given a body you can put a harpoon into. What makes Ahab formidable is not that he is mad; it is that he knows he is being unreasonable and has already asked himself why he cannot stop.

Starbuck swallows it. His failure begins here: every objection he makes is correct, and every time he obeys. Stubb, the second mate, keeps his pipe going and can't see what's worth frowning about; Flask, the third, treats a whale as an oversized rat and stabbing it as a day's work. The three harpooneers are the most capable men aboard: Queequeg, Tashtego of the Gay Head Wampanoag, and Daggoo of Africa. The hierarchy is white men giving orders aft while the non-white men stand in the bow of the boat and throw the first iron.

Act three · The work of the ship, and a boy left in the water

The long middle is the part readers skip and shouldn't: while the hunt goes on, the novel takes the nineteenth-century whaling industry apart in front of you — how you put an iron in from six feet away, how a sixty-ton animal is hauled alongside and stripped in one long spiral like peel off an orange, how the fat is boiled into lamp oil in deck cauldrons fuelled by the whale's own crisped scraps. The narrator will also stop for a whole chapter on how whales ought to be classified (by book size, and he insists a whale is a fish). These "digressions" are the book's foundation: you take one animal apart from end to end and discover you still don't know what it is.

At the first lowering something strange happens: Ahab produces five men from below decks — a boat's crew of his own, smuggled aboard and known to nobody, led by a Parsee thin as a shadow, Fedallah. Even his hunting equipment is privately held. There is one other small scene: sitting on deck squeezing lumps of congealed spermaceti back into fluid, fingers working in the warm slipperiness, Ishmael finds his ambition and his temper and his name all becoming ridiculous, and wants nothing but to go on squeezing until he and everyone else are melted together. The most ecstatic passage in the book happens in a tub of grease.

Then there is Pip, a young Black boy from Connecticut, the ship's cabin help, quick, good with a tambourine. Filling in on Stubb's boat one day, he panics when the whale strikes and jumps overboard; hauled back in, he is told that next time they will let him go — work out for yourself what a whale is worth and what a boy like you is worth. The second time he jumps, the boat goes on after its whale and leaves him: one head in the middle of the ocean, water to every horizon, sun overhead. When another boat picks him up the body is intact and the mind is not.

Melville states it with no pressure at all: the sea mockingly kept his finite body up and drowned the infinite of his soul.seed ③From then on Pip wanders the deck talking nonsense — and every piece of the nonsense is true. He is the only person aboard who says out loud what this voyage is.

Act four · Nine ships, a coffin, and a musket not fired

When whalers meet at sea it is called a gam: you board each other, trade letters, news and drink. Every ship the Pequod meets is written as a mirror held up to Ahab. On the Jeroboam a mad sailor who has appointed himself a prophet warns him off the whale; the Bachelor is full of oil and homeward bound with dancing on deck, and when her captain calls out an invitation Ahab tells him: you are a full ship going home, and I am an empty one going out; the Delight has just lost five men to Moby Dick and is lowering a body into the sea as they pass.

The most damaging encounter is Captain Boomer of the Samuel Enderby, who also lost a limb to the same animal, wears an ivory arm, and taps it cheerfully against Ahab's ivory leg. Ahab demands the whale's position. Boomer gives it, then says the plain sensible thing: best let him alone — he has one arm of mine and that is enough. Same injury, same cause; one man makes it a story he tells, the other makes it his life. The scene proves that Ahab's obsession was not produced by the wound. He chose it.

Meanwhile the ship is losing its senses one organ at a time. Ahab smashes the quadrant on the deck because the thing can only tell him where he is and not where the whale is. In a typhoon off Japan, blue flame stands on all three mastheads, the crew go to their knees, and Ahab plants his foot in the fire and speaks up at it: I acknowledge your power, and I worship you defying you. The lightning reverses the compass needles; he magnetises a new one from a sail-needle and announces himself lord of the loadstone. Days later the log-line rots through and parts, and the ship can no longer even measure how far she has come. Quadrant, compass, log-line — the three instruments by which a ship knows where she is, destroyed in turn by her own captain.

Somewhere along this stretch Queequeg comes down with a fever, decides he is dying, has the carpenter measure him for a canoe-coffin, lies down in it and finds it a good fit; then remembers an errand ashore and decides not to die for the time being. Recovered, he keeps the coffin as a sea-chest and carves onto its lid, line by line, the tattooing that covers his body and that nobody can read.seed ④Later the ship's life-buoy, a cask dried out in the sun, sinks with a man who falls from the masthead, and the carpenter caulks that coffin, nails the lid down and hangs it over the stern as the new life-buoy.

On a night watch Fedallah gives Ahab three prophecies, which like all oracles sound like a guarantee: before you can die on this voyage you must see two hearses on the sea, the first not made by mortal hands and the wood of the second grown in America; I shall go before you as your pilot; and only hemp can kill you.seed ⑤Ahab laughs — hemp means the gallows, so I am immortal by land and by sea.

On the night of the typhoon Starbuck is sent below to report to the captain. A rack of loaded muskets hangs in the cabin passage. He takes one down, stands at Ahab's door listening to him snore, and points the barrel at the wood.seed ⑥He runs the whole argument through: this man will take thirty men down with him; but firing is murder, it is mutiny, and neither the law nor God is standing behind me; then tie him up? tie him up for how long? He puts the musket back on the rack.

Then comes the Rachel. Her captain half throws himself aboard: boats scattered chasing a whale yesterday, four men not back, one of them his twelve-year-old son. He begs — charter me your ship for forty-eight hours, name your price, you have a boy of your own. Ahab hears him out, says no, and orders the course held. The Rachel turns away and goes on looking for her children.

As the whale's trail grows warm the sea goes strangely calm. Ahab leans his head over the rail and lets one tear fall into it; he talks to Starbuck about forty years at sea, about the wedding night that left a single dent in the pillow before he shipped out again, about a child who does not know his face. Starbuck, eyes wet, begs him to put the helm up now, while there is still time.payoff ⑥ ↑This is where the bill for the unfired musket comes due — Starbuck's one chance to beat Ahab with words instead of force lasts these few minutes, and he does not take it. Ahab turns away, and Fedallah's two eyes are reflected in the water beside him. The chase begins.

Act five · Three days

Day one. Ahab smells the whale from the masthead before he sees it: a white hill sliding along the surface, the spout like wool, the back snarled with rusted irons left in it by a decade of other ships. He shouts that the doubloon is his, that fate had been keeping it for him.payoff ② ↑The prize he set he collects himself — that coin on the mast was never a reward, it was the pretext he showed the crew so that each man could feel he had a stake in the transaction. What Moby Dick does that day is simple: he turns, opens, and bites Ahab's boat in half like a biscuit.

Day two. Three boats go down and all three are staved, the lines fouled in a single snarl; the ivory leg is snapped off. When they count heads, Fedallah is missing.

Day three. When the whale rises again the men see Fedallah on his back — the corpse lashed there by turns of yesterday's harpoon line, eyes still open, rising and sinking with him.payoff ⑤ ↑The first hearse has arrived, not made by mortal hands: it is the whale. The Parsee has gone before him after all. Ahab understands, and understanding is of no further use — an oracle never lies to you; it comes true word for word and means the opposite. Before going down into the boat he shuts Pip in his own cabin and orders him to stay there.payoff ③ ↑It is the one place in Ahab that never hardened: the mad boy is the only person on the ship he ever spoke to plainly, telling him he had touched his innermost centre — and it is precisely because that spot might soften that he locks him below.

That day Moby Dick ignores the boats. He turns and drives his whole head into the Pequod's side. The sea pours through the breach — the second hearse of the prophecy, the one whose wood grew in America, is the ship. The name comes due at the same moment:payoff ① ↑a vessel named for an exterminated people is exterminated completely. Before the mast goes under, Tashtego is still nailing a flag to it, and his hammer catches the wing of a sea-hawk against the wood, so that the bird goes down with the ship.

Ahab darts his last iron from the boat. What he cries, in substance, is: I roll towards you, you all-destroying but unconquering whale; from the heart of hell I strike at you.

"…for hate's sake I spit my last breath at thee."— Ahab's last dart (original text, ch. 135)

The iron goes home. The line runs out, takes a flying turn, catches him round the neck, and he is gone out of the boat without a sound, after the whale. Only hemp could kill him — and the hemp that does it is the rope he threw himself. Every prophecy is exact, and he executes all of them with his own hands.

A vortex turns for a while on the surface, and then it closes, and the sea rolls on as it rolled five thousand years ago.

The epilogue is one page. Ishmael had been pulling an oar in Ahab's boat that day, filling the seat Fedallah's death left empty, and was pitched out and left floating at the rim of the whirlpool. After the ship goes down one thing comes up from below: the tattooed coffin, caulked and nailed shut by the carpenter, riding beautifully.payoff ④ ↑The place a man built for his own death becomes his friend's boat. Ishmael lies on it a day and a night, sharks gliding past him as if padlocked; on the second day the ship that finds him is the Rachel, still searching for her own children —

"…only found another orphan."— the novel's last words (original text, Epilogue)

Put the five acts together and the fuse is not the whale, and not even the leg: the fuse is the hammer-blow on the quarter-deck. How one man hates is his own business; what takes thirty-odd lives down with him is that Ahab converted a private account into a collective contract — one coin, one round of liquor, one oath, welding the interests of a crew of wage-earners to his obsession. Everything after that is the echo of that blow. Not one man aboard approves of this, and not one man aboard gets off.

Three slow-motion scenes

1 · The quarter-deck: incitement staged as communion

Run it again: noon, all hands aft. Ahab does not begin with the whale, he begins with money — coin, hammer, nail, a bounty anyone can understand. Then he asks the three mates what they would do if a whale stove their boat, and their answers are nothing but trade common sense. Only after every man has nodded does he name the animal. Then the drink.

The technique is the order of operations. Establish the interest first, then secure agreement to the obvious, and produce the unreasonable object last — by which point refusing costs a man his own words from thirty seconds ago. Melville's sharper move is to write the chapter as a play, with stage directions, soliloquies and exits. A first-person novel that abruptly becomes drama is telling you that from here on Ishmael is one more figure in the audience, and that what is being performed is larger than he is.

And one more layer: the three harpoon irons unscrewed to serve as cups were made to kill whales. Every prop in the ceremony comes from the ship's own means of production; the only thing altered is what it is for — which is exactly what Ahab is doing to the ship.

2 · The try-works: the narrator nearly capsizes his own ship

Midnight, two great pots boiling on deck, fed with the whale's own crisped scraps; the fire turns masts, rigging and faces red, and the smoke goes off across the water with a smell nobody has ever managed to describe. Ishmael has the helm, stares into the flame, dozes — and comes to having turned completely around, his back to the bow, the tiller meaningless in his hands, the ship a moment away from broaching and going over.

His conclusion is not to stare too long into the fire, and not to see the world by that lamp, or everything in your eye will take the colour of the flame. But the real force of the passage is this: the only person who nearly destroys this ship is not Ahab, it is the man standing to one side watching and telling. The narrator gets pulled off his safe perch — he is not a reporter, he is crew, and his wages come out of the same barrel. The scene also gives the book its most exact image: a ship on fire in the middle of a black ocean, burning the body of the thing she has just killed in order to see what she is doing.

3 · The Symphony: show him the exit, then close it

Before the three days of chase, Melville lays in his lightest chapter: a sky like silk, a motionless sea, the air feminine and the water masculine and the two of them reconciled for once. Ahab puts his old face over the side and drops a single tear into the ocean — worth more, the book says, than all the riches in the Pacific.

Then the conversation: forty years of sea, the wedding pillow with one dent in it, the child whose face he cannot summon; and Starbuck beside him talking about his own wife and son and a window in Nantucket. For about a minute both men are walking back the way they came.

The craft is "offer the exit, then shut it." Had Ahab been written as iron from the start, the destruction would be a machine completing its cycle and no reader would ache. Putting the softest chapter directly before the three hardest is what makes the turning we don't take feel real. The concentration of a tragedy depends less on how bad the ending is than on how concretely "it could have gone otherwise" has been written. And the close is almost soundless: Ahab turns his head away, and Fedallah's eyes are in the water. He is neither persuaded nor refuted. He simply turns.

The central device: a novel that doesn't look like one

If a science fiction novel is known by its premise, Moby-Dick is known by its shape. A hundred and thirty-five chapters plus an epilogue, mixing at least five modes: first-person sea narrative, playscript, technical exposition (cetology, anatomy, the industrial process of rendering oil), sermon, and prose poetry. Readers who abandon the book all abandon it at the same place: the action is going nicely, and the author begins classifying the motions of a whale's tail.

The structure is not slackness; it is doing one definite thing. It brings every instrument of human knowledge to bear on a single object and lets you watch each one fail. Taxonomy, anatomy, art history (all the ways painters have got whales wrong), myth, even law — who owns a whale another boat struck first. The result is that the pile of knowledge rises and the animal grows less knowable. This is why the "digressions" cannot be cut: without them the hunt is a revenge story; with them the book becomes an experiment in whether the world can be known at all, and the finding is written on the water of the third day.

The second device is the chapter on whiteness: Ishmael admits that what frightens him is not the animal's size or malice but its colour. He runs through everything white has meant to us — purity, divinity, royalty, snowfields, the shroud — and then says that white is in fact nothing, the absence of all colour. The highest grade of horror is not a monster but a vacuum of meaning. Ahab shouts at it for an entire book and it never answers once.

The third device is the narrator. Ishmael is nominally the only witness and the only survivor, yet the book is full of scenes he cannot possibly have attended: Ahab alone in his cabin, Starbuck's reasoning with the musket in his hands. The violation is deliberate — he is a deckhand one moment and omniscient the next, and disappears for whole chapters in the middle. A survivor telling the story will always end up speaking other people's insides for them.

Finally, what is actually true. A sperm whale sinking a whaleship is not literary licence: in 1820 the Nantucket whaler Essex was rammed twice and sunk by one, her survivors drifted for months and resorted to cannibalism, and Melville read the first mate Owen Chase's account of it. A named white whale has a source too: an 1839 magazine piece described a white whale of the Pacific called Mocha Dick. But an animal crossing oceans to settle with one particular man is a novelist's requirement, not cetacean behaviour — and Melville knows it, which is why he lets Starbuck say so in the first scene.

What it makes you think about

Question 1
Is taking revenge on a thing without malice madness, or the only honest response?

Take Starbuck's side first. The whale meant nothing by it; its bite belongs with the squall that rolls a fishing boat and the cell that turns cancerous — injury with no subject. Raging at it is logically equivalent to filing suit against a falling rock. More practically, that rage conscripts everything within reach: ship, men, money, forty years of skill, all converted into instruments of repayment. And in his Quaker terms there is a further charge: to treat unmeaning nature as an enemy is to put God in the wrong place.

Now take Ahab's side, which is in fact the harder one. His question is: what entitles you to be sure there is no intention behind it? Everything you have is a membrane, and nobody has seen the far side. If there is nothing behind it, then men are being maimed at random in a universe without meaning; if there is something, then what it did to me requires an answer. In neither case is meek acceptance the only reasonable option. And — this is the part modern readers skip — refusing to accept that a misfortune has nobody responsible for it is where nearly all human progress starts. The people who would not call a mine disaster an act of God produced safety law; the man who would not call cholera fate took the handle off the pump. At the time they too looked like men quarrelling with a dumb brute.

The difference may be whether the anger gets handed over. The same refusal either turns into procedure, evidence, something other people can use, or else it circulates inside one body, distilling, until it needs the whole world to burn with it. Ahab's fault is not that he hates; it is that he privatised the hatred and put other people's lives in the cost column. The distinction is easy to spend today: the bereaved person who needs a defendant, the company that personifies a competitor into a nemesis, the man who spends the rest of his life watching one name online. You can stand entirely with him and still see where the road ends.

Question 2
You can see the disaster coming and procedure forbids you to stop it. Does the musket go off?

The minutes Starbuck spends at that door are the sharpest question in the book, and the author does not resolve it.

The case for firing is nearly unanswerable. This is not an abstract risk but a settled judgement built on thirty years at sea: the man will take the whole ship down. Thirty-odd men signed on for oil, not for a funeral. And the captain's authority is at this moment being used to wreck the ship's own conditions of survival — the quadrant is in pieces, the compass is reversed. A legitimate power is spending its legitimacy on something it was never authorised to do, and obeying it at that point is carrying it out. Classical political philosophy has a whole literature on this position.

The case against is just as strong, and closer to where most of us live. First, his certainty is only his: a captain's near-absolute authority at sea exists precisely because there is no second judge out there, and "I have concluded he will kill us all" is what every mutiny in history has said. Second, the asymmetry of costs: the killing is immediate, irreversible and done by his own hand, while the disaster is future, probabilistic and done by someone else. Our different feelings about those two are not purely cowardice; part of it is honesty about the limits of one's own judgement. Third, he has already tried procedure — argued, warned, objected in the open. He has done everything a decent man can do inside the rules.

Melville's position is hidden in the consequences: he never says Starbuck should have fired; he simply shows you the invoice for not firing. And the crueller point is that the real chance was never the musket but the mouth, and it did arrive. The question recurs in ordinary life most weeks: you can see the project failing, the numbers being dressed, the decision that will hurt someone — you have evidence and no authority. Most of us do what Starbuck does: say it properly, then comply, then go down with it. The question worth asking is not whether he should have fired, but how many earlier and cheaper junctions he let pass first.

Question 3
Nobody aboard agrees with this. Why does nobody get off?

This is the most underrated question in the novel. Ahab is mad, but one man cannot sink a ship. Stubb thinks the captain is unwell and keeps pulling, laughing; Flask does not care; the irons are thrown by the three harpooneers; the crew cheer louder than anyone on the quarter-deck. A whole ship's company knows the job is wrong and carries it through together.

The defence of them is strong, and needs no word like cowardice. Whaling paid in shares, so leaving meant no income, and many of these men shipped because there was nothing for them ashore; at sea there is nowhere to walk to; opposing a captain is a serious crime; and each man sees only his own square — lower the boat today, boil oil tomorrow, mend canvas after that. Nobody stands where the whole thing is visible. Worse, there is the coin: it lets every man feel he is not being used but is holding a ticket in a game with a prize. That is what it was nailed up for.

And the other side holds too: it is precisely because each man's compliance is defensible that the ship sinks so smoothly. Ahab needs nobody's agreement; he needs each man to do his own job well. This is the most modern discovery in the book — catastrophe is usually not driven by villains but by a crowd of conscientious people who each believe they have neither the standing nor the responsibility to stop it. And the only one who says the truth aloud has already been classified as insane, so nothing he says has to be taken seriously. Ashore, the same question: what is your organisation doing right now that everyone privately shakes their head at, and that is in nobody's job description to halt?

Question 4
Somebody else's child is lost at sea. Does your ship turn?

The Rachel takes two pages, and it is the moral scale the whole book sits on.

Ahab's refusal has reasons, and they would win in any meeting room: forty-eight hours, changing weather, moving whales, a trail followed for a year that may be lost for good; he owes this ship nothing; and the missing men have already been out a night, so the odds were poor to begin with. Every refusal that takes the form of "our mission matters more" looks exactly like this: not cruel, merely clear-eyed about resources. We all run the miniature version of the same arithmetic daily.

The other side needs no argument; Melville lands the simplest possible blow. The man says: you have a boy yourself, a small one, waiting ashore — and Ahab has just been weeping over that boy. The person refusing is not someone without feeling; it is someone who has this minute been reduced to tears by the very same thing. That is the coldest stroke in the book: obsession does not make a man unfeeling, it makes him lock the feeling part away, exactly as he locks Pip below.

And the answer the story gives is not in the argument but in the architecture: the ship that finally picks Ishmael up is the one that was refused, still out looking for her children. The Pequod used every rational instrument it had and was destroyed; the Rachel calculated nothing and quartered the ocean for a boy she would probably never find, and pulled up the only survivor. This is not divine bookkeeping — Melville does not suggest anyone is being rewarded — but something colder: in a world that answers no questions, the only thing still functioning is that somebody is out looking for somebody.

The theme Melville kept writing

Lay his work side by side and the same posture recurs: a man addressing something that will not answer, with the entire drama inside the refusal to answer.

In Moby-Dick the thing is a whale and also the colour white. In "Bartleby, the Scrivener" (1853) it shrinks to a wall and a sentence — the clerk who answers every instruction with "I would prefer not to," explaining nothing, resisting nothing, leaving for nowhere, and backing a law office and the reader into a corner. In "Benito Cereno" (1855) it is the atmosphere aboard a strange ship, where a kindly, entirely well-meaning American captain looks at the situation for hours and gets every part of it wrong — a demonstration of what goodwill plus obtuseness comes to.

Billy Budd, unpublished in his lifetime and printed only in 1924, is the mirror image: an innocent sailor kills the man who has falsely accused him, and Captain Vere, knowing him morally guiltless, hangs him under the Articles of War. Ahab smashes every procedure in pursuit of his obsession; Vere crushes his own heart in order to execute procedure; both arrive at a body. Melville's lifelong question compresses to this: when a man holds a rule in one hand and a conscience in the other, and the world offers no hint, whom does he obey? He never gives an answer. He gives the bill.

Criticism and controversy

Without flattery: this book has been contested since the day it appeared, and much of the criticism is sound.

  • Its own age genuinely disliked it. By the standard figures the American edition sold on the order of three thousand copies in Melville's lifetime; a fair share of reviewers took it for a stew of novel, lecture and sermon, and his career was effectively finished off by this book together with Pierre. Its present standing comes from the "Melville revival" of the 1920s, not from the judgement of his contemporaries — a work's fortunes and its quality are not synchronised, and here they were seventy years and one complete change of literary taste apart.
  • The British edition cut the epilogue. The 1851 London three-decker did not print that final page, which let English reviewers ask, with perfect confidence, who exactly is telling this story if the whole crew drowned. An editorial accident manufactured a famous miscarriage of criticism — and a reminder that the edition in your hands changes the book you read. Abridgements that drop the cetology chapters are still in circulation.
  • The structural argument is not settled. "Too many digressions" has been the complaint since 1851. This page took its position above, but in fairness: those chapters do break the rhythm and some of them are self-indulgent, and claiming that every page is necessary is apologetics rather than criticism.
  • Almost no women. Women appear only in a few glancing strokes ashore. That is the reality of the subject, but it also makes this an entirely male world in which every imagining of love, intimacy and the body occurs between men. Scholars read the "marriage" language around Ishmael and Queequeg in sharply different ways and have reached no consensus; the disagreement is recorded here, not adjudicated.
  • Race cuts both ways. On one side the three most competent, most reliable and most dignified figures in the book are a Pacific islander, a Native American and an African, and Ishmael's fraternity with a "cannibal" was genuinely radical for 1851 America. On the other, the narration carries the racial vocabulary of its period, and Pip is written within the conventions of that period. Reading the novel as either an anti-racist work or a racist one flattens it.

Ten sentences to take away

1 · This is not the story of a man and a whale; it is the story of how one man got thirty men who disagreed with him to die on his behalf.

2 · The fuse is the hammer-blow on the quarter-deck: a private grudge rewritten as a collective contract with money, liquor and an oath.

3 · Ahab is not a lunatic; he knows he is being unreasonable — he even asks himself why he cannot stop, and then turns his head away.

4 · Every objection Starbuck makes is right and every time he complies; he, not the captain, is the figure who should frighten you.

5 · Quadrant, compass, log-line: the three things by which a ship knows where she is, all destroyed by her captain, and nobody turns back.

6 · The whale is terrifying not because it is large but because it is white — a vacuum of meaning, where anything can be written and nothing is.

7 · The cetology chapters everyone skips are the foundation: take an animal apart completely and you still do not know what it is.

8 · Oracles do not lie: two hearses, a pilot going before him, only hemp can kill him — all literally fulfilled, all by his own hands.

9 · The only man on board who tells the truth is the boy who has lost his mind, which is why nobody has to listen.

10 · What stays with you is not the whale but the tattooed coffin: the place a man built for his own death, floating up to carry his friend home.
