Novels, Deep-Read · Agatha Christie · No.46

Murder on the Orient Express

Agatha Christie · 1934

中文 →

The snow stopped around midnight, but the train never moved again. It sits wedged at an angle in a drift somewhere in the Yugoslav countryside, no steam, no clink of silver from the dining car, the whole thing stopped as if a hand had come down on it.
One small lamp burns in the corridor of the first-class coach. Behind a door locked and chained from the inside, the American businessman who calls himself Ratchett lies in his berth with twelve stab wounds in him: some deep, some shallow, some struck left-handed, some delivered after he had stopped breathing. The window stands open. On the fresh snow outside there is not a single footprint.
The murderer is still on this train. That was never in doubt. What is strange is something else: it is December, the dead season, and there is not one empty berth in the coach.

This page contains complete spoilers — who Ratchett really was, whose hand made each of the twelve wounds, and which of the two solutions Poirot hands to the police. The entire weight of this book sits in its last twenty pages; without them all you can discuss is atmosphere. If you want to keep the suspense, read the novel first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

An American kidnapper hiding in Europe under a false name is stabbed twelve times aboard a luxury train stranded in a snowdrift; the dozen-odd passengers sharing his coach — a Russian princess, a Swedish missionary, an Italian salesman, the conductor — turn out to be, every one of them, people from the household of the three-year-old girl he murdered five years earlier. They constitute themselves as a jury of twelve, one vote and one knife each, and carry out the sentence an American court failed to deliver. Hercule Poirot assembles the truth, and then, in front of all of them, hands the police an explanation he knows to be false.

Coordinates

Agatha Christie (1890–1976) wrote more than sixty novels and is, according to the Guinness World Records, the best-selling novelist in history. Murder on the Orient Express was published in Britain in January 1934, in the middle of her strongest decade. Its American edition was retitled Murder in the Calais Coach, because the US edition of a Graham Greene novel had already claimed the name Orient Express.

Its place in the history of detective fiction is unambiguous: the interwar Golden Age, and specifically the most rule-bound wing of it — fair play, the doctrine that every clue must be laid in front of the reader, that the detective may hold nothing back, and that the solution must in principle be derivable by anyone paying attention. What this book does is dismantle the one axiom that school rests on: many suspects, a single murderer. It never breaks fair play. It breaks a rule nobody ever wrote down and every reader had assumed for a lifetime.

There are two sources in reality. The shallow one is the train itself: after marrying the archaeologist Max Mallowan in 1930, Christie rode the Orient Express regularly to and from digs in the Middle East, and in 1929 the train was famously stranded for days by a blizzard in Turkey — an incident often cited as the origin of the setting. The deeper source is a real crime. In 1932 the not-quite-two-year-old son of the aviator Charles Lindbergh was kidnapped and killed, and the "Armstrong case" in this novel is a near-transcription of it. When the book was published, the real crime was still unsolved; a suspect was not arrested until September 1934. Christie wrote a lynching, and let the lynching win, next door to an unsolved murdered child.

The story

Act One · Dawn at Aleppo: two English people with very little to say

Aleppo, Syria, five in the morning, a wind that takes a layer of skin off. Hercule Poirot, a small Belgian detective, has finished a piece of business for the French garrison and boards the Taurus Express for Istanbul. He has an immaculately maintained moustache and a mind that believes in order — in his world two eggs of unequal size on a plate constitute an offence. Two English passengers share the train: Mary Debenham, a governess returning from Baghdad, young, cool, competent as a well-adjusted machine; and Colonel Arbuthnot, home on leave from India, taciturn, each sentence pressed flat before it leaves him. They are polite, correct, and carefully distant — apparently strangers who met on the journey.

Poirot sees two cracks. When the train runs late, Miss Debenham — who has not changed expression once — abruptly loses her composure, agitated in a way that is not like her, as though some date exists that she cannot afford to miss. And near the end of the run he overhears the two of them, low-voiced, saying something to the effect of: not now, when it's over, when it's all behind us. Seed ①It sounds like lovers who cannot be seen together. Poirot files it and puts it down.

Istanbul, the Tokatlian Hotel. Before he has washed off the journey, a telegram recalls him to London. He leaves the same night, on the Simplon Orient Express.

The booking office says the train is full. Luckily his old friend M. Bouc, a director of the sleeping-car company, is aboard and finds him a berth — the one left by a passenger who booked and never appeared. Bouc arranges it and remarks on the oddity: extraordinary, in December, in the dead season, when this Istanbul–Calais coach is normally empty enough to roll around in, and this year not one berth is free. Seed ②

The coach holds a textbook cross-section: an elderly Russian princess and her German maid; the Hungarian Count and Countess Andrenyi; a Swedish missionary; an Italian-American car salesman; an English valet; a young American secretary; a stout American lady, Mrs Hubbard, who has not stopped talking since she boarded; plus the colonel and the governess. The coach is looked after by the French conductor Pierre Michel.

Act Two · A man who cannot buy protection

In the dining car Poirot gets his first good look at Ratchett: an American in his sixties with a layer of benevolence over his face and a courteous laugh. Poirot watches the eyes. He tells Bouc afterwards that this is not a good man's face — that something is looking out from under the skin.

The next day Ratchett comes to him and offers money for protection: someone has been sending letters threatening his life. Poirot declines, on grounds that are entirely unprofessional:

"I do not like your face."— Poirot, refusing Ratchett's commission (paraphrase; Part One, ch. 3)

Seed ③Ratchett does not press him. He goes back to his compartment and sleeps that night with a pistol under the pillow.

That night is the densest stretch of information in the book, and it is made entirely of sound, because Poirot is lying in his berth with nothing working but his ears: a groan next door; a bell that rings with nobody there; footsteps going and coming; a knock at the wrong door; a scarlet kimono flashing past at the end of the corridor. The crucial one comes at 12:37 — a thud next door wakes him, the bell brings the conductor to the door, and a voice inside answers in French:

"It is nothing. I was mistaken."— the reply from Ratchett's compartment at 12:37 (paraphrase; Part One, ch. 7)

Seed ④Poirot turns over and sleeps. When he wakes, the train is not moving: a night of snow has buried the track and the whole train stands in open country between Vinkovci and Brod, miles from anywhere.

Act Three · Morning: a body, and a child dead five years

At daylight Ratchett's door is locked and chained from within and will not open. They break in. He is already stiff. Dr Constantine finishes the examination and the report is itself a puzzle: twelve wounds, of wildly different depth and angle; several that barely bled and were therefore inflicted after death; several struck left-handed and several right-handed. The only possible conclusion is that there was more than one killer — or that one person very much wanted it to look that way.

The window is wide open, as though someone had gone out through it; but the fresh snow beneath it is unbroken, not a footprint on it. The snow has switched off the outside world: no police, no telegraph, no fourteenth person. From the first page of the investigation this is a closed arithmetic problem: the murderer is one of these thirteen.

Two other things are in the room. A watch in the dead man's pyjama pocket, the case dented, the hands stopped at a quarter past one — a time of death that could hardly be clearer. And in the ash tray a small scrap of paper that did not finish burning. Poirot borrows the wire frame out of Mrs Hubbard's hat-box, flattens the scrap between two pieces of mesh, holds a spirit lamp under it, and the writing comes up:

"—member little Daisy Armstrong"— the words recovered from the burnt scrap (paraphrase; Part One, ch. 7)

With that the book changes direction. "Who killed Ratchett" stops mattering for a while; what matters is who Ratchett was. Five years earlier, in America: Colonel Armstrong's three-year-old daughter Daisy was kidnapped, a ransom of two hundred thousand dollars was paid, and the child was already dead. The kidnapper, Cassetti, was arrested and walked out of court on the strength of his money and of what he had on people. The family then collapsed: Sonia, the mother, pregnant, went into shock and died in premature labour with the baby; the colonel shot himself; a French maid the police would not let go threw herself from a window and was afterwards shown to have been innocent. Cassetti changed his name and left for Europe.

The man in the berth is Cassetti. So this is not a murder. This is an invoice.

Act Four · Too many clues

Poirot begins the interviews and discovers very quickly that his problem is not a shortage of evidence but a surplus of it. Start with the alibis. Everyone has one, and every single one is vouched for by the person least plausibly in league with them: the English colonel by an American, the German maid by a Swedish nurse, a full circle of testimony joined end to end. In a coach packed with strangers, everyone's innocence happens to be held in trust by everyone else.

Then the objects. Mrs Hubbard "finds" a sleeping-car uniform button on the blanket of her own berth and insists a man was in her compartment in the night — her compartment adjoins Ratchett's, with a communicating door. Seed ⑤Meanwhile a conductor's uniform missing a button is turned out of a maid's suitcase; the scarlet kimono surfaces in Poirot's own luggage; and in the dead man's compartment lie a pipe cleaner and an expensive woman's linen handkerchief with the initial H in the corner. Seed ⑥Bouc guesses steadily and wrongly throughout, settling on the Italian salesman on the grounds that stabbing is an Italian crime.

Poirot fixes on two things nobody else looks at. The first is Countess Andrenyi's passport: there is a grease spot in the space for her name, awkwardly placed, exactly covering the first letter. Remove the grease and "Elena" becomes "Helena" — H. Payoff ⑥ ↑This is the finest reversal of cause in the book. The H handkerchief in the dead man's compartment in fact pointed elsewhere — Princess Dragomiroff's first name, Natalia, begins in Cyrillic with a letter shaped exactly like an H. But the Count, afraid it pointed at his wife, got in first with the grease. The single improvised act of love in the entire plan turns out to be its only real crack — he is not caught by the detective, he is given away by his own protectiveness.

The second is the French. Ratchett's secretary MacQueen has let slip that his employer could not read a word of French and had to have menus translated. Which means the person speaking French through that door at 12:37 cannot have been Ratchett. Payoff ④ ↑Either he was already dead, in which case the watch stopped at 1:15 is a prop laid out for the police; or he was alive and someone else was talking. One of the two timestamps is forged, and whoever forged it is on the train. What makes this beautiful is that the decisive evidence is delivered as an auditory impression: the reader hears it along with Poirot, and takes it, along with Poirot, as proof that the dead man was still alive.

Act Five · The dining car: two solutions

Poirot assembles everyone in the dining car. He has two solutions, he says, and he would like them all to hear both.

Solution one. During the stop at Vinkovci a stranger in a sleeping-car uniform boarded, let himself into Ratchett's compartment with a duplicate key, stabbed him, and slipped off in the confusion. The uniform, the button, the kimono were all left by this man. Every piece of physical evidence is accounted for, every passenger is innocent, and the police can close the file.

Solution two. He does not begin with the train. He begins with a house in America five years ago, and puts each passenger back through its front door.

Thirteen people, twelve wounds. Twelve is the size of an American jury. This is not a killing done in heat; it is a trial re-staged: indictment (the threatening letters were written by the conspirators themselves, to put an outside enemy into the file), verdict, execution, and a single agreed statement. Every one of them had to strike once — so that nobody would be cleaner than anybody else, and nobody could be charged alone.

Now everything abnormal falls into place at once. The December coach packed to the roof is no coincidence; they bought every berth in it. The passenger who booked and never appeared was never going to appear — that empty berth was the plan's margin, and into it M. Bouc inserted a Belgian who had no business being there. Payoff ② ↑The seamless ring of mutual alibis makes sense too: it is a written script, each of them reciting one line.

And the loud American woman with "my daughter" perpetually in her mouth is Linda Arden. Payoff ⑤ ↑"My daughter" was never small talk; it was Sonia. The button she "found" she had placed herself; the communicating door she opened herself. The finest piece of acting in the book is a great actress playing a woman nobody can be bothered to look at twice. The louder and coarser and more skippable Christie makes her, the harder the trick lands — the reader's impatience is her makeup.

And the words on the Taurus Express, when it's all behind us, were never a lovers' arrangement. Payoff ① ↑What makes this one work is its position: it sits in the first chapter, before the reader has any case to attach it to. Readers of detective fiction are trained animals — a man and a woman lowering their voices go straight into the "affair" file. The best seeds are not hidden. They are handed to the reader to misfile.

Poirot sits down. Bouc and Dr Constantine choose the first solution. Poirot gives that version to the Yugoslav police and withdraws from the case.

Put the five acts together and almost nothing in this book is accidental — except Poirot himself. The fuse is not Ratchett boarding this train; it is a technical acquittal in an American courtroom five years earlier. From the moment that verdict landed, everything else is aftermath: a family wiped out, an innocent maid out of a window, twelve survivors spending five years locating a man who had changed his name, buying out a coach, preparing uniforms and knives. The snow is not the fuse; the snow only cut the power to the outside world. The one thing outside the plan was the telegram recalling Poirot to London: they had prepared a scene good enough for a provincial police force and found themselves with the best mind in Europe aboard — the mind, as it happens, that Ratchett had tried to buy and that had turned him down with a remark about his face. Payoff ③ ↑Ratchett was right about exactly one thing in his life: he did need protection. And he picked the one man who would not sell it.

Three slow-motion scenes

1 · The wire hat frame: a detective novel changes engines

Poirot borrows the wire frame from a hat-box, presses the burnt scrap between two pieces of mesh, holds a spirit lamp under it, and the ink comes back up. The craft here is that every action is domestic: a woman's hat-box, a spirit lamp, two pieces of wire. No explanation, no laboratory. The reader watches a piece of handwork and then catches a single word — Armstrong.

But the real function of the scene is not the physical evidence. It is the gearshift. Before it, the reader's job is to narrow thirteen suspects to one. After it, the question inverts: not who had a reason to kill him, but who didn't. Half a page, and the puzzle turns from subtraction into addition.

2 · 12:37: the ear is the easiest organ to fool

Poirot is lying in the dark. There is no image at all, only a sequence of sounds: thud, bell, footsteps, the question at the door, the French answer through it. All of the information arrives by ear, and information that arrives by ear carries a premise no reader thinks to question — that the person speaking is the person in the room.

What is nastier is the line itself. It is short, ordinary, exactly what a man woken up would say to make someone go away; too short to be worth analysing. And it is in French. Christie has buried the fact that Ratchett knew no French inside another character's offhand complaint, far away, delivered at a moment when you are busy with something else. The decisive evidence is cut in half and dropped in two places you will never look at simultaneously. The craft of detective fiction is never concealing information; it is scheduling it.

3 · Two solutions, and the silence after them

The staging of the ending is pure theatre: everyone seated, all the lights on, the detective in the middle. By the conventions of the form, what comes next is a verdict. Christie declines. Poirot lays out both solutions, makes the second one airtight, and does not say which is true; he hands the choice to the two most senior people present. They take the false one. He nods, and leaves the room.

That move shifts the book's weight from the truth to what is done with the truth. The case was solved in the dining car; the novel does not stop there but takes one more step, to the point where a detective has to decide who he is. And the author offers not one word of commentary — the reader closes the book holding not an answer but a question they have to answer themselves.

The central device: turning one murderer into twelve

The signature of this book is not a setting but a structural operation. Taken apart, it has three rules.

Rule one: switch off the world first

The snow is not atmosphere, it is the first component of the device: no police, no telephone, no fourteenth person. The essence of closed-circle detection was never that a room is locked; it is compressing the possibilities until they can be enumerated.

Rule two: clues are not scarce here, they are manufactured

In an ordinary mystery, clues are a scarce resource and the detective's job is to find them. This book inverts that: clues are abundant and mutually contradictory. A button points at the conductor, a pipe cleaner at the English colonel, an H handkerchief at the Russian princess, and twelve wounds of varying depth point simultaneously at a left-hander and a right-hander, at a strong man and a weak woman.

The conventional reading is that the murderer is laying false trails. The correct reading is that these clues were not left behind but produced — one each, by different people. The contradiction is not a side effect of the disguise; it is the product's quality standard. So long as no clue is compatible with any other, any single-murderer hypothesis bursts under the weight of its own evidence. Using an abundance of evidence to manufacture insolubility is a deeply counter-intuitive move.

Rule three: the ring of mutual guarantees

Thirteen people vouch for one another, and every pairing crosses nationality and class — which, by the common sense of the 1930s, put enough social distance between them to make collusion unthinkable, and made every statement automatically credible. And that is the one thing that gives them away: each statement is perfect on its own, and taken together the perfection is itself the anomaly. Poirot does not catch a contradiction; he notices that there are suspiciously few contradictions.

Honestly: which parts are literature rather than criminology

As a novelistic device the plan is close to perfect; as a criminal scheme it leaks badly. Twelve wounds will not fool any competent medical examiner. The snow was not in the plan — the design clearly required the "stranger in uniform" to get off at a stop while the train was running normally, and once the snow came he could not, and the outside-murderer story stopped standing up. And the odds of twelve people keeping a secret for a lifetime are close to zero. But the leaks are exactly what tells you what the book is about. They were never trying to escape. They were trying to obtain a verdict. All they needed was an account sufficient to let the file be closed. What the plan actually depends on is not concealment but nobody wanting to look too hard — and on that they were right, because the man who ends up closing the file for them is the detective.

What it makes you think about

Question one
When the law has demonstrably failed, may twelve victims constitute their own jury?

Take their side first, at full strength. This is not a mob. They did not kill the wrong man: Cassetti's guilt was established, he never denied it, and he walked free not on innocence but on money and leverage. They did not circumvent due process either; they replicated it — twelve people, the number of an American jury, one vote and one knife each. And the most persuasive point of all: they are not enforcing the law on society's behalf, they are the injured parties themselves. A dead infant, a mother dead of shock, a father who shot himself, a maid who went out of a window: no institution anywhere was ever going to settle that account.

Now the other side, also at full strength. "Technical acquittal" is a piece of sleight of hand: the technicality they object to is the law. Process is not the wrapping around justice; process is the only wall standing between justice and lynching, because in the eyes of every lyncher the accused is plainly guilty. Worse is the second thing they did: they did not merely kill Cassetti, they manufactured a stranger who does not exist. Had the Yugoslav police later arrested some real and unlucky man on that description, whose account would that go on? An operation that presents itself as patching a hole in the law begins by putting perjury into the file.

The structure is not far from us. Every "the law can't touch this, so let's handle it ourselves" moment — an online pile-on, a public execution of somebody's name — re-runs the logic of this coach: the evidence looks sufficient, the anger really is justified, and everyone involved believes they are a juror rather than a mob. What separates the two has never been motive. It is whether there is a procedure that allows itself to be overturned.

Question two
Does dividing one death into twelve wounds actually divide the responsibility?

There is a psychologically precise calculation in the design: one strike each, wounds of differing depth, some landing after the man had already died, so that no one can know whether their blow was the fatal one.

The case that responsibility really is shared has weight. This is not evasion, it is participation. They could have let the person who hated him most do it while the others supplied alibis, leaving eleven pairs of clean hands — and they refused that design. Dragging yourself into complicity on purpose is a way of assuming a burden, not of diluting one. And factually, "whose blow killed him" genuinely cannot be established; the legal chain of causation really is broken.

But the other side is more instructive. Humanity has built a startling number of responsibility-dividing devices, and they all point the same way: the firing squad in which one rifle holds a blank, so that every marksman can tell himself it may not have been him; the board vote that afterwards nobody experiences as their own decision; the algorithm that declines a loan, where the person who wrote the model, the person who approved the launch and the person who clicked confirm are none of them the person who refused. What these mechanisms have in common is that they do not reduce the harm. They reduce the number of people who lie awake afterwards. If twelve wounds really left each person carrying a twelfth of the guilt, where did the rest of it go? It did not vanish; it simply became something nobody claims. And the same "I was only a cog" logic underwrote the worst catastrophes of the twentieth century. What can vindicate them is not the technical arrangement of one strike each; it can only be whether the thing they did was right.

Question three
What entitles a man whose religion is order to make one exception?

Poirot's whole personality rests on order. He believes in rules, in symmetry, in method, in the moral weight of the truth as such. And on the last page he lies, efficiently: he simply sets out the false solution, lets other people choose it, and accepts the choice.

The defence is strong. He is not shielding criminals; he is refusing to serve as the last component of Cassetti's own system — the system that walked a rich man out of a courtroom, which would have won twice if it now got twelve people hanged with Poirot's help. A person who treats justice as the goal should not treat procedure as an idol.

The counter-case is just as hard. The danger of exceptionalism is never the one exception; it is the precedent that can now be cited. On what authority? On his own conviction about where justice lay this time — which is precisely what he condemns in vigilantes: private certainty deciding who dies and what gets recorded. He played God on the grounds that this time he could really see, and every successor will say the same. And the forged file remains in the world: a murderer who does not exist has been entered into a public record by the one man in the book whose job was to protect records.

Back in your own life: "just this once" is the hardest phrase there is. It arrives every time with excellent reasons — this case really is unusual, this person really does deserve it, this rule really is rigid. And the value of a rule lies exactly in its still being there on the day you most want to break it.

Question four
Why were you relieved when the truth came out?

By Act Five, most readers' first reaction is not "so they are murderers" but a peculiar comfort: good, it was them. A novel about twelve people conspiring to kill a sleeping man leaves you in a decent mood. That fact deserves examination.

Christie buys your allegiance with three things. First, the victim is disposed of in advance: before you know that anyone is a murderer you already know who he was, and know it very concretely — a three-year-old, a mother in premature labour, an innocent maid out of a window. She swapped the murdered party from "a sleeping old man" to "an unpaid invoice," and that swap was complete the moment the burnt scrap came up under the lamp. Second, the violence is rendered as sound: not one gory image in the book, the killing happens in the dark, and all that survives is a thud and a few lines of a medical report. Third, the murderers are thirteen likeable people, with whom you have shared a coach for two hundred pages before they kill anyone.

Is that manipulation? The defence: all novels arrange your sympathies, and that is narration rather than deceit; Christie never tells you they were right, she merely lets you walk over to their side and then, on the last page, shows you where you are standing. Making you take a position and then letting you see the position you took is something a novel can do and an argument cannot.

But the thing worth being wary of is here too. Those same three moves — turn the victim into an invoice, make the violence invisible, make the perpetrators charming — are the standard equipment of every episode of collective justice in the real world. A viral post today gives you the victim's photograph and age first and the accused person's name second; you never see the consequences, you only see the shares. The most remarkable thing about this book is not that it got you to forgive twelve murderers. It is that it spends a whole novel proving how easily you can be got there.

The recurring subject: what Christie kept writing about

Line up her major books and one obsession keeps surfacing: what is to be done about the person the law cannot reach.

In The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (1926), Poirot names the murderer and then leaves him the option of settling it himself. In And Then There Were None (1939), a judge lures ten people the law could not touch onto an island and executes them one by one, ending with himself. Murder on the Orient Express is the mildest and most radical version: the execution is carried out by twelve ordinary people, and the detective chooses not to expose them. By Curtain (1975), Poirot's last case, the road runs out — he kills, with his own hands, a man who never touched anyone and only pushed others into murder, and then withholds his own heart medication. Four books, four answers to one question, and they grow less respectable as they go: from "leave him a decent way out," to "I will say nothing," to "I will do it myself, and then I must die too."

The second recurring subject is formal: she made a career of dismantling her own trade's axioms. Ackroyd removes "the narrator does not lie." Orient Express removes "there is one murderer." And Then There Were None removes "somebody has to survive to tell it." A rule can be taken out of detective fiction without the structure collapsing — provided you have already moved that rule's entire load somewhere else.

The third is the least showy: her murderers are never monsters, they are neighbours. She spent a lifetime writing about what is under the respectable surface, and the frightening part is that the surface is not a disguise. Those people really are as they appear, and really did kill someone.

Criticism and controversy

Without flattery, this book has carried several disputes since publication, and the critics mostly have a case:

  • "Is this even fair play?" Raymond Chandler singled the novel out for ridicule in The Simple Art of Murder (1944), to the effect that only a half-wit could guess such a solution. The defence is solid too: the sold-out off-season coach, the twelve wounds of different depths, the too-tidy ring of mutual alibis — the clues are all laid out. The real disagreement is not about clues but about whether a reader is willing to give up an assumption nobody ever wrote down.
  • The characters are largely types. The excitable Italian, the stolid German maid, the meek Swede — most of them carry one label. There is a layer of self-mockery in the book (Bouc reasons by nationality throughout and is wrong throughout), but it does not clear the charge: apart from Poirot, Mrs Hubbard and the princess, these figures are closer to pieces on a board than to people.
  • The morality of the ending. Plenty of critics hold that the book makes collective lynching too comfortable — that what it delivers is emotional completion rather than an ethical difficulty. The opposite reading is that the novel deliberately pushes the choice onto the reader and that Poirot's silence is not an endorsement. The argument has never been settled, which is itself evidence that the book is still alive.
  • The ethics of the source. The Armstrong case is transparently taken from the Lindbergh kidnapping, and when the novel appeared the real crime was unsolved and the family living. Using a real murdered child as the engine of an entertainment drew objections then and draws them now.
  • Period language in her work. The 1939 British first edition of And Then There Were None was published under a title containing a racial slur then in common use, and the title was later changed by publishers; recent editions have also revised some wording, which readers variously regard as necessary or as an unacceptable alteration of a historical text. Stated here as verifiable fact, without a verdict.
  • Don't mistake the films for the book. Sidney Lumet's 1974 version and Kenneth Branagh's 2017 version both add and cut characters, motives and endings, and a good deal of what "everybody knows" about this story comes from the screen rather than the page.

Ten lines to take away

1. This is not a story about who killed him. It is a story about who he was — the book changes engines the moment "Armstrong" comes up under the lamp.

2. Twelve is not a coincidence; it is the size of a jury. They were not taking revenge, they were re-staging a trial that never finished.

3. The fuse is not on this train. It is in an American courtroom five years earlier; everything after that verdict is aftermath.

4. The snow is not atmosphere but apparatus: it deletes the police, the telephone and the fourteenth person, compressing the possibilities until they can be counted.

5. Here clues are not a scarce resource but a product — abundance of evidence used to manufacture insolubility.

6. The only real crack in the plan is the grease the Count smeared on his wife's passport: the single improvised act of love in the whole design.

7. The best seeds are not hidden, they are handed to the reader to misfile — "when it's all behind us," in chapter one.

8. One strike each is not about escaping guilt but about nobody being cleaner than anybody else; still, dividing responsibility never reduces harm, only the number of people who lie awake.

9. The case is solved in the dining car, and then the novel takes one more step — to where a man must decide who he is. The author says nothing at all about it.

10. What should chill you is not twelve murderers. It is the breath you let out when the truth arrived: the book exists to prove how easily you were moved to their side.
