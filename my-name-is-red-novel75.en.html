Novels, Deep-Read · Orhan Pamuk · No.75

My Name Is Red

Benim Adım Kırmızı · Orhan Pamuk · 1998

中文 →

Istanbul, the winter of 1591, a fine snow falling. At the bottom of a disused well outside the city, a man's body lies folded among rubble and black water, his skull caved in.
He starts to talk. He says he is dead, that his family is still searching the streets for him, that he knows exactly who pushed him in — and that the man is walking around the city right now as if nothing happened.
Behind his murder, he says, is a conspiracy against our faith, our traditions, and the way we see the world.

This page contains complete spoilers — which of the three painters is the killer and why, who murders Enishte Effendi, why Shekure remarries while her father's body is still warm, what Master Osman does to his own eyes in the treasury, and whose face sits in the final picture. The book wears the costume of a murder mystery; you can't get at what it actually wants to talk about without knowing who did it and why.

In one sentence

In the Ottoman court workshop at the end of the sixteenth century, the Sultan secretly commissions a book to be illustrated partly in the manner of the "Franks" — the Venetians; one illuminator, afraid this is blasphemy, is murdered by a colleague, who then kills the old man running the project to cover his tracks; a man back from the provinces hunts the killer while trying to win back the cousin he lost twelve years earlier; and the answer turns on the shape of a horse's nostril, and on one painter's need to put his own face in a picture.

Where it sits

Orhan Pamuk (born 1952) is an Istanbul writer who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2006. He wanted to be a painter until his early twenties, then turned to writing. My Name Is Red came out in 1998 and was the novel that made him an international name; Erdağ Göknar's English translation won the International IMPAC Dublin Literary Award in 2003.

Its slot in literary history: a historical whodunit whose murder plot carries an argument about how human beings ought to look at the world — Islamic miniature painting sees as God sees; the Venetian portrait sees as one person does. It is a distant cousin of The Name of the Rose: a closed guild, a dangerous book, and a detective story that is really chasing an idea.

The story

Act I · Homecomings: a corpse in a well, a woman at a window

The talking corpse is Elegant Effendi, a gilder in the palace workshop whose job is laying gold leaf on page borders. A few days before, on a snowy night, he walked out to waste ground with a fellow artist, who smashed his head with a stone and tipped him into the well. Chapter one is his monologue from the bottom: he doesn't know if anyone will find him; he asks only to be avenged.

The same winter a man called Black comes back to Istanbul after twelve years away. As a young man he lived in his uncle's house and fell in love with his cousin Shekure. He declared himself with a picture: Hüsrev and Shirin, the Persian lovers, at the moment when Princess Shirin sees Prince Hüsrev's portrait hung on a tree and falls for him at first sight. The picture earned him his uncle's coldness and Shekure's refusal. He left, worked as a secretary for pashas on the Persian frontier, and never got over her.

It is the uncle who summons him back. Known to everyone as Enishte Effendi — "Uncle" — the old man once served as an envoy to Venice and saw palace walls covered with portraits: every man of means had his face painted and hung, so exact that you could pick him out of a crowd. Back home he persuaded Sultan Murad III to commission, in secret, a book marking the approaching thousandth year of the Islamic calendar, illustrated partly in the Venetian manner: perspective, shading, and — most dangerous of all — a portrait of the Sultan himself. Master Osman, head of the workshop, is not to know the details; the city's zealots must never know at all. Enishte needs someone literate, someone who understands pictures and can be trusted, to write the book's text. He thinks of Black.

Shekure has come back to the house too. She married a cavalryman who rode off to the Persian wars four years ago and never returned, alive or dead. She was living with his family, with her two sons Shevket and Orhan, until her husband's brother Hasan started wanting her; she fled to her father. She is not a widow and no longer quite a wife — legally stuck in a place with no exits.

Shekure speaks to the reader directly, and with startling candor. She knows Black is back; she has let him glimpse her at a window, the way Shirin glimpsed Hüsrev in a picture. She runs the numbers: would he be a good father? Does she still love him — the boy she remembers, or this man with a bit of money now? And she admits something that frightens her: Setup ①she can no longer remember her husband's face. She dreams of him and the face is always blurred. She calls it longing. It is forgetting.

Her letters travel with Esther, the Jewish peddler who carries cloth from house to house. Esther gets chapters too — how she slips love letters among the bolts, how she reads each customer — and half the secrets in Istanbul pass through her bundle. Through Esther, Shekure returns the old Hüsrev and Shirin picture to Black: that business seems to be closed; if we talk, we talk about now.

The younger son, Orhan, also gets a chapter. Setup ②He watches this stranger who has suddenly appeared, watches his mother hurrying between the window and her letters, watches his brother Shevket hate Black on sight. A child's eye wedged between the adults' plots and appetites looks, at the time, like a bit of light relief.

Act II · Three painters and a talking dog

Besides the dead Elegant, three master painters are working on Enishte's book. In the workshop they go by the nicknames Master Osman gave them as apprentices: Butterfly, Stork, and Olive. Black visits each in turn, ostensibly carrying messages from his uncle, actually trying to work out which one is a murderer.

Each painter tells Black three parables, so the visits play almost like job interviews. The parables circle three questions the workshop has argued about for centuries.

The first is style and the signature. By the old rules a miniaturist doesn't sign his work and shouldn't have a manner of his own: the horse you paint should be the horse as God sees it, the horse every master before you painted — not the one you saw. Style, in this view, is a flaw, a personal habit nobody corrected, like an accent. Setup ③The three tell it differently: one insists a true master has no style; another says style can't be hidden, any more than a man in disguise can hide the way he walks.

The second is blindness. Setup ④The workshop is full of stories about masters who went blind in old age, eyes ruined by a lifetime of hairline brushwork. But in these legends blindness is a reward, not a punishment: the blind master can still paint the perfect horse, because he never painted the horse his eyes saw, only the one in memory — God's horse. The most famous story is about Bihzad, the great master of Herat: when the city fell and a new ruler demanded he paint in a different style, he is said to have blinded himself with a golden needle.

The third is time. Should a picture contain a moment? The miniature's world is timeless, with no particular afternoon light. The Venetians paint one person, on one day, from one angle.

Meanwhile the killer is talking too. He has his own chapters, titled "I Will Be Called a Murderer," where he describes his panic and how he puts on the same grief as everyone else. But he also speaks in other chapters under his nickname — Butterfly, Stork, or Olive — so the reader hears every one of his voices and still can't tell which face is his.

There is another force in the city. A preacher from Erzurum, Nusret Hoja, thunders from the mosque pulpit against coffee, music, pictures, and visits to saints' tombs, all of it innovation and backsliding. His following is growing. It was his sermons that convinced Elegant that Enishte's book was blasphemous, and Elegant threatened to talk. That is why he died.

The painters drink at a coffeehouse where a storyteller hangs a picture on the wall each night and speaks as whatever it shows. Setup ⑤The first night it's a dog. The preachers call us dogs unclean, says the dog — but take a look at the preacher himself... It flays the Erzurum faction and the room roars. Later nights bring a tree, a gold coin, Death, Satan — and the color red itself. The pictures on that wall are being painted for him, on the quiet, by the workshop's own artists.

At last Elegant's body is hauled out of the well. The whole workshop attends the funeral. So does the killer.

Act III · The house of the hanged Jew

Shekure arranges to meet Black in an abandoned house where, people say, a Jew was once hanged, and where nobody goes. It is an afternoon; the old man is alone at home. In that cold empty room the two of them embrace for the first time in twelve years. She stops him short of the last step: she wants a marriage, not an affair.

That same afternoon, the killer calls on Enishte.

They sit and talk about painting. The visitor wants to know what exactly the book will show, whether the final page — the portrait of the Sultan — is finished, whether he has been laboring for a sin. The old man realizes who he is talking to and keeps talking anyway: about the Venetian portraits, about how a face once painted can never be forgotten. The more he talks, the more frantic his guest becomes. Finally the killer grabs the heavy bronze inkpot off the table and brings it down on the old man's head. He takes the unfinished final page with him.

Shekure comes home to find her father lying in his blood. She doesn't scream. She does a calculation, fast and cold. With her father dead, Hasan's family has grounds to reclaim her and the children. With her father "alive," she can marry now. She hides the body in the house and tells everyone the old man is ill. She produces false witnesses to swear her husband died in battle, finds a judge to grant her the right to remarry on the spot, and the next day she marries Black.

At the wedding the bride's father is "sick in bed"; he has been dead a day and a night. Only afterward do they announce that he has "just" passed away. It is the most nerve-racking sequence in the book — not a drop of blood, just a woman moving between grief and arithmetic without a second's hesitation. She also sets Black a condition: until her father's killer is found, he may not touch her.

Act IV · Three days in the treasury

With Enishte dead, the Sultan's book is at the center of a scandal. The Sultan sets a deadline: within three days, Master Osman and Black must find the murderer, or the painters will be put to torture one by one.

There is a single clue. Among Enishte's surviving pages is a horse drawn unlike any other — its nostril is oddly shaped, as if it had been slit. Nobody would do that on purpose. It is a habit in some painter's hand that he doesn't even know he has.

Master Osman has run the workshop his whole life by the old rules. He dislikes Enishte's book and has suspected the old man of corrupting his best pupils. To trace that nostril, the Sultan grants him and Black access to the palace treasury, where centuries of books taken or presented from Persia, Herat and Tabriz lie locked away — volumes he has heard of all his life and never seen.

They search for three days and nights. Black is hunting for a clue. Master Osman is looking at pictures. He moves through them like a starving man let into a granary, page after page, recognizing legendary hands, seeing the whole lineage of his craft — and the end it is heading toward. They find the answer: in the old Mongol days, horsemen really did slit their horses' nostrils so they could breathe on long campaigns. Painters drew what they saw; the detail was copied from master to pupil down the generations until nobody remembered what it meant, and all that remained was a habit passed down from some teacher's hand.

Then Master Osman finds the needle — the golden needle with which Bihzad is said to have blinded himself. Payoff ④ ↑In Act II, the painters' blindness stories sounded like guild folklore. Here a living old man sits among the ancient books and drives that needle into his own eyes. He would rather go blind having seen the most beautiful things of his life than watch his workshop with open eyes as it learns to paint like the Venetians. The elegance of it: the novel spent hundreds of pages presenting blindness as an aesthetic ideal, and you took it for a metaphor. Here it becomes literal, and chosen.

Out in the city, the storyteller performs that night as a woman and mocks the Erzurum faction once more. The preacher's followers storm the coffeehouse and smash everything in reach. Payoff ⑤ ↑The dog that skewered the preacher in Act II got the biggest laugh of the night, and every painter who drew for the storyteller was laughing. The laughter piled up evening after evening; here the bill comes due all at once. The coffeehouse is wrecked, and the storyteller's voice falls silent for good.

Act V · The final picture

The nostril narrows the field without settling it. Black, armed, confronts Butterfly, then Stork, and the three of them go together to the abandoned dervish lodge where Olive lives.

There they find the stolen final page.

At its center is the Sultan, painted the Venetian way, with shadow and depth, so lifelike it frightens them. And in that picture someone has painted himself in, in the same manner — a painter's face, looking straight back at whoever looks at it. It is Olive.

Olive confesses. He says he killed Elegant at first to protect the book and everyone working on it: Elegant was going to inform, and they would all have died. But later, in front of that page, he found he wanted more than to stay alive. He wanted to be seen. Payoff ③ ↑Back in Act II he and his colleagues debated "is style a flaw?" as solemnly as a seminar. Now the answer arrives twice — once in the hand he couldn't control, the nostril that gave him away; once in the heart he couldn't control, the face he slipped into the Sultan's picture. A man can deny he has a style; he can't avoid leaving his mark.

Olive's dagger wounds Black, and Olive runs — he means to get to India, where the Mughal emperor is said to value painters. He bursts out of the lodge and, on the road, runs into Hasan, who has long nursed a grudge against Black. Hasan kills him with one stroke. The murderer dies of another man's jealousy, one that has nothing to do with his crimes.

Epilogue · Shekure

The last chapter belongs to Shekure. Black comes home wounded; the injury never fully heals, and for years afterward he carries a quiet melancholy. The Sultan's book is never finished. The workshop slowly declines: the painters try to imitate the Venetians and do it badly, and the old way isn't properly kept either.

She says she always wanted one picture: of herself with her two sons — Payoff ① ↑(this is a paraphrase). In Act I, admitting she couldn't recall her husband's face looked like a widow's guilt. Only now does it show its weight: what she wants isn't a pretty picture but a face that won't be forgotten — the very thing Enishte brought back from Venice and Olive killed for. No one can paint it, and her face and her sons' faces will be forgotten too.

Finally she says she told all this to her younger son, Orhan, who later wrote it down; for the sake of a good story there's no lie he won't tell, so don't believe all of it. Payoff ② ↑The child tucked among the adults in Act I turns out to be the book's author — and the real author is also named Orhan, with a mother named Shekure and an older brother named Shevket. A novel about painters forbidden to sign their work quietly signs its own name on the last page.

If you want the fuse that set all this off, it isn't the well and it isn't the inkpot. It is the moment Enishte stood in Venice in front of a wall of portraits and saw, for the first time, that an ordinary man could be painted as one of a kind and never forgotten. He carried that idea home and handed it to the Sultan, to the painters — and planted it in the heart of one painter who wanted to be seen. Everything after — Elegant's fear, Olive's two murders, Master Osman's needle, Shekure's unpaintable picture — is the aftershock of that one look.

Three slow-motion shots

One · Chapter one in the well: let the dead speak first

A murder mystery usually opens with someone finding a body. Pamuk opens with the body: the dead man lies in the well, complaining, remembering, asking for vengeance, even worrying whether his family will grieve.

Why it works: it announces the book's rules on page one — here anyone may talk: the dead, the killer, a gold coin, a color. The opening shifts the reader's appetite from "who did it?" to "how does this world see?" And it hides a sly unfairness: the dead man says he knows who killed him and won't say. The first person in the book to have the answer is the one who can never testify.

Two · The hidden body and the wedding: a woman who never hesitates

When Shekure finds her father, Pamuk doesn't point the camera at her tears. He points it at her arithmetic: what Hasan will do, how the judge will rule, where the children will live, whether Black can be relied on.

Why it works: because we're inside her, we're forced to do the sums with her instead of standing outside to judge. The pace is brutally tight — from body to wedding the narration barely breathes, chapters handing off from voice to voice like doors slamming one after another. By the time the death is announced as "just now," the reader realizes they've been an accomplice to the deception, and a willing one. The scene turns plot into a predicament: in that city, the only tools a woman had to protect her children were marriage and lies.

Three · The needle in the treasury: a man who chooses to see, then stop seeing

Master Osman's nights in the treasury are the slowest pages in the book. The investigation fades into the background; the camera holds on an old man and old pictures. He recognizes a master's brushwork, works out which older book a particular horse was copied from, as if he were reading his own family tree.

Why it works: seeing and blindness reach their peak in the same scene. He sees more beauty than ever before in his life, then ends his seeing with his own hand. Hundreds of pages of blindness legends have paved the way, so it doesn't feel abrupt — just terribly logical. He isn't fleeing the new world; he's choosing what the last thing he sees will be.

The book's central device

One · Fifty-nine "I"s: a book where everything gets a voice

The novel has fifty-nine chapters, and each is headed by a speaker: I Am a Corpse, I Am Called Black, I Am a Dog, I Will Be Called a Murderer, I Am Your Beloved Uncle, I Am Orhan, I Am Esther, I Am Shekure, I Am a Tree, I Am a Gold Coin, I Am Death, I Am Red... People, animals, objects, a color, the devil — all take turns addressing the reader.

The device does three jobs at once. First, it pushes the rules of the detective game to their limit: the killer is among the narrators, you've heard him describe the murder in his own words, and you still can't name him, because he's also chatting with you under another name. Second, it stages the very contest between two ways of painting: each chapter is a fixed point of view — the Venetian "one person, from one spot" — while the book assembled is like a miniature, seeing everyone at once from above. Third, the object chapters (a coin narrating its travels around Istanbul, a tree that would rather be a meaning than a tree) are the storyteller's act: the novel moves the coffeehouse wall of pictures straight onto the page.

The cost is real: the constant handing-off and circling back often drags, and some chapters read like essays. More on that under criticism.

Two · "Style is a flaw": an aesthetic doctrine turned into evidence

The miniature tradition, roughly as the novel's characters state it (grounded in art history but pushed to an extreme): a painter shouldn't paint what the eye sees but the world as God sees it, so the picture has no vanishing point — the spot where all lines converge in perspective. Distant figures needn't shrink; important figures are drawn larger. A horse should be painted as horse itself, refined as masters copy masters across centuries. In this creed personal style is an impurity: wherever you differ from the masters is where you haven't learned enough. A signature is vanity.

The clever move is turning this doctrine into a forensic tool on the spot. The slit nostril proves that "no style" is impossible: a habit passed down generation after generation, its meaning long forgotten, stays in the hand like a fingerprint. The killer is caught not through motive or a broken alibi but because his hand has a personality.

To be clear: this is the novel's construction, not an art-historical verdict. Ottoman painting did not wholly reject portraiture — court artists of this very period produced portrait series of the sultans, and as early as 1480 Mehmed the Conqueror had sat for the Venetian painter Gentile Bellini. Setting "East vs. West" and "God's eye vs. the individual's eye" up as a clean opposition is a literary need: the book wants a crisp conflict of ideas strong enough to carry a murder.

What it makes you think about

One · Keep a dying craft alive, or learn the new way?

You are Master Osman. Your ruler wants your workshop to learn Venetian portraiture. You know the new way will win: it's more lifelike, more flattering, and the Venetians do it better than you could. Do you teach your pupils the new way, or go down with the old one?

The strongest case for learning the new way first. Every tradition was new once. The miniature itself is a mongrel of Chinese, Persian and Mongol influence — the slit nostril proves it, a fossil of a forgotten reality. Refusing change isn't loyalty to a tradition; it's loyalty to wherever the tradition happened to stop. And the choice isn't abstract: the workshop is full of living people who need to eat, and if the Sultan no longer wants their pictures they have no livelihood. Master Osman's needle looks heroic; in practice it abandons his pupils to a future he refuses to face. The ending bears that out: the workshop declines, the new style is learned badly, and the old one isn't saved. Those who refuse the transition don't preserve the tradition; they just give it an uglier death.

The other side is just as hard. Some things stop being themselves once they're "improved." The miniature isn't painting that hasn't discovered perspective yet; it's a different claim about the world — that a human being is not the center of the universe and a picture shouldn't serve one person's eye. Painting it in perspective is like setting scripture to a pop tune: the tune may be catchier, but it's saying something else now. Master Osman's needle is a refusal to become a second-rate Venetian — which is exactly what the workshop later became.

Where does the author stand? Pamuk offers no verdict, but his sympathy clearly lies with what's being lost, alongside a clear-eyed sense that it can't be kept — the emotion he would later name hüzün, a Turkish word for a shared, citywide melancholy. Bring it home: a calligrapher facing digital fonts, a craftsman facing the assembly line, a translator facing machine translation — which kinds of holding on are guardianship, and which are just refusing to admit you've grown old?

Two · Wanting your face on your work: vanity, or a human right?

Olive's deepest motive isn't money or faith. He wants to be seen — to put a face that's his own into a picture made for the Sultan. Is it a sin to want people to know "I made this"?

First, the workshop's old rule at full strength. Not signing isn't oppression; it's a humble kind of freedom. Your craft belongs to something far larger than you — a tradition, a guild, a faith — and your work can outlive you precisely because it was never "yours." Once signatures appear, competition, envy, reputation and credit-stealing follow; the workshop's rivalries and Olive's final madness all grow out of that word "I." The masons of a cathedral, the compilers of a dictionary, the engineer who wrote one module of the software you're using — most of them are nameless, and their work matters no less.

The other side: spending a lifetime on work while being forbidden to let the world know you existed is its own kind of erasure. "Style is a flaw" sounds noble, but whom does it serve? The workshop chief above you, and the ruler who takes all credit in his own name. The Sultan may have a portrait; the painter may not even have a corner. Olive's crime isn't wanting to be seen; it's that he killed to be seen.

The novel stands somewhere in the middle, tilted: it condemns Olive, then lets its own author sign the last page. Bring it home: you built the crucial piece of a project and the report says "the team" — is that sting you feel vanity, or something you're owed?

Three · Shekure hides her father's body: betrayal, or a mother's duty?

Her father has just been bludgeoned to death in his own house, and the daughter's first move isn't to call the authorities or to mourn. She hides the body, procures false witnesses, and marries the next day. What do you make of her?

The prosecution's case at full strength. She disrespects the dead: she leaves her father lying in the house overnight so his "consent" can cover her wedding. She corrupts the law: perjured witnesses and a rushed ruling are exactly what rot looked like in that society. She isn't honest about feeling, either: she herself can't say whether she's marrying Black for love or because he's the best option on the table. Someone who is coldest at the moment she should be most grief-stricken — a reader has every right to feel a chill.

The other side: these were the only tools she had. In that city, a woman with a missing husband and a freshly dead father had no property rights and no say; her children could be taken by her in-laws at any moment, and the brother-in-law who wanted her owned a sword. If she hadn't hidden the body, the ending was easy to foresee. Her composure isn't the absence of grief; it's that grief didn't get to go first. Pamuk lets her speak to us in her own voice precisely so no one can judge her from outside.

Bring it home: at a funeral, some people are weeping while someone else is on the phone sorting out insurance, the house, the children's school. Does that person seem cold to you — or do you know the weepers can only weep because someone is doing that?

The obsession: what Pamuk keeps writing

Pamuk returns again and again to one city and one fault line: Istanbul caught between East and West, Turks imitating the West while mourning what they are losing. My Name Is Red tells it through a sixteenth-century workshop. His earlier The White Castle (1985) tells it through a Venetian scholar captured and brought to Istanbul, where he lives alongside an Ottoman scholar who looks exactly like him, until their identities bleed into each other and nobody can say who is who — the East-West story in another key.

The second obsession is seeing and being seen. He wanted to be a painter, and this book is his attempt to put a painter's eye inside a novel. The Museum of Innocence (2008) goes further: its hero collects every object his beloved touched, and Pamuk later opened a real museum of the same name in Istanbul. Keeping a face, a thing, from being carried off by time — Shekure's unpaintable picture becomes a whole building.

The third is religion against the secular. Snow (2002) moves it to a contemporary border town in Turkey: Islamists, secularist soldiers, girls in headscarves. The Erzurum preacher and the wrecked coffeehouse in My Name Is Red are that conflict's deep historical source. And in Istanbul: Memories and the City (2003) he gave a name to the mood running through all of it: hüzün.

Controversy and criticism

Pace and pedantry. The commonest complaint: too long, too slow, too fond of lecturing. The painters' rounds of parables and Master Osman's leafing through the treasury wear out many readers. As a mystery, the solution has also been called perfunctory: the killer is finally identified through confrontation and a bit of luck rather than airtight deduction.

Is "East vs. West" too tidy? Some critics and art historians point out that the novel reduces the differences between Islamic miniature and Renaissance painting to a duel of worldviews, when the real history was full of exchange and mixing (flagged above). Others accuse Pamuk of writing East-West stories for Western readers — exoticizing his own civilization for export. Defenders argue he is dismantling the binary, just by building it very neatly first.

Shekure: the best character, or the plot's prize? She has her own voice and her own calculations and is often seen as the book's most vivid figure. But some readers note that structurally she remains the object men compete for — Black wants her, Hasan wants her, and solving the case "delivers" her. The novel gives her a point of view but little room to act; that can be read as realism or as a limitation.

The author's public situation. This has nothing to do with the plot but is often raised alongside it. In 2005, after telling a Swiss magazine about the killing of Armenians and Kurds in the late Ottoman era and afterward, Pamuk was charged in Turkey with "insulting Turkishness"; the charges were dropped the following year. The case split opinion in Turkey: some saw him as a symbol of free expression, others as pandering to the West. It was still being argued over when he received the Nobel in 2006.

Ten lines to take away

③ The miniature sees with God's eye, the Venetian portrait with one person's; the fight isn't about technique but about where a human stands in the world.
