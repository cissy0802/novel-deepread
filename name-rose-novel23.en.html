Novel Deep-Read · Umberto Eco · No.23

The Name of the Rose

Il nome della rosa · Umberto Eco · 1980

中文 →

The top floor of the abbey has no windows. At the far end of it a mirror blocks the way; press two letters in the line of scripture carved above it and the mirror swings open on a room no one has entered in seventy years.
Inside sits an old man who went blind decades ago, the only surviving copy of a book open on his lap. He heard the footsteps long before they arrived. He has been waiting his whole life for this. He says: read it.
Then, as his visitor pulls on gloves and begins to turn the pages, he reaches out, tears the leaves off one by one, puts them in his mouth and swallows.
The corners of those pages are poisoned. Three men have already died on that poison. He eats the rest of it himself, mouthful by mouthful.

This page contains complete spoilers — how each of the seven dead men died, what the forbidden book actually is, who is hiding behind the mirror, why the greatest library in Christendom ends as ash, and what the closing line of Latin means. All the weight of this novel sits behind its ending: without knowing that the killer's motive is laughter must never be made legitimate, it is only a very good medieval murder mystery. If you want the suspense preserved, read the novel first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

In the winter of 1327 a Franciscan friar who used to be an inquisitor arrives at a Benedictine abbey in northern Italy with a teenage novice, ostensibly to prepare a political negotiation, and walks into seven deaths in seven days; reasoning his way into the deepest room of a labyrinthine library, he discovers that the killer is not a man but a poisoned book — the lost second volume of Aristotle's Poetics, the one about comedy and laughter — and that the blind old monk guarding it would rather eat the book and burn the library to the ground than let laughter receive the highest possible endorsement.

Coordinates

Umberto Eco (1932–2016) was an Italian semiotician and scholar of medieval aesthetics who taught for decades at the University of Bologna. He did not write a novel until he was forty-eight; before that he was the author of The Open Work and A Theory of Semiotics. The Name of the Rose appeared in 1980, won Italy's Strega Prize in 1981, and went on to sell in the tens of millions — a book stuffed with untranslated Latin, scholastic philosophy and fourteenth-century church politics became a global blockbuster, which is itself one of the odder events in twentieth-century publishing.

Its place in literary history is easy to name: the most successful hijacking in postmodern fiction — a detective shell bolted onto a historical novel, with an essay about whether signs can ever be read correctly hidden inside. Its immediate ancestor is Borges: the labyrinth library descends directly from "The Library of Babel," and the villain is called Jorge of Burgos — a blind old man who rules a library, which is to say Jorge Luis Borges himself, cast as the enemy. The book is a love letter to an idol, and also a patricide.

The story

Act One · A horse nobody has seen

Late November 1327, two men ride up a mountain. In front is William of Baskerville, an English Franciscan who knows herbs, machines and logic, and who carries a recently invented novelty perched on his nose — spectacles, which almost nobody in Europe has yet seen. He served as an inquisitor once and resigned the office himself. Behind him rides Adso of Melk, a Benedictine novice, the teenage son of a German baron, who narrates the whole book as a very old man.

Before they reach the gate, a knot of monks comes hurrying out looking for something. William reins in and tells them: you are looking for the abbot's horse — black, five feet high, magnificent tail, small round hooves but a very steady gait, small head, fine ears, large eyes. He went that way. The cellarer stares: you have seen him? No, says William. Seed ① He has seen hoofprints in the snow, the height of a snapped branch, a few long black hairs caught in a bramble — plus one premise that does more work than any of it: the abbot's horse is necessarily the finest horse in the abbey, and a crowd of frightened monks is out hunting it, so it matters to them. As for the name Brunellus, that is simply what the best horses were called in those days.

Putting this scene first is the author signing a contract with the reader: this world can be read, provided you know how to read signs. The next six hundred pages are that contract being torn up, slowly, in front of you.

The abbot, Abo, receives them and hands over a problem: three days ago the young illuminator Adelmo of Otranto was found dead at the foot of the cliff. The window above was locked from the inside; the body was outside. Find out what happened, says the abbot — with one condition. You may not enter the library.

The library occupies the top floor of the Aedificium: a square building with an octagonal tower at each corner, kitchen and refectory below, scriptorium in the middle, and above that the labyrinth — the largest collection of books in Europe and the least accessible. The rule is simple. A monk who wants a book sends up a request; whether he gets it is the librarian's decision. Only the librarian knows where any book is, and only he knows how to walk up there without getting lost. The current librarian is Malachi of Hildesheim; his assistant is Berengar of Arundel.

William goes to look at the dead man's desk in the scriptorium. On it are Adelmo's illuminations: in the white spaces around the capitals he has painted a world turned upside down — rabbits with bishops' heads, a donkey treating a patient, a knight fleeing from a hare. The monks crowd round and laugh. Then a voice comes from the corner: Jorge of Burgos, the oldest and most feared monk in the abbey, blind for years, able to recite half the library from memory. He does not laugh. These things should not be drawn, he says. Nowhere in the Gospels is it written that Christ laughed; laughter turns the sacred into the ridiculous; a monk may smile, he must not guffaw. The room goes cold. William disagrees, they trade a few rounds, neither convinces the other. Seed ②

That night the second man dies.

Act Two · Blood, water, and a black finger

The Greek translator Venantius of Salvemec is found head-down in a vat of pig's blood; it happens to be slaughtering day. Everyone thinks immediately of Revelation: at the second trumpet, a third of the sea became blood.

Examining the body, William notices something nobody else cares about: the tip of the dead man's right index finger is black, and so is his tongue, as if stained with ink, but not quite ink. Seed ③ He offers no explanation. He simply files it.

Then he establishes that Adelmo was not thrown from anywhere. There was sleet that night; the body slid to the ravine from the wall above, which means he jumped. The reason, reassembled later, is squalid: the young illuminator wanted to see a book the library would not give him, and Berengar the assistant librarian offered to help, with his body as the price. Afterwards Adelmo broke down under the guilt and threw himself off the wall — but not before passing on to Venantius a phrase he had prised out of Berengar, about something called finis Africae, the end of Africa.

So Venantius went up. He took a book out of the forbidden room, carried it down to the scriptorium, read it through the night making notes, and was dead by dawn. It was Berengar, in a panic, who dragged the corpse out and tipped it into the blood — anything to move the business away from the library. He took the book too.

On Venantius's desk is a sheet of parchment covered in zodiac symbols, a cipher; held near a flame it yields a further line written in invisible ink. Decoded, it says: the secret of the end of Africa, hand over the idol, work on the first and the seventh of the four. Grammatically complete. Semantically useless.

Which leaves them no option but to break into the library at night. What it actually is starts to emerge: not rows of shelves but a honeycomb of small rooms, each with a verse from Revelation carved above its door, the initial letters of the rooms spelling out place names — Africa, Hibernia, Germania, Anglia. Worse, some rooms burn hallucinogenic herbs and some have distorting mirrors on the wall. Adso, in the dark, meets a swollen twisted giant and nearly collapses with terror; it is himself. Seed ④ They barely find their way out.

The oldest monk in the house, Alinardo of Grottaferrata, half memory and half rambling, sidles up with a theory: don't you see, boy, the deaths are following the seven trumpets of the Apocalypse — hail, blood, water, a third part of the heavens, locusts and scorpions. The Antichrist is coming. Seed ⑤ William scoffs at first. Then the deaths keep matching, and he starts using the pattern to predict who dies next.

Third: Berengar, the assistant librarian, drowned naked in a tub in the bath house. Third trumpet, water. William looks at his hand. Right index finger black. Tongue black. Two bodies, the same stain.

Somewhere in these nights, in the kitchen, Adso meets a girl from the village below who has slipped inside the walls. She comes to trade: certain monks give food for her body, and the cellarer and Salvatore broker it. She has no money, no surname, and hunger under her clothes. What happens between them is the only sexual experience of the boy's life; afterwards he is terrified, ashamed, and discovers that he loves her. He does not speak her dialect, she cannot read, and from beginning to end they never exchange a single sentence either of them understands. He will never learn her name. Seed ⑥

Act Three · The pope's men arrive

Now the other plot lands on top. William's official errand is to prepare a negotiation: Pope John XXII, in Avignon, and the Franciscans are at each other's throats over a question — did Christ and the apostles own property? The radicals say no: Christ owned nothing, therefore a true church should own nothing. That sounds like theology and is entirely politics, because if Christ owned nothing, what exactly is a papacy richer than most kingdoms? Behind it lies the struggle between the Holy Roman Emperor Louis and the pope, with the Franciscans on the emperor's side. Both delegations arrive, and the papal party contains a man who chills the room: the inquisitor Bernard Gui — a real historical figure, author of a working manual for the prosecution of heretics.

The conference is a catastrophe. The theological debate has barely caught fire when Gui arrests two men: the cellarer Remigio of Varagine and his hanger-on Salvatore, a wretch who speaks a private pidgin assembled from fragments of several languages. The arrests have nothing to do with the murders. In their youth both men followed Fra Dolcino — a real heretic, executed in 1307 — whose movement began in apostolic poverty and ended in armed insurrection. A black cat and a cockerel are found on them, props for the night-time trade in food and flesh; Gui declares them instruments of witchcraft.

The interrogation is the coldest passage in the book. Gui does not need evidence, only a story that coheres: heretic → sorcerer → murderer. Remigio denies, then wriggles, and when the instruments are carried in he breaks — confessing loudly to every one of the murders, murders he did not commit, and then beginning to enjoy the confession, as though he had finally earned a place at the centre of the stage. The case is closed. The real killer is still walking round the courtyard while the justice system packs up, satisfied. The girl is taken away too, as a witch. Adso watches her led out and does not dare say a word; he assumes she was burned, and never finds out.

Act Four · An armillary sphere, a thousand scorpions, and a door

The herbalist Severinus of Sankt Wendel tells William that a strange book has turned up in his laboratory, in Greek, with other scripts bound in. By the time William gets there the man is dead, his skull broken open with a metal armillary sphere — fourth trumpet, a third of the heavens struck. The book is gone. The killer is Malachi the librarian, and his reason is idiotic enough to be unanswerable: Jorge had whispered to him that the book concerned his own affair with Berengar, that it could destroy him. He burst in, seized it, and killed a man on the way.

Then Malachi dies too, dropping during vespers, saying with his last breath something to the effect that it had the power of a thousand scorpions. Black fingers. Black tongue. Fifth trumpet, locusts and scorpions.

And here William finally connects that small detail from three days earlier. All three men have a blackened right index finger and a blackened tongue because all three did the same thing: they turned pages. The leaves were poisoned and pressed together, and the only way to part them is to wet a fingertip on the tongue. The more absorbed you are, the faster you turn, the more certainly you die. Payoff ③ ↑ The beauty of the device is that it welds curiosity and death into a single gesture: nobody is waiting in the library with a knife — the book only kills the people who are genuinely reading it.

Abbot Abo now makes the last decision of his life: bury the affair, stop William. He goes to the library alone, by a secret passage to the forbidden room that only he and the librarians know. He steps in; the mechanism above him drops; the passage is sealed. The most powerful man in the abbey suffocates inside his own wall, and nobody hears it.

On the final night William cracks the cipher. "The first and the seventh of the four" is not four books or four men. Above the mirror runs a line of scripture — super thronos viginti quatuor, upon the thrones twenty-four — and in Latin quatuor has exactly seven letters. Press the first, Q, and the seventh, R. As for the "idol" the hand must work above, the idol is the thing in the mirror: the monster that paralysed Adso with fear was his own reflection, and the mirror itself is the door. Payoff ④ ↑ The reader has already had a hand on that door, hundreds of pages earlier, back when its only job was to frighten a boy.

The mirror swings open. Behind it is the finis Africae.

Act Five · The end of Africa

Jorge is sitting inside. He is waiting because he always knew someone would come: the louder a book's name is spoken, the less anything can keep people out.

The volume on the table is a composite codex — an Arabic manuscript, a Syriac one, a burlesque, and at the back a Greek text: the second book of Aristotle's Poetics. Book one, on tragedy, everyone has read. Book two, on comedy and laughter, was lost to history. In this novel it was never lost. It has been shut in this room the whole time.

Read it, says Jorge, it is yours. William opens it wearing gloves; he worked out days ago that the pages were poisoned.

Then comes the argument the whole book exists for. Jorge's objection is not that Aristotle said something forbidden. It is precisely the reverse — the problem is who said it. The entire scholastic edifice rests on the authority of the Philosopher; anything he wrote can be used as the foundation of a proof. And so:

  • a peasant roaring with drunken laughter is merely vulgar, and it changes nothing: he still fears hell in the morning and still goes back to the field;
  • but if Aristotle devoted an entire treatise to demonstrating that laughter is an art, a technique, a road to truth, then laughter becomes legitimate;
  • and laughter kills fear — what has been laughed at can no longer frighten, and what cannot frighten is no longer sacred;
  • and without the fear of God there is no faith, and without faith there is no church.

So to Jorge this is not a bad book. It is a weapon. Payoff ② ↑ That squabble about marginal drawings on day one, which read like an old pedant objecting to cartoons, is the one moment when the murderer announces his motive in public. He said the whole thing out loud at the start; nobody thought to put "against laughter" and "serial deaths" in the same sentence. Eco puts the motive in the most conspicuous place available and then spends six hundred pages making sure you cannot see it.

As a young man Jorge came from Burgos and was the obvious candidate to become librarian; the post went to someone else. For decades afterwards he decided from the shadows who would hold it — Malachi was his choice, being obedient and ignorant of Greek. He kept the book himself. He did not burn it: he is the kind of man who cannot bring himself to destroy even a heretical volume — a book must be preserved, it simply must not be read — so he compromised and painted poison into the corner of every leaf. Whoever comes in, whoever reads, dies. Preservation and prohibition, reconciled at last.

William names all of it, and then names his own position. He is sitting in this room because he followed a false trail. The sequence of seven trumpets was an old man's babble; the first death was a suicide, the second, third and fifth were the book acting alone, the fourth was Malachi settling an entirely unrelated private terror. Payoff ⑤ ↑ And when Jorge heard that William was reading the deaths as an apocalypse, he was not alarmed — he accepted the reading, since it suggested a larger hand arranging events with him as its instrument, and let what followed go on looking like trumpets. A false pattern that two people separately decide to believe becomes, functionally, real. The detective followed a law that does not exist and caught the right man.

What he was chasing, William says, was order; and there is no order in the universe. The hypotheses that carried him here should be thrown away now that he has arrived — like kicking away a ladder once you are up it.

Jorge stops talking. He tears out the pages and eats them, finishing the poison he had set aside for other people. William lunges; in the struggle the lamp goes over into the books. It is the most flammable building on earth: centuries of parchment on wooden frames, a labyrinth on one floor, one revolving mirror for a door. The fire starts in the end of Africa, takes the library and the scriptorium, jumps to the church, the stables, the whole enclosure. Monks run about with buckets and nobody knows what to save first. Three days later the abbey is a black field.

William survives with nothing. He and Adso part on the road; Adso later hears that he died in the great plague.

Many years afterwards the old Adso goes back to the ruin and picks through the ash. What he finds are fragments — half-burnt leaves, a few lines, single words. He carries them home, copies them out and keeps them, assembling a small library made of stumps of sentences: a library about a library that no longer exists. He is old now and his eyes are failing, and he does not know what the words he has copied out actually say.

The last line of the novel is a Latin hexameter:

Stat rosa pristina nomine, nomina nuda tenemus.— closing line (original text; a verse from the medieval poet Bernard of Cluny)

Roughly: the rose of old stands only as a name; bare names are all we hold. The girl, the library, the book, the entire Middle Ages — gone, and only names left over. And the one person he ever loved does not even have a name. Payoff ⑥ ↑ Only here does the title settle its bill: the novel is about names coming loose from things, and the hardest instance of it is given to a nameless, speechless peasant girl who was burned.

Look at the seven days together and the fuse is not any of the murders. It is a compromise made decades earlier by a man who would neither destroy a book nor allow anyone to read it. From the moment the poison went onto the paper, everything else was simply queuing up: Adelmo sold himself to see it, Venantius sat up all night to read it, Berengar stole it to hide it, Malachi grabbed it over something utterly unrelated, and the abbot walled himself in trying to suppress the whole business. Not one of the dead was selected by a killer; every one of them walked over on his own legs — the murder weapon is not a knife, it is curiosity.

Three slow-motion replays

1 · The horse in the snow: trust built in three pages, dismantled over six hundred

Technically the scene does three things. First it demonstrates instead of explaining: we watch William pull an entire horse out of some hoofprints, a broken branch and a few hairs, which beats any amount of "he was a clever man." Second, it turns the genre signal up to maximum — the surname Baskerville points straight at Holmes, and the apprentice is called Adso, one consonant from Watson. By admitting up front that he is borrowing a shell, Eco earns the right to break it later.

Third, and this is the trap: something in that deduction is not a deduction at all. The horse's appearance and direction are read off physical traces. The horse's name is guessed from the fact that fine horses were usually called Brunellus — a probability, a stereotype, not evidence. It happens to land. Payoff ① ↑ Six hundred pages later, when William admits by the light of the burning abbey that he caught Jorge on a trail that did not exist, you can go back and see that the opening miracle was already half luck — performed in full view, applauded by everybody. The scepticism of this entire novel begins with the name of a horse.

2 · The night in the kitchen: writing a transaction in the language of the Song of Songs

The scene between Adso and the girl is the boldest stylistic move in the book. Eco does not describe bodies. He has the old monk, recalling this fifty years later, describe it in the only language he was ever taught — the imagery of the Song of Songs, the praise of the bride. Her eyes, her neck, her breasts are all rendered in borrowed scriptural similes. Not one phrase is his own.

The choice does three jobs at once. It keeps the passage scalding and clean, avoiding pornography entirely. It renders with great precision a boy who has never touched the world: he has no words, so he borrows them. And the sharpest layer is the third — this is the novel's whole theme performed in miniature. The book is about how human beings can only see what is in front of them through texts they already have; Adso reads the one love of his life in somebody else's sentences.

Colder still is the other half, which only the reader can see: this is not a romance but a transaction priced in food, and she came into the kitchen because she was starving. A boy is singing a hungry girl in the most sacred language available to him, and within days she will be dragged away as a witch. Eco never steps forward to point any of this out. He simply sets the two things side by side.

3 · The last room: staging the climax as an argument

A detective novel normally climaxes in a chase. This one climaxes with two old men sitting down to talk — one blind, one short-sighted, in a windowless room, arguing about whether laughter should be permitted. It is tenser than any chase for two reasons: the evidence is on the table between them (a book that kills whoever turns its pages, so that every page William turns has the reader thinking about his gloves), and the villain holds all the arguments — Jorge's reasoning is complete and self-consistent, and his quarrel with William is not about facts but about whether truth may be sacrificed to keep order. The argument ends not in persuasion but in eating.

The book's central device: a library that lies

Literary novels do not always run on a device. This one does — the building, and the epistemological joke built around it. Four layers.

1 · The library is a map of the world

Books are shelved not by subject but by place of origin: works from Africa live in the rooms whose letters spell Africa, English books in England, and the geometry of rooms and towers composes the map. Which means you must already know where a book came from in order to find it — precisely what an ordinary monk cannot know. The catalogue system is itself the lock on the door. And William does not solve the maze from inside it. He solves it from outside: marking rooms and bearings at night, then standing in the courtyard by day counting windows until the floor plan can be deduced. The labyrinth is insoluble from within and a rather simple diagram from without — a sentence you can lay over the entire novel.

2 · The detective machine has been sabotaged on purpose

The standard contract of detective fiction: the world looks chaotic, a hidden order exists, the detective exposes it, order is restored. Every clause here is twisted. The murders have a pattern (seven trumpets) and the pattern is false; the detective solves the case and his chain of reasoning is wrong; at the moment of revelation the evidence, the motive and the crime scene all burn together; and nobody gets justice. William's remark about the ladder — climb up on your hypotheses, then kick the ladder away — is Wittgenstein's move, put in the mouth of a friar in 1327. The Middle Ages in this book are real; the mind living inside them is twentieth-century.

3 · The book that does not exist

To be honest about it: the second book of Aristotle's Poetics is genuinely lost. What survives is an anonymous Greek outline known to scholars as the Tractatus Coislinianus, which some believe reflects its contents — a hypothesis, not a settled fact. What Eco did was take a real hole in the record and put a book inside it. This is far better than inventing a fake volume: the absence is real, so the book carries real weight, while any reader can verify that it does not exist, so nobody is being conned. Comedy rather than some other lost treatise, because laughter alone holds all three threads at once — theologically it touches fear and faith, politically it touches mockery of authority, and in literary terms it is the missing half of Aristotle's system.

4 · A letter to Borges

The villain is Jorge of Burgos: blind, prodigiously retentive, master of a labyrinth library. Jorge Luis Borges went blind in later life, directed Argentina's national library, and wrote "The Library of Babel." Eco took the writer he admired most and sent him in to play the man who defends do not read this with poison and fire. That is not malice, it is a verdict: whoever treats the world as a library will end up loving the library more than the world.

What it makes you think about

Question 1
If nothing survives being laughed at, is "everything is fair game for a joke" freedom — or a slow solvent?

Take Jorge's side first, and put it more strongly than any living defender of censorship could.

His argument is not that laughter is sinful. It is a claim about mechanism: mockery is a universal solvent and it does not discriminate. It dissolves a pope's greed and it dissolves a martyr's courage; it exposes frauds and it silences serious people. It also enjoys a brutal asymmetry — refuting an argument is laborious, laughing it off takes one line. A society that can laugh at anything eventually finds it can no longer solemnly hold anyone responsible for anything, because every solemnity can be turned into a meme faster than it can be made. Jorge is not worried about God losing face. He is worried that once fear has been laughed away, the last rope tying people together is cut. His diagnosis is essentially correct. He simply chose to save the rope.

Now William's side, at full strength: a truth that has to ban laughter in order to stand is no longer a truth, it is discipline. Laughter is dangerous precisely because it is the one critical instrument that requires no permission, no learning and no literacy — which is why it is always the first thing regulated. And Jorge's programme contains a fatal contradiction: he protects faith with poison and defends the fear of God with murder. An order that has to kill to survive has already conceded that it cannot stand up.

The re-run today is almost line for line. Online irony strips the dignity off everything, which is excellent when the target is a fraud or an authority and corrosive when the result is that no sincere statement can be made without a pre-emptive self-mockery, and "taking it seriously means you lose" becomes the default setting. The question worth asking is not which side you are on, but this: when was the last time you used a joke to deflect a question you could not actually answer? Was that William's weapon, or the solvent Jorge was afraid of?

Question 2
Should knowledge have gatekeepers — or does every gate eventually exist to protect the gate?

The strongest case for gatekeeping. This abbey is not a villain's lair; it is how knowledge actually survived for several centuries. With no printing press, parchment worth its weight in silver, and war outside the walls, classical texts came down to us precisely because of institutions that locked books on a mountain, refused to lend them and copied them by hand. Strictly speaking, it is because of men like Jorge that we can still read book one of the Poetics. And the gatekeeper's reasons are not all self-serving: some texts do harm — a wrong prescription, an elegantly constructed theory of hatred — and when they reach unprepared hands, somebody else pays. Every current argument about whether to label, downrank or remove runs on the same logic: not "you may not know," but "I decide when you are ready."

The other side is just as hard. A gatekeeper can only keep the gate according to his own taste, and taste hardens into fact. Jorge picked Malachi to be librarian because he was obedient and knew no Greek: the real output of a permission system is not safety but a lineage of mediocre, compliant custodians. And then there is the fragility of concentration — the rarest manuscripts in Europe stacked in one building, one lamp knocked over, all of it gone. Dispersal, duplication and publicity are what actually keep texts alive, a verdict the printing press delivered a century later.

Bring it home: you almost certainly hold information that is "not suitable" for someone — a child, a parent, a subordinate, a friend who is already having a bad week. Your reasons were good. The problem is what the reason turns into with use: it starts as protecting them and becomes protecting yourself from having to handle their reaction. Jorge's corruption did not begin in malice. It began with they are not ready yet.

Question 3
How far is a coherent explanation from the truth — and what if the coherent one happens to catch the right man?

The novel runs the same experiment twice. Bernard Gui needs a story that holds together: the cellarer is a heretic, heretics practise sorcery, sorcery leads to murder — every link citable, every link consistent with the world he has seen. Once the instruments appear, the defendant begins collaborating on the story too: a confession does not come out of memory, it grows into the shape of the question. The case closes, everyone relaxes, except that the murderer is still crossing the courtyard.

William also wants a coherent story: the seven trumpets. He is better than Gui in that he is willing to be refuted, but the disease is identical — he sees the pattern first and then goes looking for facts that fit it. And he does catch his man, which is the genuinely disturbing part: this time the wrong method produced the right answer, so nothing exists that could have told him the method was wrong. Had he condemned an innocent instead, the book would be far easier to read.

In defence of pattern-hunting, the strongest case is that you cannot act without a hypothesis. No investigation, diagnosis, hire or investment waits for complete information; you form a guess and walk it into reality. The ladder is necessary. The only question is whether you remember to kick it away. Harder still: your hypothesis changes what you are observing — Jorge kept going down the apocalyptic road because he heard someone was reading the deaths that way. You decide a colleague is undermining you, so you start guarding yourself, so he starts undermining you. The pattern was false, and the two of you made it true together.

Which makes the practical version of this question not "am I right" but: if this account of mine were wrong, where would I see it? When you have no answer to that, what you are holding is not an explanation, it is a faith — and faith needs no evidence, so it never gets corrected. That is exactly the condition of the blind man sitting in the last room.

Motifs: what Eco kept writing

Line his novels up and the obsession is unmistakable: a text can kill, and what kills is never the text — it is the reading.

Foucault's Pendulum (1988): three bored editors splice occult material into a fake world conspiracy, purely as a prank, and then real believers turn up and kill for a secret that does not exist — invention starts collecting bodies the moment somebody takes it seriously. Baudolino (2000): a born liar whose fabrications keep turning into official history. The Prague Cemetery (2010) is the most direct of all — how the Protocols of the Elders of Zion, a forgery that fuelled decades of persecution, might have been stitched together by a professional forger. Fake document, real corpses. Numero Zero (2015) moves the same operation into a modern newsroom.

On the academic side he fought one long war. The early Open Work (1962) argued that texts are open and readers complete them; by around 1990, watching "any reading goes" become an orthodoxy, he turned round and wrote Interpretation and Overinterpretation, insisting that openness has limits — not every reading is permitted by the text. The Name of the Rose sits precisely between the two poles: it mocks Jorge, the tyrant of the single correct reading, and it equally mocks the clever men who nail seven trumpets onto a series of accidents. What it opposes is the thing both extremes share — mistaking your own pattern for the world's.

Criticism and controversy

Stated plainly, the objections to this book have real force:

  • The first hundred pages drive readers off. Architecture, canonical hours, sectarian genealogies and theological background stop a great many people at the gate. In the Postscript to The Name of the Rose (1983) Eco broadly conceded that this was deliberate: the opening is a threshold, and the reader has to be trained into the kind of reader the book requires. Candid — but hard to distinguish from a retrospective self-defence, since blaming the reader is the most comfortable position an author can occupy.
  • Untranslated Latin. The novel is scattered with Latin left unglossed. Some call it atmosphere; others call it an elitist threshold: those who understand smile, those who don't are reminded they don't belong here — which is the exact mechanism of exclusion the novel spends six hundred pages criticising.
  • One woman, and she has no name. The only substantial female character is the peasant girl who trades her body for food and is dragged off as a witch: no name, no comprehensible line of dialogue, no interiority, her function being to give the boy an awakening and the theme a footnote. You can argue that a monastery setting makes this inevitable, or that namelessness is the theme — neither defence cancels the fact that the one character reduced entirely to an instrument is also the one woman.
  • Profound or merely ingenious. Some critics regard it as a bestseller in highbrow costume: what it mainly supplies is the pleasure of feeling that you are reading a difficult book, at a lower intellectual density than its surface suggests. Hard to falsify, but worth leaving on the table — a book that makes you feel cleverer and a book that makes you cleverer are not always the same book.
  • Liberties with history. The date, the poverty controversy between John XXII and the Franciscans, Bernard Gui, the Dolcinians, Ubertino of Casale are all real (and spectacles did exist in Italy by then); the abbey, the labyrinth library and the survival of Poetics II are invented. Read this as medieval history at your peril.
  • Do not mistake the film for the novel. Jean-Jacques Annaud's 1986 adaptation changes a good deal, most visibly the ending: in the novel the girl is taken by the Inquisition and Adso never learns her fate, while the film gives her a quite different exit, and Gui's fate is rewritten too. It is a good film that warms up the two coldest things in the book.
  • The author's own controversial remarks. Eco was a lifelong public intellectual (his 1995 essay on "Ur-Fascism" is still quoted constantly). In 2015, after receiving an honorary degree in Turin, he told reporters something to the effect that social media had given legions of idiots the right to speak, putting them on a par with Nobel laureates. Some read that as clarity; others noted that coming from the author of a novel about how gatekeepers rot, the irony was close to perfect.

Ten lines to take away

1 · The horse in the snow is a contract: the world can be read. He then spends six hundred pages tearing it up in front of you.

2 · The weapon is not a knife, it is curiosity: the book kills only the people genuinely reading it, and the faster you turn, the surer you die.

3 · Three dead men had black fingers and black tongues because all three did one thing — wet a fingertip to separate a page.

4 · The murderer states his motive out loud on day one; nobody puts "against laughter" and "serial deaths" in the same sentence.

5 · Jorge is not the enemy of books. He is the most extreme book-lover there is: a book must be preserved, it simply must not be read.

6 · He did not burn it, he poisoned it — the only arrangement in which preservation and prohibition can both be satisfied.

7 · The labyrinth is insoluble from within and a simple diagram from without. Lay that sentence over the whole novel.

8 · The detective follows a law that does not exist and catches the right man — a wrong method rewarded, so nobody ever fixes it.

9 · A man destroying a book with his mouth: suicide, blasphemous eucharist, and the final form of censorship — not burning, but swallowing.

10 · The rose of old stands only as a name. And the one girl he ever loved did not leave one.
