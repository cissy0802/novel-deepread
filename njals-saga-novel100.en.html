Njal's Saga

Njáls saga (also Brennu-Njáls saga, "The Saga of Burnt Njal") · anonymous · c. 1280 · 159 chapters

中文 →

On an autumn night the dog barks once, very loud, and then stops.
The man inside wakes and says: they have killed him, and my own death will not be long behind.
By daylight forty men are around the farm. He stands at the loft window with his bow, one arrow for each man who comes close, until someone outside slashes the bowstring through.
He turns to his wife: give me two locks of your hair, and I'll twist a new string.
She asks: does it really matter?

This piece contains complete spoilers: who refuses those two locks of hair, who reins in his horse, looks back at a hillside and never leaves, who carries a dead man's molar in his purse for years, who lies down with his wife under an ox-hide as the roof burns, and who, on a stormy night at the very end, walks through his enemy's door. Without the whole chain of vengeance, burning through two generations, there is no way to ask the saga's real question: can law put an end to revenge?

In one sentence

At the end of the tenth century, on an island with no king and no police, the cleverest lawyer alive spends his life settling other people's blood feuds and is finally burned to death in his own house with his wife and sons. This is not a hero tale. It is a story about why peace always falls just short: every time, someone has put up enough silver, and every time a single sentence, a silk robe or a lock of hair brings the whole thing down.

Coordinates

"Saga" comes from the Old Norse verb for "to say": a long prose narrative. In the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries Icelanders wrote down several dozen "sagas of Icelanders" (Íslendingasögur) about their own ancestors, the generations who settled the island from Norway and elsewhere in the late ninth century. Njal's Saga is the longest of them, written around 1270 to 1290 by an author whose name we do not know, about events of roughly 960 to 1020.

It is usually counted the summit of the form. What matters is when it was written: Iceland had just come through half a century of civil war (the Sturlung Age), and in 1262–64 it submitted to the king of Norway, ending more than three hundred years of self-rule without a monarch. A people that had just lost its own legal order sat down to write about how its ancestors had lived by law alone, and how revenge kept breaking that law apart. Keep that in mind the whole way through.

The story

Act one · "Thief's eyes": a beautiful girl and a marriage that never was

The saga opens in a valley in western Iceland. At a feast, a wealthy farmer named Hoskuld calls his little daughter Hallgerd over and asks his half-brother Hrut whether she isn't lovely. Everyone in the hall agrees. Hrut is quiet for a moment. She is lovely, he says, Setup ①and many will pay for that beauty; what he doesn't understand is how "thief's eyes" came into the family (ch. 1, roughly). Her father is not pleased. On page one it reads like nothing more than a sour uncle's insult.

Then comes Hrut's own bad luck. In Norway he became the lover of the queen mother Gunnhild, while hiding that he was already betrothed at home. At parting she laid a curse on him, and when he married his bride Unn back in Iceland, the two could never consummate the marriage. Unn told her father the truth at the Althing, the island-wide assembly held each summer, and divorced Hrut by declaring it before witnesses. The hard part was the dowry. Her father sued for it; Hrut challenged him to a duel on the spot; the old man would not fight, and the dowry was simply kept.

It looks like a digression. It is really the stage being built: on this island the law is spelled out in great detail, but no one enforces a judgment. If the other man fights better than you, your verdict is a piece of paper.

Years later Unn asks a kinsman to recover the old debt: Gunnar of Hlidarendi, a farm in the south, the finest warrior in Iceland. He is said to be able to leap his own height in full armor, and he will later own a halberd that sings aloud before it kills. Gunnar knows nothing about legal procedure, so he goes to his best friend, Njal of Bergthorshvol.

Njal is the saga's title and its heart. He is no fighter. He is the greatest lawyer in the land, mild-mannered, able to see things before they happen, and he has one feature people laugh at: he cannot grow a beard. His plan for Gunnar is a little masterpiece. Disguise yourself as a peddler, stay the night at Hrut's farm, ask your host how one goes about summoning a man to court, then repeat his words back to him exactly. That counts as a lawful summons. Hrut realizes too late who the peddler was, and at the Althing he has to give the dowry back.

Meanwhile Hallgerd has grown up: tall, hair down to her waist, a temper like fire. She is married off the first time without being asked; her husband slaps her, and her violent foster-father kills him. Her second husband slaps her in a quarrel too, and the foster-father strikes again. Two husbands, two slaps, two deaths.

Then, one summer at the Althing, Gunnar meets her. They talk a long while on the assembly field and are soon betrothed. Hrut warns Gunnar to his face; Njal, hearing of it, says only that nothing but trouble will come of her. Gunnar marries her anyway.

Act two · The slope is fair: Gunnar

The trouble starts between the wives. At a feast at Njal's farm, Njal's wife Bergthora asks Hallgerd to give up her seat to another guest. Hallgerd refuses, sneers at Bergthora's fingernails, and then at Njal himself: Setup ②that "beardless old man." Later she coins a nickname for Njal's sons, "dung-beards," as if they had to grow their beards with manure. Bergthora hits back by mentioning Hallgerd's two dead husbands. The friendship between the two men does not move an inch.

Over the next few years the two women take turns having each other's servants killed. Hallgerd has one of Njal's men killed; Gunnar pays compensation. Bergthora has the killer killed; Njal pays, reportedly with the very purse Gunnar had just handed him. Back and forth, several lives, each one settled at its proper price. It is the coldest joke in the book: two men keep their friendship alive with one bag of silver passing between them, and each time it changes hands, someone else is in the ground.

In a famine, Hallgerd sends a slave to steal butter and cheese from the storehouse of a neighbor named Otkel, and then to burn the storehouse down. At dinner Gunnar sees the cheese and asks where it came from. She answers carelessly. He understands, slaps her, and says he will not be a partner to thieves. Payoff ① ↑The sour remark from chapter one comes due. "Thief's eyes" was never spite about a pretty face. A shrewd uncle had seen her particular recklessness: hungry, she takes; having taken, she burns; having burned, she serves it at the table. The beauty of it is how petty the payoff is. Not murder. Cheese.

Hallgerd says one thing after the blow: Setup ③she will remember that slap, and repay it if she can. It is the third slap of her life.

The theft makes Otkel Gunnar's enemy. One day Otkel gallops past Gunnar as he sows his field, and his spur gashes Gunnar's ear. Not long after, Otkel dies on Gunnar's halberd. Now Njal gives his friend a prophetic piece of advice: never kill twice in the same family, and never break a settlement that good men have made for you. Keep those two rules and you will live long; break them and you will not (around ch. 55, roughly). Gunnar promises.

But vengeance has its own momentum. After a brawl at a horse-fight and an ambush where he fights against heavy odds, Otkel's son plots against him, and Gunnar kills him too. Njal's first rule is broken.

The assembly sentences Gunnar to three years' exile abroad; if he stays in Iceland, anyone may kill him without penalty. On the way to the ship, his horse stumbles. He jumps down and finds himself facing home. He looks up at his own hillside. "How fair the slope is; it has never seemed so fair to me" (ch. 75, roughly). Pale cornfields, new-mown meadows. He says he will ride home and go nowhere. His brother Kolskegg cannot talk him round, sails alone, ends up in the emperor's guard in Constantinople, and never comes back.

Njal's second rule is broken. From this moment, in law, Gunnar is a man anyone may kill.

That autumn his enemies gather forty men. They kill his hound first: that is the howl in the opening scene. Someone among the attackers proposes burning the house; their leader, Gizur, refuses outright. He will not do it, he says, even if his own life depended on it. Remember that. Some forty chapters later it has a terrible echo.

Gunnar holds the loft alone. A Norwegian climbs onto the roof to see whether he's home, takes the halberd through the window, climbs down, and tells his companions: find out for yourselves whether Gunnar is at home; all I know is that his halberd is. Then he falls dead. Gunnar's arrows keep coming until someone cuts his bowstring. He asks his wife for two locks of hair. She asks whether it matters much. His life depends on it, he says.

Payoff ③ ↑Then, says Hallgerd, I remember the slap you gave me, and I don't care whether you hold out a long time or a short one. Gunnar says only: everyone has his own way of winning fame; I won't ask you twice. His mother curses her on the spot and says her shame will be remembered for a long time. The account of one slap is settled to the penny: three husbands struck her; two died by her foster-father's hand, the third by her refusal.

Gunnar brings down several more men with the halberd and dies of exhaustion. One moonlit night afterward, Njal's eldest son Skarphedin and Gunnar's son see the burial mound standing open, lit from within, and Gunnar inside it, cheerful, chanting a verse: better to die fighting than to yield.

Act three · A slide across the ice: a molar, a foster-son, a new court

The story's weight shifts to Njal's household. The eldest son, Skarphedin, is pale, with a permanent half-sneer, and his axe has a name: "Battle-Ogress". There are also Grim and Helgi, and Njal's son-in-law Kari, a warrior who came from Orkney.

Gunnar's kinsman Thrain, who married Hallgerd's daughter, once caused trouble in Norway that landed two of Njal's sons in an earl's prison. When the sons come home and demand compensation, they are mocked to their faces, and Hallgerd, who is there, brings out "dung-beards" again.

In winter the Markarfljot river freezes. Njal's sons wait in ambush on the far bank. Skarphedin leaps the open channel in the middle of the ice, lands on the ice and goes sliding, fast as a loosed arrow, straight toward Thrain, and strikes as he passes. Thrain's skull splits; some of his molars scatter on the ice. Setup ④Skarphedin bends down, picks one up, and puts it in his purse. The saga does not explain why.

The killing is settled with money, as usual. But Njal does something larger: he takes Thrain's young son, Hoskuld, as his foster-son and raises him at home. It is the most far-sighted, and most tragic, act of kindness in the book: stitching two families' feud closed with a child. Hoskuld grows into a gentle, upright young man, and Njal's sons treat him as a brother.

Njal wants a future for him: a chieftaincy. Iceland's chieftains, the godar, were local leaders who handled both religious duties and lawsuits, and their number was fixed. So Njal proposes a reform at the Althing: Setup ⑤a "Fifth Court," a court of appeal above the four regional courts, deciding by majority vote, with a batch of new chieftaincies created alongside it. The reform passes. Hoskuld becomes a chieftain and marries a proud woman named Hildigunn, niece of the great southeastern chieftain Flosi. It is at the Althing that Njal states his creed: "With law shall our land be built, and with lawlessness laid waste" (around ch. 70).

In the year 1000 the Althing decides that the whole island will become Christian, and Njal is among the first to accept the new faith. The lawspeaker who presides over the assembly lies under his cloak for a day and a night, then rises and declares: if we split the law in two, we split the peace. A society held together by law instead of a king was probably never better than at this moment.

Then the saga's most poisonous man steps forward: Mord, the chieftain who had already given advice at the siege of Gunnar. Hoskuld is so well liked that Mord's followers drift over to him, and Mord begins working both sides. He tells Hoskuld that Njal's sons mean to kill him; Hoskuld doesn't believe it. He tells Njal's sons that Hoskuld will sooner or later avenge his father; they believe it.

One dawn Hoskuld goes out to sow his field, wearing the cloak his wife's uncle Flosi gave him. Njal's three sons, Kari and Mord rush him from behind a fence; Skarphedin strikes first. Hoskuld falls to his knees and says, as he dies: "May God help me and forgive you" (around ch. 111).

Njal weeps. He says he would rather have lost two of his own sons and kept Hoskuld alive, and that he can already see the deaths of his whole house.

Act four · A bloody cloak and a heap of chickweed: the fire

Hildigunn wants vengeance. When Flosi visits, she has the high seat ready for him; he refuses it. Then she brings out the cloak, the one Flosi gave Hoskuld, the one he died in, and throws it over Flosi's shoulders. The dried blood rattles down all over him. She demands that he avenge her husband, or be less than a man. Flosi flings the cloak off and says: "Cold are the counsels of women" (around ch. 116). He neither agrees nor refuses.

At the Althing, Flosi still chooses the law first. The arbitrators set a price never heard of before: triple the usual compensation. The silver is gathered and piled up. It is almost done.

Then Njal adds one unnecessary thing: on top of the pile he lays a silk robe and a pair of boots. Flosi picks up the robe and asks who gave it. No one answers. He laughs: probably your father, that beardless old man; looking at him, plenty of people can't tell whether he's a man or a woman.

Payoff ② ↑It is exactly what Hallgerd spat out at that feast long ago. A quarrel between two housewives, decades old, picked up word for word by an enemy and thrown onto the most generous concession Njal ever made. Skarphedin answers at once: it isn't a woman's garment, it's a pair of breeches, and Flosi will need them, since he is the mistress of the troll of Svinafell, who uses him as a woman every ninth night. Flosi kicks the silver across the ground. He doesn't want money now. He wants blood.

The settlement is dead. Back at Bergthorshvol an old servant woman, Saeunn, whom no one takes seriously, keeps beating a heap of chickweed behind the house with a stick. Setup ⑥This pile will be used to light the fire that burns Njal and Bergthora, she says; burn it now. Nobody does.

Late that autumn Flosi arrives with more than a hundred men. Skarphedin wants to fight in the open. Njal orders everyone inside, remembering how much it cost forty men to take Gunnar in his house. Skarphedin knows his father is wrong and obeys anyway, saying in effect: my father is fey now, and if he wants us to burn indoors with him, I'm not afraid to die.

The house can't be stormed. Flosi says: if we leave, we are all dead men; there's only fire left, a great sin before God, for we are Christians ourselves (roughly). He chooses fire. What Gizur would not do to Gunnar even at the cost of his own life, Flosi does: the warrior of the old age stopped at that line; the newly baptized man crosses it.

Payoff ⑥ ↑At first the fire won't catch. Someone goes round behind the house, carries over the heap of chickweed, and sets it alight, and the flames pour down through the roof. Saeunn's ravings come true to the letter. The beauty of it lies in its clumsiness: the prophet is not Njal but an old servant nobody listens to, and Njal, the man who can see his own death coming, never thought to burn the heap.

Flosi goes to the door and invites the women, children and servants to come out, and they do. He invites Njal out: you don't deserve to burn. I am old, says Njal; I can't avenge my sons, and I won't live in shame. Flosi invites Bergthora out. I was given to Njal young, she says, and I promised him that we would share one fate. With their little grandson, Kari's boy, who refuses to leave, they lie down in bed and have the steward spread an ox-hide over them.

Skarphedin and Grim hold out among the collapsing beams. On the wall above, one of Flosi's men, Gunnar Lambason, leans down and jeers: are you crying? Skarphedin says no, takes something from his purse and throws it. It hits Lambason in the eye and knocks the eye out onto his cheek. Payoff ④ ↑It is Thrain's molar, the one he picked up on the frozen river in act three and has carried in his purse for years. Only now does the reader understand: this man always knew the account would come due someday, and he had his ammunition ready. The saga does not explain a word of it.

Helgi tries to slip out in women's clothes; Flosi recognizes him and cuts off his head. Kari climbs along a burning beam, his clothes alight, plunges through the smoke, rolls into a stream, and lives.

The next day, under the ox-hide, the three bodies are untouched by fire, and Njal's face, people said, looked brighter than in life. Skarphedin is found standing against the gable wall, his legs burned away below the knee, his axe driven deep into the wall, two crosses burned into his body, which people said he had made himself.

Act five · Law, the sword, and one embrace

What follows is a lawsuit on an island-wide scale. The prosecution's strategist is Thorhall, Njal's foster-son and best pupil in the law, who happens to be laid up in his booth with a monstrous boil on his leg and can only send advice by messenger. The defense bribes a lawyer skilled at procedural traps with a gold bracelet. The case goes all the way to the Fifth Court.

Payoff ⑤ ↑And in the Fifth Court the prosecution's case is thrown out over a procedural slip. Njal won that court for his foster-son Hoskuld; now the men who burned Njal walk free in it. In act three it was a loving father's gift; here it becomes a trap he built for himself. The saga makes no comment. It simply lets the two events meet in the same courtroom.

When the news reaches the booth, Thorhall grabs a spear and drives it into the boil on his leg; blood and pus spray out. He limps outside and strikes the first blow. The Althing becomes a battlefield.

After the fighting, an old chieftain, Hall of Sida, stands up. His own son has just been killed in the brawl. To make peace possible, he says, he will ask nothing for his son. He is the only person in the whole saga who voluntarily gives up what he is owed, and only then does the settlement hold. The burners are sentenced to exile.

Kari will not accept it. His little son, his parents-in-law and three brothers-in-law all died in that fire; he did not sign the settlement and is not bound by it. He hunts the burners one by one, as far as Orkney, where at the earl's feast Gunnar Lambason is telling the story of the burning with plenty of embroidery. Kari bursts into the hall and takes his head off with one stroke; it rolls onto the table.

A good many of the burners follow the Earl of Orkney to Ireland and die at the Battle of Clontarf in 1014. Flosi and Kari each make the journey to Rome, Flosi to be absolved, Kari on pilgrimage.

At last Kari sails for Iceland and is shipwrecked near Svinafell, Flosi's home. On a night of blizzard, soaked through, he walks straight into his enemy's house. Flosi rises from the high seat, goes to meet him, and embraces him. They are fully reconciled. Kari marries Hoskuld's widow, Hildigunn. The saga ends with a flat sentence: here ends the saga of Burnt Njal.

The fuse

The fuse of this chain is not the theft and not the axe. It is Njal's wisest act of kindness: adopting Hoskuld. He meant to stitch the feud shut with love, and instead placed his enemy's son at the very center of his own house. Mord's slander works because "he'll avenge his father someday" is not absurd at all: in that world, avenging your father is a man's plain duty. Njal's best move rested on the assumption that a boy would forget his own father.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion one · The horse stumbles

Gunnar has agreed to go. The narration moves fast and flat: riding, fording, heading for the ship. Then the horse stumbles, a meaningless little accident, and he jumps down, facing home. He looks up and sees the hillside, the fields, the meadows. He says that sentence, and then that he is going back.

Why does it hit so hard? First, the saga almost never tells you what anyone is thinking. It is all outside view: actions and speech. So when a warrior here says something, of all things, about beauty, it weighs ten times what it would anywhere else. Second, the causality runs backward. He didn't decide to stay and then look back; the horse turned him around, he saw, and then he decided. Third, what breaks Njal's rule isn't pride. It is a hillside: the gentlest reason in the book leads to the surest death.

Slow motion two · The blood falling out of the cloak

The shots in Hildigunn's scene: first the high seat, which Flosi won't take; then the cloak dropping onto his shoulders, the dried blood falling, sound and touch both present. Only then does she speak.

The force comes from an object speaking for a person. If she only wept, Flosi could soothe her and leave. But the cloak was his own gift, and the blood is falling on him; he can no longer treat this as someone else's business. "Cold are the counsels of women" is usually quoted as a misogynist maxim. Put back in the room, it sounds like a cornered man defending himself: he knows she is right, and he knows what saying yes will cost. That is why he tries the law first.

Slow motion three · Under the ox-hide

The fire is already burning, and the saga suddenly slows down for an exchange that is almost domestic. Come out, old man; he won't. Come out, old woman; she won't. The grandson won't leave his grandmother. Then one very concrete act: the steward spreads an ox-hide over them.

Why does it work? Because the most violent event in the book is written as its quietest scene. The roof is caving in, yet the camera stays on a bed, as if they were simply going to sleep. Bergthora's line pulls a political feud back to the plainest of marriage vows: they don't die of defeat; they choose not to be parted. The next day the bodies are unburned. It is almost a miracle, and the saga offers no comment, leaving it to the reader to decide whether this was God's doing.

The book's core device: a legal machine without a king

First, understand the stage. This was a real system in tenth-century Iceland (filtered through an author writing three centuries later):

① No king, no police, no prisons. Free farmers attached themselves to a chieftain of their choosing and could switch if unhappy. ② Each summer the Althing met for two weeks; the lawspeaker recited the law and the courts heard cases. ③ A verdict had two main outcomes: compensation, or outlawry. Lesser outlawry meant three years abroad; full outlawry meant anyone could kill you with impunity. ④ Who enforced verdicts? The winner, with his kin and friends.

The logic runs like this. Without central force, the only way to stop revenge is to convert it into money. For compensation to work, both sides must feel their honor is intact. So the crucial component isn't the statute; it is face: whether a man can accept payment without being thought a coward. That's why the saga's settlements collapse not over the amount but over a sentence: "dung-beards," "beardless," a joke about a troll. Money can be counted. Humiliation can't.

Which links hold? Compensation itself is a clever invention: it admits the urge for revenge and puts a price on it. Which are literary necessity? The saga times every failure for maximum drama and gives Njal near-perfect foresight, so the tragedy feels inevitable. Historians also warn that much of the legal procedure reflects the author's own century: Iceland's earliest history, the Book of the Icelanders (Íslendingabók), credits the founding of the Fifth Court to a different lawspeaker, not to Njal. This is fiction, not an archive, but its grasp of how a kingless society keeps the peace is sharper than most archives.

The other device is the style. Almost no psychology, no explanations, very few adjectives; the biggest events in the plainest words (a man run through by a halberd reports only that "the halberd is at home"). This coldness hands all judgment to the reader. You can only guess who someone is from what they do, as in life.

What it forces you to think about

One · Should Gunnar have turned back?

The verdict orders you away from your home for three years, and you think it unjust: everyone you killed came at you first. If you go, you accept the verdict. If you stay, anyone may kill you.

The case for going: law is useful precisely because you obey it when you disagree. Every strong man believes his own case is the exception, and once the strong start choosing which verdicts to honor, a system without police cannot survive a single day. By staying, Gunnar shows everyone who comes after that verdicts can be ignored. Three years is not long.

The case for staying, made as strongly as I can: a man's home is not an address he can leave for a while. It is everything that makes him who he is: land, name, kin, his place in that society. More deeply, he isn't defying the law; he is refusing to let the law define what is worth having. Better a short life here than a long one elsewhere. The law cannot prove that judgment wrong. It can only punish it.

Where does the saga stand? It never says he was wrong. It gives him a glorious death and has him singing in his grave; it also makes Njal's prophecy come true word for word. The author seems to believe two things at once: he was right, and he had to die for it. In your own life: have you ever accepted a ruling you thought unfair, a transfer, a decision, a family's division of property? When you walked away, were you upholding the rules or giving yourself up?

Two · Is a slap worth a life?

The case against her: Hallgerd refuses the hair. Whatever came before, this is life and death, and two locks of hair cost her nothing. And the slap came because she stole and burned a neighbor's storehouse. The fault was hers.

The case for her, made as strongly as I can: count from the beginning. Nobody asked her before her first marriage, and all three husbands struck her face. In a society where a woman cannot take up arms or plead in court, the only thing truly in her hands is "no." On the day of the slap she said, right then, that she would remember. That isn't revenge after the fact; it is a declaration made in advance. What she does is exactly what every man in that world does and is celebrated for: remember an insult, then settle the account at the moment it matters most. Gunnar doesn't ask twice. He understood the account.

Where does the author stand? The narrative lets her reputation sink to the bottom, yet it records all three slaps, every one, and makes no excuse for any of the men who struck her. In your own life: in a relationship, when someone said "I'll remember that," did you take it as mere temper? How long may a small humiliation wait, and when may it be brought out, before settling it becomes unfair?

Three · Should Kari have accepted the settlement?

Hall gives up his son's compensation and the whole island breathes again. Kari's little son and his wife's family died in that fire. He doesn't sign. He keeps killing.

The case for accepting: settlement is possible only because each side gives up some of what it is owed. Hall gave up a son. A society that gives victims a veto over peace will never have peace.

The case against, made as strongly as I can: others negotiated the settlement; it was his family that died. Whose peace is it, if the person who lost the most never agreed to it? Besides, the lawsuit was wrecked by a technicality, and in a world where the winner enforces his own verdict, Kari simply is the enforcer. Most important: in this saga only Kari's vengeance leads to a true reconciliation that money did not buy. He has killed enough, Flosi has atoned enough, and only then is the embrace possible.

The author seems to lean toward Kari; the ending rewards him. But the author also makes Hall the noblest man in the book. In our own world, plea bargains, civil settlements, a victim's family signing a letter of forgiveness all ask the same question: if someone else forgives on your behalf, is it forgiveness?

Motifs: what the Icelandic sagas keep writing about

This book has no author's name, so it can only be set back among the other sagas. Those equally anonymous works keep returning to the same obsessions: who has the last word, honor or law; how women exercise their will through men's hands; whether the new Christianity can tame the old ethic of revenge.

Laxdaela Saga shares characters with this one (Hrut appears there too), and its heroine Gudrun, married four times, leaves a line in old age that readers have turned over for centuries: she was worst to the one she loved most. In Egil's Saga, the warrior-poet loses a son to the sea and composes a lament: what is left when there is no one to take revenge on? Grettir's Saga follows a full outlaw who survives nearly twenty years in the wilderness, writing outlawry from the inside.

Njal's Saga is the summation: it puts a warrior, a sage and an avenger in one chain and lets three ways of living test the legal machine in turn. And there is another layer. The Sturlung civil wars included a famous house-burning of their own: at Flugumyri in 1253, the chieftain Gizur Thorvaldsson survived by hiding in a vat of whey while his wife and sons died in the fire. Many scholars think the burning in this book was written for readers who had lived through that era.

Controversy and criticism

History or fiction? For a long time it was read as half a chronicle, but it was written nearly three hundred years after the events, with heavy invention and reshaping; scholars have largely abandoned reading it as a source. It also means that the hillside, the cloak and the molar are mostly to the credit of one writer, not of oral record.

The women. The most common charge: two "inciters," Hallgerd and Hildigunn, carry almost all the moral blame, and Flosi's "cold are the counsels of women" is often lifted out as a misogynist proverb. Defenders point out that the saga lays out their situations plainly (marriages arranged for them, faces struck, no right to take revenge themselves), that the condemnation mostly comes from characters rather than the narrator, and that Bergthora is one of the noblest figures in the book. Both readings have textual support.

Structure. Many readers find Hrut's opening section, the foreign voyages and the long legal maneuvering of act five a slog, and the Clontarf episode looks grafted on; others answer that the digressions are how a saga paints a whole society.

A Christian cloak? Njal's glowing corpse, the burners' penance in Rome, the final embrace: some read these as a Christian frame laid over a pagan revenge story, making the ending too neat. Others think that is the author's point: the old ethic burned itself out so the new one could come in.

Translations. The first full English translation was George Webbe Dasent's The Story of Burnt Njal (1861), in a deliberately archaic register; later translations come closer to the original's hard, plain surface.

Crossovers

  • Anthropology · Gluckman, "The Peace in the Feud": Max Gluckman argued in 1955 that because people belong to several cross-cutting groups, any feud leaves someone with kin on both sides who must mediate. Gunnar and Njal are exactly that tie. Unlike Gluckman's case, here an adopted child turns the tie into a fuse.
  • Evolutionary game theory · the echo of tit-for-tat: In Axelrod's tournaments tit-for-tat did best, but add misunderstanding and two tit-for-tat players fall into an endless echo of retaliation; "generous tit-for-tat," which occasionally forgives unconditionally, breaks the loop. The wives' servant killings are a textbook echo; Hall waiving his son's price is the one generous move. The difference: in the model forgiveness is random, and Hall's is a choice.
  • Social psychology · Nisbett and Cohen, culture of honor: In their 1990s experiments, men raised in the American South reacted to a face-to-face insult with sharper stress-hormone and aggressive responses; the authors traced this to herding traditions, where livestock is easy to steal and the law is far away, so reputation is protection. Iceland was a herding island. Unlike the experiment, the saga follows one insult across thirty years.
  • Law and economics · David Friedman on medieval Iceland: In a 1979 paper Friedman treated Iceland as a case of privately enforced law: compensation claims could be sold to someone strong enough to collect, and the system worked for a long time. The saga reads like a book of counterexamples: each failure turns on honor, not incentives.

Ten lines to take with you

① A remark about "thief's eyes" on page one becomes, dozens of chapters later, a piece of stolen cheese.

② Two men keep a friendship alive with one purse of silver passed back and forth, and each pass buries someone.

③ Njal gives Gunnar two rules for staying alive; Gunnar breaks both, the second on a hillside.

④ The horse stumbles, he looks back at the slope: the tenderest sentence in a very hard book.

⑤ She said she would remember the slap; she did, when the bowstring broke.

⑥ A molar rides in a purse for years, and finally flies out of the fire.

⑦ Njal built a new court as a gift for his foster-son; the men who burned him walked free in it.

⑧ Money can be counted, humiliation can't: the settlement that mattered most broke on a joke about a beard.

⑨ Two old people under an ox-hide: one says "I am old," the other says "I promised him."

⑩ On a stormy night his enemy walks in, and Flosi stands and embraces him. It took the island fifty years and one great fire to learn that gesture.
