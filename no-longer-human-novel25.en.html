Novels, Deep-Read · Dazai Osamu · No.25

No Longer Human

Ningen Shikkaku · Dazai Osamu · serialized 1948

中文 →

Three photographs on a table. In the first, a boy of about ten stands by a garden pond, ringed by women, head tilted, grinning badly — until you notice that both of his hands are closed into fists. Nobody smiles with clenched fists.
In the second, the same person, now a student, is almost implausibly handsome; look twice and you find that the handsomeness carries none of the weight of a living body.
The third is the strangest. A corner of a filthy room, a small charcoal brazier, two hands held over it, the hair already going gray. This time he is not smiling. There is no expression at all. You finish looking, turn away, and cannot remember what the face looked like.

This page contains complete spoilers — who dies in each of his attempts on his own life, why two acquaintances deliver him to a mental hospital, what the bar madam says on the last page, and when the third photograph was taken. Everything that matters in this book is loaded into its final two pages; without them you can only discuss atmosphere. If you want the suspense, read the novel first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

A rich man's son who has never understood why human beings do anything talks his way into the human race by playing the clown, then slides down through drink, women, lies and drugs: in the double suicide it is the woman who dies and he who lives; at the end two acquaintances trick him into a mental hospital, and at twenty-seven, his hair white, he stamps himself disqualified — and yet the last sentence of the book is spoken by a woman who barely knew him, who says he was as good as an angel.

Where it sits

Dazai Osamu (1909–1948), born Tsushima Shūji, was the eleventh child of a great landowning family in Kanagi, Aomori Prefecture, in Japan's far north. Ningen Shikkaku was serialized in the magazine Tenbō in 1948; before the run was finished he drowned himself with Yamazaki Tomie in the Tamagawa Canal. The bodies were recovered on 19 June, which was his thirty-ninth birthday. The final pages of this book were printed after its author was dead.

Literary history files him with the postwar Buraiha — the "Decadent School," writers who met the ruins of 1945 by refusing every respectable posture and treating dissipation as a position. But the book's real coordinates lie on a different axis. Japanese fiction has a long confessional line called the I-novel (shishōsetsu), in which the author writes his own life directly onto the page and the reader is expected to be reading the author. This looks like the I-novel taken to its limit. In fact it plants a mine inside that tradition — which is the machine of the whole book, and gets taken apart in section 6.

It has long been among the best-selling novels in Japan, usually named alongside Natsume Sōseki's Kokoro, and generation after generation of seventeen-year-olds have recognized themselves in it. That is both its achievement and its largest controversy.

The story

Act one · Three photographs, and a child who does not understand people

The novel does not begin with its protagonist speaking. It begins with an unnamed "I" — a writer — holding three photographs of a stranger and looking through them, finding each one less right than the last. seed ①Who the man is, what became of him: not a word. Then the writer pushes three notebooks across the table. He wrote these.

The first line of the notebooks is the most famous sentence Dazai ever wrote:

"I have lived a life full of shame."— opening of the First Notebook; rendered literally here, English translations differ

The man writing is Ōba Yōzō, a child of a landowning family in the northeast. His problem is not misfortune; it is incomprehension. He does not know what hunger feels like. When the household sits down at the appointed hour, he sits down too, pushes food he does not want into his mouth, and privately wonders what the others are getting out of it. He looks at human beings and sees a species of unknown construction — a species that might at any moment notice that he is not one of them.

The method he invents is clowning (Japanese dōke): trousers deliberately worn backwards for the family's benefit, absurdities written into his school compositions so the teacher will read them aloud. He is precise about what this is. It is not a sense of humor. It is his last courtship of the human race: make you laugh first, and you will not come looking.

The day the trade opens is described exactly. His father, leaving for Tokyo, gathers the children and asks each what present they want. Yōzō cannot produce a syllable — not because he wants nothing, but because stating a wish freezes him solid. What if it is the wrong wish? seed ②That night, once the house is asleep, he creeps into the sitting room, finds his father's notebook and writes into it: lion-dance mask. His father had mentioned lion-dance masks in passing — Yōzō has guessed what his father wants to hear. The next day his father laughs about it in front of everyone: so the boy wanted the lion mask after all.

And one thing he records very lightly: the servants did things to him that should not be done to a child. He says only that he was taught sad things, that he was violated — and then that he told no one. His reason is not shame but despair: no one would believe him, and to open his mouth would be to admit he was a person to whom something had happened. The first silence in this book happens here. There will be one more, and it will cost far more.

Middle school, gym class, the horizontal bar. He has prepared a magnificent failure for the class: the run-up, the leap, the deliberate belly-flop into the sand pit, the shaking of sand out of his hair. The room roars. He is walking away when a finger prods him in the back. It is Takeichi — the weakest boy in the class, permanently running at the nose, pus leaking from one ear, beneath everyone's notice. He puts his mouth near Yōzō's ear and says, very quietly:

"You did that on purpose."— Takeichi, First Notebook (paraphrased; the Japanese is a single word, wazato)

Yōzō writes that the world went dark. seed ③The first thing he does afterwards is convert Takeichi into a friend: feeding him, squeezing the pus out of his ear, walking him home. This is not friendship, it is hush money. And Takeichi leaves two remarks in the house that Yōzō records as prophecies: first, women will fall for you; second, you will become a great painter. seed ④The second comes after Takeichi is shown the self-portraits Yōzō paints in secret — pictures in which he has drawn himself as a monster. Takeichi calls them ghost pictures.

Act two · Tokyo: Horiki, an illegal meeting, and a woman who dies in the sea

He passes into a school in Tokyo and almost immediately stops attending. At a painting studio he meets Horiki Masao, a slightly older art student — greasy, worldly, wholly without introspection — who teaches him drink, tobacco, brothels and the pawnshop, and who spends his money and uses his ear from beginning to end. And yet Horiki is the only person present for the entire book. Yōzō spends his life evading human beings; the one he cannot evade is a man he does not respect.

He also joins a Marxist reading group, running errands and printing leaflets with real diligence, for reasons that have nothing to do with belief: the circle is illegal. The lawful world keeps him rigid; the furtive air of the basement lets him breathe, because he has always privately considered himself a criminal, and among criminals he does not have to explain himself. One day he stops going, the way you drop a coat.

Then Tsuneko: a café hostess in Ginza whose husband is in prison for fraud. The two of them give off the same smell of poverty and exhaustion, and they spend a night together without settling anything. They know each other for a very short time — but it is one of the few stretches of his life in which he is not performing. Soon afterward the two of them walk into the sea at Kamakura.

She dies. He lives.

Then the police station, where he is held on suspicion of aiding a suicide. The real blade falls in the prosecutor's office. Under questioning he coughs twice — he has genuinely been coughing blood, the cough is real, but out of pure habit he makes it a little louder than it needs to be, to buy a little sympathy. The prosecutor looks up, smiles very slightly, and asks: really?

payoff ③ ↑That single word is worse than Takeichi's. When Takeichi said on purpose behind his back, Yōzō at least knew he was performing; this time he does not — the ingratiation has grown into his respiratory tract. Dazai sets these two exposures dozens of pages apart and gives not one word of prompting between them.

The family suppresses the matter and no charge is brought. He is handed over to a man called Flatfish — Shibuta, who deals antiques for his father and whose face genuinely resembles one. His family strikes his name from the register.

Act three · A kept man: Shizuko, Shigeko, and a prayer

The upstairs room at Flatfish's house is airless, and the atmosphere of living on someone's charity is rendered with unpleasant accuracy: Flatfish never says anything outright, only hints, over and over, that Yōzō ought to state what he intends to do with himself, circling and circling and waiting for him to speak first. Yōzō cannot. He could not name a present he wanted; he certainly cannot name a life. He leaves a note and runs to Horiki.

At Horiki's he meets Shizuko, a magazine journalist, a widow with a five-year-old daughter, Shigeko. He goes home with Shizuko that night and stays, keeping himself by drawing serial cartoons for popular magazines under a pen name — not his monster self-portraits, but cheap gags traded for drinking money.

payoff ② ↑Worth stopping here. This is the only work he can hold down for his whole life, and the trade opened one night when he was ten. Writing lion-dance mask in his father's notebook and handing an editor a strip of gags panel by panel are the same act: work out what the other party wants, then supply it without comment. Every other livelihood is beyond him; this one he has practiced for twenty years.

payoff ④ ↑By this act both of Takeichi's prophecies have come true, and each has come true with a blade in it. Women will fall for you — women do take him in, one after another, and each of them is dragged down for it. You will become a great painter — he has become a man who draws, and what he draws is gags to keep strangers cheerful. Takeichi was not wrong; it is only that every word he said grew crooked.

It is also in this period that Horiki lectures him, using the sentence everybody uses: the world will not allow it. Yōzō answers silently: the world? isn't the world just you? seed ⑤He does not say it aloud, but he reports that from then on he feared seken — "the world," society as a judging presence — a little less. Whoever invokes the world to hold him down turns out, each time, to be one person standing in front of him. It is one of the few things he ever genuinely works out.

Shigeko likes him and calls him father. One day he overhears the child telling her mother that she wants a real father, and praying to God for one. Standing outside the door, Yōzō understands something: he is not a father and never will be. He is not driven out; he discovers that he is in the way. He leaves, goes to a small bar in Kyōbashi, and the madam puts him upstairs.

Act four · Yoshiko: the one time he nearly becomes a person

Opposite the bar is a tobacco shop, and behind the counter is a girl of seventeen or eighteen named Yoshiko. Her distinguishing quality is not beauty. It is that she trusts people — without reserve, everyone, first. This is not naivety; naivety is inexperience, and what she has is a capacity, an ability to put her whole guard down. The first thing of consequence she says to Yōzō is that he should stop drinking. seed ⑥

They marry. He genuinely stops drinking; he draws in the daytime and eats dinner with Yoshiko at night. It is the only lit passage in the book and it is as short as one breath — he writes that during it he even began to think he might turn into a human being. Dazai drops in a very quiet line here, to the effect that a coward is frightened even of happiness, and can be injured by cotton wool. He knew, writing that sentence, what was coming.

One summer evening Horiki comes over. Upstairs the two of them start a word game: first comedy nouns and tragedy nouns, then antonyms — the opposite of flower, the opposite of woman — and then they reach crime. Horiki answers instantly: punishment. Yōzō shakes his head and keeps going. Perhaps the opposite of crime is goodness; perhaps God; perhaps the law; perhaps — honey? He drifts further and further out, feeling that he is about to touch something remarkable.

And at that moment it happens. In the room below, a small tradesman who comes and goes from the house is raping Yoshiko. He sees it through a gap.

He does not shout. He does not go down. He backs away, and only comes downstairs some time later.

payoff ⑥ ↑What follows is the book's most famous passage and the one most worth turning over. What destroys him is not that his wife has been violated — he writes that he does not even experience it as betrayal. What destroys him is the discovery that a spotless capacity for trust can be used like that. And that capacity was the entire reason he went near her: what he loved in Yoshiko was that she was undefended, and being undefended is the wound itself. The thing he loved and the thing that cut her open are one thing. Dazai gives no cushion. He simply lets Yōzō ask, inwardly: is trust a sin? No one answers.

One note is required here, because every criticism this novel has ever attracted starts from it: he stood on the stairs, saw it, and did not move.

Yoshiko changes after that; she flinches at people. He starts drinking again. One night he finds the box of sleeping powder she has hidden, swallows all of it, sleeps for three days and wakes up.

Act five · Morphine, the asylum, and a shack in the north

He coughs blood. At the pharmacy the proprietress — a disabled woman on a crutch — gives him not alcohol but morphine. With the first injection, something that has been held rigid in company for thirty years lets go; by the next day he needs more. For the drug he draws faster, borrows more, tells larger lies, and finally stops caring how he looks doing it.

At last he writes to Flatfish for help. What arrives is Flatfish and Horiki, with Yoshiko. They say they are taking him somewhere to recover. The car drives for a long time. Only on arrival does he understand: this is a mental hospital. Barred windows, a lock, a man in white leading him in.

payoff ⑤ ↑Read this against the argument in act three. His silent retort to Horiki — isn't the world just you? — was the single sharpest judgment he ever made, and here it comes true to the letter: what puts him behind the bars is not an abstract "world," it is exactly these two specific men, an acquaintance and a bookkeeper. He was right, and then he was locked up by the thing he was right about.

Inside, he understands the thing that becomes the book's title: once you are in here you are not a human being, you are a lunatic. Human being: disqualified.

Three months later he is released. His father has died of a gastric ulcer in a Tokyo hospital. He notices something that surprises even him: with his father dead, the vessel inside him that had held all his fear and unease is suddenly empty, and even his suffering has gone unreal. The fear of his entire life turns out to have been aimed, for the most part, at one man; with the man gone, the fear has nowhere to land. His elder brother takes him north and installs him in an old thatched house with an aging servant and a small allowance. The last paragraph of the notebooks: he is twenty-seven this year, and because of all the white hair, everyone who sees him takes him for over forty.

Then the camera cuts back to the writer. He has these three notebooks and three photographs from the madam of that small bar in Kyōbashi, and he asks her, in passing, whether she knew the man. Her answer is the last sentence of the book, and the only time anyone speaks in Yōzō's defense: the Yō-chan we knew was honest and considerate, and if only he hadn't drunk — no, even when he drank — he was as good as an angel.

payoff ① ↑Now go back to the three photographs on the first page. The third one — white hair, the corner of a room, a brazier, a face that leaves no impression — is almost beyond doubt the man after they sent him north. Dazai has built the book as a ring: you believe you are reading a confession, but what you have been doing, from first page to last, is assembling a person to fit a face you cannot remember.

Put the five acts together and there is no event here of the kind that makes a plot — nothing you can point to and say without this, none of it. The cold father, the servants, Tsuneko's death, the afternoon of Yoshiko: each is brutal enough, and none is the fuse. The fuse was lit much earlier, on a night when a ten-year-old felt his way into a dark room, opened his father's notebook and wrote lion-dance mask. That night he made a decision: since I do not understand what people want, I will guess; and if I guess right and supply it, I will not be sent away. Everything after — the clowning, the drink, the women, the ingratiating cartoons, the double suicide, the morphine, the asylum — is that one decision being honored in steadily harder circumstances, until the day comes when he can no longer guess. He was not broken by life. He used up the method he chose when he was ten.

Three slow-motion shots

1 · The three photographs: why the book opens at a viewing

Dazai does not let his protagonist speak first. He makes you serve as coroner. Before you have read a single sentence of Yōzō's, you have performed an external examination on the man, and the finding is: something is wrong here.

The move accomplishes three things. First, conclusion before process — the book is therefore not a suspense story but the supporting notes to a post-mortem. From page one you know the man is finished; you only don't know how. Second, three photographs are three ages, which draws the descending curve of a life in advance, so that every short stretch of good weather inside the notebooks is read with a clock running under it.

Third and most important: the third face leaves no trace. This is a genuinely abnormal thing to describe. A photograph can be ugly, or cruel, or pitiable — but "you cannot remember it afterwards" is barely a visual experience at all; it is a psychological one. Dazai spends an entire book explaining that one clause, and the answer only assembles itself on the final page: no expression ever crossed that face that had not been performed. A person can groom himself into being unmemorable, which is a great deal worse than being ugly.

2 · "On purpose" and "really?": the same slap, delivered twice

Dazai gives the most frightening blow in the novel to the least powerful person in it. Takeichi is the boy nobody would bother with — the nose, the ear, the general invisibility — and it is precisely he who lays one finger on Yōzō's back and, in three words, dismantles an engineering project of more than a decade.

There is a technical choice here that goes largely unnoticed: the man who sees through him is not an authority. Had a teacher or his father said it, the moment would still belong to oppression, and Yōzō could hate it or hide from it. Because Takeichi says it, the trick is revealed to be capable of failing in front of anyone at all. From this moment he must defend himself against the entire world.

The second time comes dozens of pages later. The prosecutor's soft "really?" is the same event, but heavier: the first time he knew he was performing, this time he does not. And Dazai gives no signal at all — he does not write he suddenly remembered Takeichi. An essay has to finish its sentences; a novel leaves the sentence to be finished inside the reader. You feel something drop at the second exposure and cannot say why, which is exactly how it works.

3 · The antonym game, interrupted: comic timing used to cut a man open

The lowest emotional point in the novel is written in its lightest register. Two men lie about upstairs playing a word game, back and forth, with laughs in it, in something close to the rhythm of a comedy double act. Dostoevsky put crime and punishment side by side in a title — is that little conjunction a pairing or an opposition? Yōzō is drifting toward something, and at that exact moment Dazai cuts the shot downstairs. No transition. No preparation.

The drop is the whole force of the passage: upstairs, a man thinking abstractly about crime; downstairs, a crime in progress against his wife, which he sees and does not move for. The definition of a word and the object the word refers to have been placed on two floors of the same house.

It is the best piece of structural design in the novel and also its most merciless self-indictment: if Yōzō was ever going to be saved, the opening lasted a few seconds — and he spent those seconds on an abstract question. Dazai adds no comment. He does not need one; stacking the two rooms has already written it.

The machine: a confession you are not supposed to fully believe

No Longer Human looks like a confession. It is in fact a confession somebody found — and that difference is where the whole apparatus lives.

The structure: an outer layer in which an unnamed writer holds three photographs and three notebooks, and an inner layer that is Ōba Yōzō's first person. The author is not Yōzō, and Yōzō is not Dazai. And the frame does something no I-novel does: it disagrees with what it contains.

Walk the logic through one step at a time:

  • Premise: from the age of ten, Yōzō has lived by deceiving everyone, and he is extremely good at it — thirty years good.
  • Inference: then why should this document be the exception? Would a man who has performed his entire life not also be performing when he writes his confession? "I am the worst of men" can itself be a courtship — with morality, and with the reader, as the object.
  • The part that holds: this suspicion is not imposed by a clever reader; it is installed by the text. Dazai deliberately places a witness outside everything, and her testimony contradicts the confession head-on. He wants you to doubt.
  • The part to be careful with: you cannot simply invert it and conclude that Yōzō is a liar and the madam is right. She only ever saw the man in a bar. Her account cannot cover Tsuneko, Shizuko or Yoshiko — and none of those three left any testimony at all. The design is not asking you to pick a truth. It is sending you away holding two accounts that cannot both be right.

There is a further mechanism inside the title. Japanese shikkaku is the vocabulary of officials and referees: disqualification, the striking of a name from a list. Somebody blows a whistle, raises a card, declares you out. The absurdity of this book is that the man blowing the whistle and the man sent off are the same man. No institution ever formally rules that Ōba Yōzō is not a human being; he cuts the stamp himself, presses it himself, and then spends a life proving the stamp was correct.

One honest label: this is a literary device, not a psychological finding. Generalizing "a confession cannot be fully believed" into "all accounts of suffering are suspect" converts a novel into a weapon. Dazai doubts his own document, not other people's.

What it forces you to think about

QUESTION ONE
A lifetime of playing the clown: is that deception, or an extreme form of consideration?

Take the "consideration" side to its strongest. Yōzō's clowning has a cost, and he pays all of it himself: the fear, the disgust and the exhaustion are swallowed, and what he serves up is only what is pleasant to receive. The person in your life who keeps every gathering afloat, who never becomes anyone's problem, who covers the awkward gaps — is doing the same work. Calling it "deception" is cheap; what exactly did the deceived party lose? They got a pleasant afternoon and a dinner with no silences in it. If the price of honesty is that everyone present has a worse evening, why should honesty automatically be the virtue?

Now take the other side to its strongest. The damage from clowning does not fall on the audience; it falls on the performer, and the side effect is fatal: it makes being loved impossible. No one can love a person who has never appeared. Yōzō is liked, over and over — by Tsuneko, Shizuko, Yoshiko, the madam — and what each of them likes is the article he supplied. So he spends his life inside the human crowd and his life convinced he is outside it, and that is not fate's irony; it is a result he personally guaranteed. It isn't that he was never loved. It is that he voided every instance of it in advance.

This dilemma turns up at every fork where you could say the true thing. There is no general solution, but there is a question you can put to yourself: when I swallowed that, was I sparing the other person trouble, or sparing myself risk? The difficulty is that in the moment the two feel identical.

QUESTION TWO
"The world won't allow it" — does the world exist?

Take Yōzō's side to its strongest. Seken — "the world" — is a plaintiff that never appears in court. Nobody has met it, and yet anybody may represent it: people will talk, that's just how it's seen, it wouldn't look good. What the speaker is actually doing is borrowing an imaginary majority to put leverage behind one person's opinion. Seeing through it is enormously freeing: there is one man in front of you, and his opinion is one man's opinion. Whole lives are held in place by "what will people think," where the people have no names.

Now the reverse, also at its strongest. The world is not fictional; it simply does not live inside any single person. It is the resultant of countless individual acts — rents, reputations, who gets hired, how fast someone is frozen out. You can out-argue the man in front of you. You cannot out-argue ten thousand people independently choosing the same way. And Yōzō's own end proves it: formally, two specific men put him in the asylum, but what made that seem reasonable and left nobody accountable for it was an entire settled consensus that people like him belong behind a lock. He broke the rhetoric of the world. He never touched its mechanics.

Applied to your own life, "what will people think" deserves splitting in two. Does this "people" have a name? If it does, it is probably one person's opinion and you can go and talk to them. If it does not, then either it does not exist, or it is harder to fight than any individual could be. The worst case is that it is both: nameless when it is spoken, and heavy when it lands.

QUESTION THREE
Is a spotless capacity for trust a sin — and what do we call the man on the stairs?

First, grant the weight of Yōzō's question. Yoshiko's trust is not childishness; it is a capacity for being undefended, and it is why she is cut. The world does reward guardedness and punish openness: a person who never suspects anyone is, as a matter of plain statistics, more likely to be cheated, used and assaulted. Teaching a child to trust people is, in one sense, raising their probability of being hurt — and we teach it anyway. There is no exit from that paradox, and Dazai compresses it into a single question.

But the question is also wrong, and beautifully wrong, which is why it should be turned over. Hanging responsibility for an injury on a quality of the injured party, however elegantly phrased, quietly walks the perpetrator out of the frame. The person who raped Yoshiko was the tradesman, not Yoshiko's trust. This passage contains the best prose in the book, and it is precisely because it is so good that it needs inspecting: asking "is a spotless trusting heart a sin?" performs a concealed operation. It promotes a specific rape into a philosophical proposition about purity — and at the moment of promotion, the victim stops being a person and becomes a symbol. Nowhere in the book does it say what Yoshiko felt, or what she thought afterwards.

Then the harder question: he saw it, and backed away. Throughout the book he accuses himself of ruining people — but what he accuses is always the quality, cowardice. A quality is a temperament, a fate, something one cannot help. "That day I did not go downstairs" is a specific inaction at a specific moment, with a time, a place, and a person being harmed in it. Writing a single failure to act as a lifelong disposition is the most accomplished and the most suspect piece of craft in this novel.

Everyone has stood on those stairs: the joke in the group chat that went too far, the colleague being worked over in a meeting, the family fact everybody knows and no one raises. Afterwards our account of ourselves is almost always the temperament version — I'm not good at confrontation. That is usually true. It is not a free pass.

QUESTION FOUR
Does this book rescue people, or pin them in place?

Take the rescue first. The hardest part of suffering is often not the suffering but the belief that you are the only one. What this book did was give a name to a feeling that had never had one — the sense of having to perform continuously in company, of being about to be found out, of not being the same species as everyone else in the room. Seeing it set down, word by word, tells you that you did not invent the illness. And Dazai does not console, does not offer a way out, does not say it gets better; because he does not console, readers believe him. That may be the most concrete thing literature can hand anyone: you are not the only monster.

Now the other side, also at its strongest. What makes the book dangerous is exactly what makes it effective: it fits self-pity with a very beautiful vocabulary, beautiful enough that people are reluctant to put it down. Once "I'm one of the disqualified" becomes an identity, it is simultaneously an exemption — no need to go to work, to apologize, to repair anything, because the disqualified cannot do those things. And Dazai himself died inside that vocabulary: the last installment went to the magazine and the man went into the water. The critics are not being cruel when they say that a book which makes sinking look poetic can be an accelerant for someone already going down.

There is a less comfortable answer in between: at seventeen and at twenty-seven this is not the same book. At seventeen you read it and feel understood. At twenty-seven you ought to be frightened — frightened by the fact that Yōzō never once reaches for anything, that he spends the entire novel waiting to be taken in, and that everyone who takes him in is damaged by it.

Which is why the last line is what you have to look at. If all you leave with is "he understands me," you have not finished the book. Finishing it means reaching the final page and finding a woman still speaking for him — a man who sentenced himself to be non-human had a place kept for him among other people the whole time, and simply never dared to go and look. That is not consolation. It is a harder bill: the possibility of being understood was always there, and it required him to show up once.

The recurring obsession

Line his books up and the same fixation surfaces in all of them: what do I look like in someone else's eyes. Not the metaphysical version, not who am I, but the constantly-calculating one — am I presentable at this instant, have I been seen through. His characters are nearly all doing one job: managing their own image, and dying of the effort.

The Setting Sun (1947) follows a collapsing aristocratic household: the daughter, Kazuko, decides to live; the brother, Naoji, does not. Naoji's suicide note is another draft of Yōzō — a man who can neither return to his class nor pass as one of the people, explaining in the most sincere possible tone why he has to disappear.

Villon's Wife (1947) is the best antidote to No Longer Human: the same species of man, written from the wife's eyes. The husband is a poet who steals; the wife goes to work at the very bar he stole from, to pay it back, and ends up tougher and more alive than anyone else in the story. That story proves something important — Dazai was entirely capable of writing how a woman sees a man like this. Which makes Yoshiko's silence in No Longer Human a choice, not a limit.

Run, Melos! (1940) is the same obsession with the sign reversed: a man runs a full day to get back and save the friend standing hostage for him. The subject is trust honored. One book about how warm it is when trust holds, another about how cold it is when trust is betrayed, and the same author standing between them.

Two more belong here because they correct a popular misreading. Tsugaru (1944) is an account of a journey back to his home region, ending with a visit to the woman who nursed him as a child — a rare stretch of undefended warmth in his work. Otogizōshi (1945) retells Japanese folk tales, written through air-raid warnings, and is very funny. The man who wrote No Longer Human wrote these too; reading Dazai as someone who could only wail is reading him wrong.

Criticism, honestly

  • The most quoted line is not in this book. In the Chinese-reading world especially, a sentence usually rendered "I am sorry to have been born" is welded to this novel. It is not from it: it belongs to The Flag-Bearer of the Twentieth Century (1937), where it appears as a subtitle, and Japanese sources generally hold that Dazai took the phrase from a friend. Its migration onto this book's cover owes a great deal to film and to the internet. A line that is not in a book becoming the book's signature is itself now part of the book's reception.
  • Narcissism and self-pity. The strongest charge is that it aestheticizes weakness. Yōzō never does one inconvenient thing for another person; the pain is entirely inward-facing, and it is written very beautifully. Mishima Yukio is this camp's most famous enemy: he said openly that he disliked Dazai's literature and that a good half of that particular illness could be cured by cold rubdowns, gymnastics and regular hours. By Mishima's own later account he said "I dislike your literature" to Dazai's face, and Dazai answered, in substance: and yet you came here, so you must like it after all.
  • The women. Tsuneko dies and her death is written as Yōzō's trauma; Shizuko and Shigeko are used and left; Yoshiko is raped and the passage is weighted toward the collapse of Yōzō's worldview. The women in this book each have a way of living, and almost none of them has an interior. The defense is that this is first person and Yōzō cannot see other people. The defense holds — and explains the cause without cancelling the consequence.
  • The biographical trap. Yōzō is not Dazai. Dazai was an extremely industrious writer with more than two hundred pieces across a decade and a half, ranging from folk-tale retellings to light satire. But the book itself issues the invitation: the double suicide at Kamakura, the drug dependence, the psychiatric ward all have counterparts in his life, and he died before the serialization finished. That invitation is very hard to refuse — and precisely because it is hard to refuse, some part of this book's standing does not belong to literature. It appeared around his suicide and has never been separable from it since: the novel we read today is a book rewritten by its author's ending.
  • The title in translation. Donald Keene's 1958 English version fixed the title as No Longer Human, which turns a ruling into a description of a state — gentler in tone, and heavier. The Japanese reads far more like a disqualification notice pinned to a name.

Ten sentences to take away

1 · The book opens on three photographs and closes on someone else's testimony; the confession in between is pinned between two outsiders.

2 · The fuse is not any of the disasters. It is a ten-year-old writing lion-dance mask in his father's notebook, deciding to live by guessing what people want to hear.

3 · The person who sees through him is the weakest boy in the class, not a teacher and not his father — which is why, afterwards, he must guard against everyone.

4 · "On purpose" and "really?" are one slap delivered twice. The second hurts more, because that time he did not know he was performing.

5 · Upstairs he is asking what the opposite of crime is; downstairs a crime is happening. The best structure in the book and its harshest self-indictment.

6 · "Is a spotless trusting heart a sin?" is a beautiful question — beautiful enough to convert a specific rape into an abstract proposition.

7 · "Isn't the world just you?" is clever, but he only cracked the rhetoric of the world; what finally locked him up was those two specific men.

8 · Disqualified is a referee's word. The absurdity here is that the man blowing the whistle and the man sent off are the same person.

9 · At seventeen the book makes you feel understood. At twenty-seven it should frighten you: Yōzō never reaches for anything; he waits to be taken in.

10 · The last sentence is not in the notebooks, it is on the frame: a woman says he was as good as an angel. He spent a life proving he was unfit to be human, and someone always disagreed — he simply never dared to ask.
