The Old Man and the Sea

The Old Man and the Sea · Ernest Hemingway · 1952

On the third day, out on the water, the fish finally begins to circle. The old man braces the line across his back and takes in a few inches with every turn, until the old scars on his hands split open and blood runs down the cord.
The fish is two feet longer than his boat. Its broad stripes show lavender under the surface. It does not look like prey. It looks like another vessel.
He lifts the harpoon over his head and drives it down with everything he has left.
That is the moment he wins. Over the next dozen hours the sea will eat that victory, mouthful by mouthful, down to a white spine.

In one sentence

An old Cuban fisherman who has gone eighty-four days without a catch and is written off by the village as terminally unlucky rows out alone past where anyone else goes, hooks a marlin of well over a thousand pounds, holds it for two days and two nights, kills it on the third morning and lashes it alongside his skiff — and then, over the dozen hours of the trip home, has every pound of it stripped off by sharks, arriving with an eighteen-foot skeleton, carrying his mast up the hill to his shack, falling asleep face down, and dreaming of the lions he saw on an African beach when he was young.

Coordinates

Ernest Hemingway (1899–1961) rewrote the sentence in English. He came out of newspapers, wrote short, wrote actions and dialogue, and stripped out nearly all the adjectives and nearly all the explaining of interior states. In Death in the Afternoon (1932) he put his working principle plainly: an iceberg moves with dignity because only an eighth of it shows — if a writer genuinely knows what he is leaving out, the omission makes the reader feel that part is there. Later readers named this the iceberg theory, or the theory of omission.

The Old Man and the Sea is that method pushed to its limit, and the last book-length work published in his lifetime — about twenty-seven thousand words, thin as a long story. The timing matters: his previous novel, Across the River and into the Trees (1950), had been widely panned, and a good many people had concluded that Hemingway was finished. In September 1952 Life magazine ran the whole story in a single issue, which by contemporary accounts sold millions of copies within days; the Scribner's book edition followed the same month. It took the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction in 1953, and in 1954 Hemingway received the Nobel Prize in Literature, the Swedish Academy's citation praising his mastery of narrative and singling out this recent work.

Its place in literary history is therefore slightly odd: a small book used to rehabilitate a major writer, which has since become his most widely read and his most professionally suspected. Its core did not come from nowhere — in a 1936 Esquire piece about the Gulf Stream, Hemingway recorded an anecdote: an old fisherman alone in a boat hooked a great marlin, was towed far out, and when he was found two days later the sharks had eaten the fish and the old man was crying.

The story

Act I · Day eighty-four: a man officially declared unlucky

Cuba, a fishing village near Havana. The old man is Santiago, and he fishes alone in a skiff in the Gulf Stream. He has taken nothing for eighty-four days. His face and hands carry the brown blotches the tropical sun raises off the water, and his hands are scored with the deep creases that heavy line cuts over years. Everything about him is old except the eyes, which are the color of the sea, cheerful and undefeated.

His sail is patched with flour sacks, and furled around the mast it looks like the flag of permanent defeat. Setup ①That is not the old man's phrase about himself. It is the verdict the novel hangs over him on its first page.

For the first forty of those days a boy, Manolin, went with him. After forty, the boy's parents said the old man was now definitively salao — the worst form of unlucky — and put him in another boat, which took three good fish in its first week. Setup ②

The boy has not really left, though. He cadges food for the old man at the Terrace, brings him yesterday's newspaper, and comes up to the shack in the evening to talk. The shack holds a bed, a table, a chair, a charcoal fire on the dirt floor, and two colored religious pictures left by his dead wife. Her photograph he has put away on a shelf in the corner; hanging on the wall it made him feel too lonely.

Almost all their talk is baseball. They talk about the Yankees and about the great DiMaggio — whose father, the old man notes, was a fisherman — and they come back repeatedly to the bone spur in DiMaggio's heel: a thing that must hurt terribly, and the man plays anyway, and plays that well. Setup ③The old man also tells about a day in his youth on the docks at Casablanca, hand-wrestling the strongest man there: they started one morning and finished the next, a full day and night, referees changing shifts, and he won, and afterwards they called him The Champion.

The boy asks where he will go tomorrow. Far out, the old man says, and back when the wind shifts; he wants to be gone before light. That night he does not dream of storms, or of women, or of great occurrences, or of great fish, or of his wife. He dreams of places, and of lions on a beach — the ones he saw at evening from a ship off Africa as a boy, playing in the sand like young cats. Setup ④

Act II · Day eighty-five, rowing out: the strike at noon

It is still dark when the boy wakes him and they drink coffee. He rows out of the harbor, hearing other boats scatter across the water, and keeps going until the land is gone, out to the deep place he thinks of as the great well, where the bottom drops away sharply and the current strikes the wall and every kind of fish collects.

He puts four lines down at four depths, each hook sheathed so completely in fresh sardines that even the point is buried in fish. The thing he is proud of is that his lines hang straight: other men let their baits drift; his bait waits exactly where he wants it. I have no luck any more, he thinks. But I am precise.

In the morning he takes a ten-pound albacore, clubs it and drops it in the shade of the stern. Setup ⑤Near noon the green stick on his deepest line dips, and dips again — something below is testing with great care. The old man holds absolutely still and reads the weight through his fingers: very light, and very large. He feeds line, lets the fish take it, waits until the whole sardine-covered shank has gone down, and then sets the hook with both hands and his whole back.

Nothing happens. Or rather, what happens is this: the boat starts moving out to sea. The fish does not jump and does not thrash. It simply tows the skiff, steadily, not fast, with a strength that is not open to argument. The old man brings the line across his shoulders like a harness and takes the pull with his body — the line cannot be made fast to the boat, because one surge would part it. So the brake has to be a man's back. The sun goes down and the green line of the coast is long gone. Hold on, he tells himself, and we will see who lasts.

Act III · Two days and two nights: two opponents on one line

Time now compresses into a taut cord. He cannot sleep and cannot let go. The only food aboard is a bottle of water, that small tuna, and later a dolphin — the fish, not the mammal — with two flying fish in its belly.

The first night his left hand cramps into a claw he cannot open. He calls it a traitor, tells it to go ahead and turn into a claw if it likes, it will do it no good. A cramp needs food, so with his right hand and his teeth he cuts the albacore — the one he had meant for bait — into strips and forces them down. Raw, no lime, no salt, and it makes him want to be sick. Payoff ⑤ ↑It is the thriftiest and hardest stroke in the book: in Act II that fish is tossed in the stern and the reader files it as spare bait, and here it becomes the only food there is. The bait saves the man who set it — the novel never says that sentence. It just puts the strips in his mouth.

After sunrise on the second day the fish comes up for the first time. It clears the water and he sees all of it: two feet longer than the skiff, a bill like a baseball bat, the broad stripes on its flank pale purple in the light. He runs the weight in his head: over fifteen hundred pounds. He starts talking to it, calls it brother, says he has never seen anything greater or more beautiful or calmer or more noble — and then says that he will kill it before this day is over. Those two statements do not contradict each other in him. What he has done all his life is love a thing and kill it properly.

A small warbler comes down from the north and rests on the line. He talks to it, tells it to take a good rest and then go in and take its chances like anyone else. It flies off. The episode runs under three pages and is the only time another living thing shares that cord with him.

Both his hands are cut open now. He soaks them over the side and watches the blood thin out in the water, and then he thinks about DiMaggio: that man plays with a bone spur in his heel, so could I stay with this fish with hands like these? Payoff ③ ↑In Act I the baseball talk looks like an old man and a boy filling an evening. Out here it shows what it was for — the old man has nobody to measure himself against, so he has to build a reference point, and the reference point has to be someone working hurt. The beauty of the move is its plainness: the novel never gives him a maxim, it just lets him remember a ballplayer's heel.

On the second night, when he is at the end of himself, he remembers Casablanca: if he could win then, he can hold now. Fish, he tells himself, I'll stay with you until I am dead.

Act IV · Day three: the harpoon, and a victory too big for the boat

On the third morning the fish begins to circle. That is the sign it is spent, and it is the most dangerous phase: each turn he gains a little line, and each time the fish lunges the line cuts him again. He is dizzy and there are black spots in his vision. You can kill yourself doing it, he tells himself, but you are going to bring him in.

The circles tighten. The fish comes up alongside, so long he cannot take it in at once. He drops the line, raises the harpoon in both hands and puts his whole weight behind it, driving it in behind the great pectoral fin. The fish comes up alive out of the water, its full length in the air, and falls back, and the wave it throws comes into the boat.

It is too big to bring aboard. He lashes it along the outside of the skiff, bow to bow and stern to stern, like tying a second vessel to his own, and raises the patched sail for home. For the moment he is at peace: fifteen hundred pounds at even thirty cents a pound will keep him and the boy for a long time.

He gets one hour. The novel allows him exactly one hour of having won.

Act V · The trip home: the weapons go down one rung at a time

The first shark comes up the blood trail — a mako, which he calls dentuso, with teeth like a man's fingers, fast and beautiful and afraid of nothing. It takes about forty pounds in one pass. The old man drives the harpoon into its brain and it sinks — and takes the harpoon and the rope down with it.

From here the rest of the book is a subtraction. The blood is in the water, every shark within reach knows there is a dead marlin out here, and each wave leaves the old man holding one thing less.

By the second wave the harpoon is gone, so he lashes his knife to an oar and makes a lance of it. He kills both of those and the single one that follows — at the cost of the blade snapping off against bone. Two more come before sunset and he beats them away with a short club. At midnight the pack arrives together and he swings the tiller in the dark at things he can only locate by sound, and when the tiller breaks he goes on swinging the stub. At one point something gives in his chest and he tastes copper.

Then he stops, because there is nothing left to fight for: the sharks are worrying scraps off the bones and no longer coming at him. He tries to straighten up the boat and finds that it is light — light as an empty boat, because what is lashed outside it now is a spine, a head and a tail.

He tells the fish he is sorry. And he settles his own account: I went out too far.

"I went out too far."— Santiago, repeatedly, on the way home

And before the first shark he had already said the line this book is most quoted for:

"But man is not made for defeat. A man can be destroyed but not defeated."— Santiago, alone in the skiff

Act VI · Ashore: an eighteen-foot skeleton and a tourist's question

He makes the harbor in the small hours. Everyone is asleep; nobody meets him. He ties up the skiff, shoulders the mast with the patched sail still furled around it, and starts up the road to his shack. Payoff ① ↑The flag of permanent defeat from page one is finally cashed, not as a phrase but as an action: he carries that flag home. The novel does not convert it into a flag of victory and does not add a word of commentary. It simply makes an old man under a heavy load sit down five times on the hill, count what is left of the climb, and stand up again. At the top he falls face down on the newspapers with his arms straight out and the palms up, and sleeps.

In the morning the boy comes, sees his hands, and goes out crying. He brings coffee and says: they did not beat you. Not the fish. No, the old man says — it was afterwards. The boy says he is coming out with him again, that he still has everything to learn. Payoff ② ↑In Act I the boy was removed by his parents on the grounds that the old man was salao; here he tears that verdict up himself. He does not argue about luck. He just decides it no longer governs him. The move is fine because it refuses to be moving: the closest thing to a victory in this book is a child saying he will be back tomorrow.

On the beach the fishermen measure the skeleton: eighteen feet from nose to tail.

That afternoon tourists come to the Terrace. A woman looks down at the long white spine and the great upright tail in the water and asks what it is. The waiter tries, with the English he has, to say shark, and to explain that sharks did it. She says she had no idea sharks had such beautiful tails. Her companion says he had no idea either.

Up the hill, the boy sits by the bed. The old man is asleep. The last line of the book is:

"The old man was dreaming about the lions."— the final sentence

Payoff ④ ↑In Act I that dream was given as an aside: an old man who no longer dreams of storms or women or great fish, only of places and of lions playing on an African beach. After eighty-odd hours of struggle the sentence comes back unaltered, with nothing added. The beauty is that the absence of change is the whole of the information: this man has been emptied out, his hands are ruined, the fish is gone, and what is in his sleep has not moved an inch. The sea took everything it could reach and never touched that.

The fuse, in one paragraph: the thing that sets this story off is not the fish, it is that he rowed to where nobody else goes. Eighty-four empty days made him need to prove something, so he went far out; going that far out is what put him in reach of a fish that size; and going that far out is also why he could not get it home — every minute of blood spreading in warm water is interest being charged on the extra miles. The reason he wins the fish and the reason he loses it are the same reason. The novel says this very quietly: what he repeats to himself is "I went out too far," not that the sharks were monstrous.

Three slow-motion passes

1 · The strike at noon: the biggest event in the book written as procedure

The fish taking the bait is the only turning point this novel has. How does Hemingway handle it? Not with the old man's elation, not with fate — with fingers. The line dips, and dips again; he judges that a big fish is moving off sideways with the sardines; he feeds line; he calculates how long the fish needs to swallow the shank; and then he pulls with both hands and his back.

The point of view is welded to two hands from beginning to end — everything the reader knows arrives through the feel of the cord, exactly as much as the old man knows and not an ounce more. That is the iceberg working at its smallest scale: delete the emotion, keep the action, and the reader supplies the emotion more accurately than any sentence could. Write instead "he knew this was the moment he had waited eighty-four days for," and the scene collapses, because the reader starts watching the author rather than the water.

2 · The fish surfaces: numbers first, image second

A full day passes between the hookup and the first sight of the marlin, and during that day everything the reader gets is data: the angle of the line, the speed of the boat, the old man's running estimate of the weight. The sequence is deliberate: make the size believable arithmetically, then let it be seen. When the fish does clear the water, Hemingway gives three things — length (two feet longer than the skiff), the bill (like a baseball bat), the stripes (broad, lavender). No accumulation of similes, no leviathan. The dimensions were already built by the previous day's figures; all that is needed here is the color. A day of compression, released in three lines.

3 · A man arguing with his own hand: how a solitary novel generates dialogue

There is one character present in this book and a great deal of talk in it, because the old man speaks to everything: to the fish, to the bird, to his left hand. The cramp passage is the peak of the method — he calls the hand a traitor, sneers that it may as well turn into a claw, bargains with it like an unreliable shipmate. The device solves a technical problem and produces meaning as a byproduct: negotiating with part of your own body splits one man into several — one who still intends to win, one who has already quit, one standing off to the side watching. Hemingway never writes that the old man feels alone. He puts a man in the middle of the ocean arguing with his left hand.

4 · The last two pages: cut from a skeleton to a sentence misheard

In its final pages the novel does something it has not done once: it leaves the old man's point of view. The camera cuts to the Terrace, to a tourist, to a line of mangled English. The reader has just come through three days and nights with this man and knows what that spine is; the people in the frame do not, and are admiring the tail.

The force of the cut is the gap, not the indictment. The tourists are neither stupid nor cruel — they are passing through and they do not know. The full weight of a thing exists only inside the person who lived it and the child who watched him; to the rest of the world it is debris on a beach waiting for the tide. Then the camera goes back up the hill and ends on a man dreaming of lions: hand the story to a world that cannot read it, then quietly take it back.

The central device: a novel with almost no plot, and what holds it up

There is no subplot, no second character on stage, no suspense — the reader works out early that he will probably not keep this fish. The book performs one piece of physical labor from start to finish and nothing else happens. Three things hold it up.

One: procedure in place of psychology. How many fathoms the lines go down, how the bait is wrapped, how the cord crosses the back, how a knife is lashed to an oar — the densest writing in the book is in these places. This is not background research, it is characterization: watch how a man works and you know who he is. The old man's pride, restraint, precision and severity with himself are delivered without a single adjective, entirely through method. It is Hemingway's lifelong approach — bullfighting, hunting, war: get the technique exactly right and the character grows out of it.

Two: self-address in place of dialogue. A side effect is that every piece of self-encouragement is audible, and therefore available to be doubted — you hear him say a man can be destroyed but not defeated while watching his hands rot. That line is not a motivational poster because of where it is spoken: beside a fish that is being eaten.

Three: numbers as a metronome. Eighty-four days, fifteen hundred pounds, thirty cents a pound, two days and two nights, eighteen feet. These carry the forward pressure that plot would normally carry: with no events, precision becomes the event. And the eighteen feet is the one measurement in the book taken by outsiders — the old man never measures the fish. The moment the skeleton is measured, the thing stops being one man's experience and becomes a figure in the world, and figures hold nothing.

The Christian imagery needs its proportions stated. His palms are opened by the line; the sound he makes when the sharks hit is compared to a man feeling a nail go through his hands into wood; he carries the mast uphill; he sleeps face down with his arms out and his palms up. These are arrangements that are demonstrably in the text, not reader projection — but reading the whole book as a passion play overshoots: the old man redeems nobody, he is settling his own account. Hemingway's own later account of it ran roughly: he tried to make a real old man, a real boy, a real sea and a real fish, and if he made them true enough they would mean many things. The images are real; the allegorizing is your business.

What it forces you to think about

Question 1
"Destroyed but not defeated" — is that a truth, or the one thing a loser can still give himself?

Take the skeptical side to its strongest first, because it is harder than it looks. He has spent a harpoon, a knife, a tiller, most of his skin and possibly a rib, and brought home a carcass that cannot be eaten or sold. Any honest accountant calls that a total loss. What "not defeated" does is remove outcomes from the evaluation — as long as the bearing holds, it doesn't count as losing. The trouble is that once that move is allowed, nothing counts as losing, and the capacity to correct goes with it. A man who takes "I failed with dignity" as consolation is very likely to row out exactly as far next season, because he no longer has to learn anything from results. And the bill is not paid only by him: the fish died for nothing, and a boy now has to feed an old man with ruined hands. Undefeatedness of spirit turns very easily into a license not to change a situation that should be changed.

Now the other side, which is harder to refute. If value is settled entirely by outcome, then most lives are scrap: thirty years of unwitnessed work, the care of someone who will not recover, a craft practiced to a level nobody wants — all of it zeroed. No one can live on that ledger. More to the point, the old man's line is not an abstract declaration; it has a precise referent. He did not let go of the line. He did not cut the fish loose to save his strength. He did not stop after the third wave. "Not defeated," here, means he did not quit at any of the moments where quitting would have been perfectly reasonable — a checkable statement of fact, not an adjective. And he is not using it as comfort: the next thing he says is that he went out too far. He holds honesty about the result and refusal to yield in the process at the same time, and neither cancels the other.

Brought back to your own position — that thing you have been at for years which may well never produce a result — there is no general solution, but there is a usable line. Notice whether you are using "not defeated" to explain what has already happened, or to decide what has not happened yet. The first is meaning. The second is usually refusal to adapt.

Question 2
He knew he should not go that far, and went. Is that what craft requires, or is it vanity with an alibi?

Blaming him is easy, and the first person in the book to do it is him. He repeats that he went out too far, and he says something harsher still: that he killed this fish not only to stay alive and to sell it, but out of pride, and because he is a fisherman. He converted the shame of eighty-four empty days into a wager, and the stake was not only his own life but the fish, the boy's expectations, and what remains of his years. And any experienced fisherman can do the arithmetic on a fifteen-hundred-pound animal bleeding along the outside of a small skiff for a dozen hours in warm water. He was not ambushed by fate. He walked forward knowing.

The other side stands up too, and it is this book's real stake. Every craft taken to its limit requires going where others do not: that fish exists only at that depth, and not rowing that far means never meeting it. The problem with "don't go out too far" as a rule is that it guarantees you never hook anything large. And "too far" is a judgment available only afterwards — had the sharks not come, had the wind been kinder, had he been ten years younger, the same voyage would be called nerve. Our distinction between arrogance and ambition is mostly reverse-engineered from results. Nor was he reckless: the whole novel demonstrates his precision about depth, current and wind. His error is not the error of ignorance; it is a professional's error at the edge of his own competence, and that edge can only be located by crossing it.

In ordinary life this is the shape of quitting a job for the thing, putting savings into a project, investing in a relationship one more time. Beforehand, prudence and mediocrity look identical; afterwards, courage and stupidity look identical. The novel's only hint is structural: the old man does not regret the distance, he regrets not protecting what he caught. He audits the execution, not the ambition. Whether that is a deeper form of pride, Hemingway does not say.

Question 3
He calls the fish his brother and then kills it. Is the respect real, or is it packaging?

The suspicious case is sharp. Strip the lyricism out of what he says to the fish and the content is: you are great, you are noble, I love you, and I will kill you before dark. It is a one-sided relationship — the fish did not consent to the brotherhood. Hunters have crowned their prey in every culture, and the function is clear: it makes killing solemn, and therefore bearable, and the beneficiary is not the animal but the man holding the harpoon. The same language is more obviously suspect ashore: "I have enormous respect for my opponent" usually follows the opponent being destroyed, and "this was a difficult decision" is how layoffs are announced. Respect that changes no behavior is just a soundtrack for the behavior.

The defense is at least as strong, and closer to the bone of this book. Treating an opponent as an object is the precondition for killing — every large-scale harm in history begins by downgrading the other party to a number, a pest, a material. The old man does the reverse: he raises a thing he has to do to a level equal with himself, and so the cost is charged in full to his own account. And his respect has a sequel few people pay: he is eaten too. He is not reflecting from the shore; he is stripped of his prize, emptied of strength, and brought home with two ruined hands — he and the fish end in the same condition, and neither walks away whole. Most tellingly, the novel makes him apologize, out loud, to a skeleton, with nobody there. A man merely performing respect has no reason to apologize where no one can hear.

The everyday version reaches anyone who eats meat, or who has had to make a decision that hurt someone: does acknowledging the other party's weight make the act better, or only make you feel better? There is one usable test — whether the respect cost you anything. His did.

The obsession: what Hemingway kept writing

Lay his books side by side and one posture recurs: a man inside something he is going to lose, and how he finishes doing it. The interest is never in the result; it is in whether the private rules he set for himself are still standing when the result arrives.

In The Sun Also Rises (1926) Jake Barnes carries a wound from the war, cannot have the person he wants, and gets through his days on drinking, fishing and bullfights — form is the only thing he can still control. A Farewell to Arms (1929) is blunter: a man deserts the war, rows his love across a lake into a neutral country, thinks he has got out — and Catherine dies in childbirth and he walks back to the hotel in the rain. In For Whom the Bell Tolls (1940) Robert Jordan blows the bridge, breaks his leg, and stays behind to wait for the enemy so that the others can get further away. All of these men fail, and in each case what the novel is watching is the same thing: whether, during the hours of losing, the man went out of shape.

The Old Man and the Sea is the cleanest statement of it because every impurity has been removed — no war, no love, no society, only a man, a thing he knows how to do, and an outcome that will go against him. The eye he brought to bullfighting in Death in the Afternoon is transferred intact to fishing: what matters is not whether the bull dies (it will) but whether the man's movements in those minutes are clean.

One thing cannot be stepped around. In July 1961, at Ketchum, Idaho, Hemingway killed himself with a shotgun. It changes not one word of the novel, but it makes "a man can be destroyed but not defeated" a more complicated sentence. Treating that as irony, or as exposure, is too cheap; the honest reading is that this ethic of holding on was written by someone who knew exactly how hard holding on is.

Criticism and controversy

Without flattery: this is the most contested book in Hemingway's shelf, and a good deal of the criticism lands.

  • The charge of self-imitation. Short sentences, restraint, the man who endures — these were discoveries once, and by 1952 they were Hemingway's recognizable trademark, so critics have argued the book is an author skillfully performing himself. One frequently cited academic essay argues outright that the sea and shark writing is inaccurate and the feeling manufactured; it is titled "Fakery in The Old Man and the Sea," by the American scholar Robert P. Weeks. Agree or not, the verdict that it is easier and more ingratiating than The Sun Also Rises has never gone away — twenty-seven thousand words, one line of action, one character, closer in kind to an extended parable than to a novel.
  • The allegory problem. Textbooks, motivational culture and poster shops have reduced it to a single sentence about being destroyed but not defeated. That is both its success and its loss: a book about staying accurate inside concrete labor gets read as a slogan. Read as a maxim, all the awkward parts flatten out — his own admission of pride, his apology to the fish, the climb with the flag of defeat on his shoulder.
  • What the prizes were for. The Pulitzer and the Nobel arrived back to back, directly after a badly received book. Many critics hold that these were substantially career awards, for which this small book supplied a respectable occasion. That does not diminish its quality, but calling it Hemingway's acknowledged summit is not accurate — in professional estimation The Sun Also Rises and the short stories usually rank higher.
  • No women at all. There is no named female character: the wife is dead and her photograph is put away, and the only woman who appears is the tourist at the end who mistakes the spine for a shark. You can call that the reality of a fishing village and of the sea; you can also call it the standing configuration of Hemingway's world, in which value, testing and intimacy all occur between males — the old man and the boy, the old man and DiMaggio, the old man and the fish. His distinction between calling the sea la mar, as those who love her do, and the younger fishermen's masculine el mar, spoken of as a contestant, gets cited by both sides.
  • Period marks in the language. The English deliberately renders the fishermen's speech in a slightly formal, archaic register to suggest the Spanish underneath; some find this plain and dignified, others find it turns Cuban fishermen into figures in a fable. The Casablanca arm-wrestling passage also uses a period term for the opponent's race that pulls a modern reader up short — a 1952 text showing its date, and more honest to name than to route around.

Ten sentences to take away

1 · This is not the story of an old man beating a great fish; it is the story of a man who beat his opponent and lost to the scavengers who arrived afterwards.

2 · The reason he wins the fish and the reason he loses it are the same reason: he went out too far.

3 · The densest writing in the book is all procedure, because watching how a man works tells you who he is.

4 · The small tuna kept for bait becomes his only food; the novel never points this out, it just puts the strips in his mouth.

5 · He argues with his own left hand in the middle of the ocean — loneliness performed rather than reported.
