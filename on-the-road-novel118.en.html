Novels, Deep-Read · Jack Kerouac · No.118

On the Road

On the Road · Jack Kerouac · 1957 · Five parts

中文 →

Night in Nebraska. A flatbed truck runs west with its headlights on, no tarp over the back, seven or eight hitchhikers sprawled across the boards with the wind roaring past their ears. A bottle goes from hand to hand. Somebody starts humming a Southern song and the others hum along.
A young man from New York lies on his back under a sky full of prairie stars and feels, for the first time, that he has touched the skin of the continent.
He took to the road to chase someone. That someone is six hundred miles away in Denver, slicing each day into pieces and handing them out to two women and a poet.

This piece contains complete spoilers: how each of the five crossings ends, where the man who drove everyone crazy finally goes, and whose name the last paragraph says. The meaning of the book is hidden on a street corner at the very end: it looks like a joyride, but it is really an elegy, written by a man who spent four years running behind someone else, for that someone and for his own youth. Without that corner, you can't see what all the celebration was a send-off for.

In one sentence

A young writer, newly divorced and convinced that "everything was dead," is set alight by a live-wire kid just out of reform school, and over the next four years crosses the continent five times (New York, Denver, San Francisco, New Orleans, Mexico City) chasing jazz, girls and something called "IT"; every trip ends with somebody left behind, and the last person left behind, on a winter street corner in New York, is the one who set him alight.

Coordinates

Jack Kerouac (1922–1969) was the son of a French-Canadian working family in Lowell, Massachusetts; the household spoke a French dialect and he didn't learn English until he was about six. His first novel, The Town and the City (1950), was conventional and made little noise. In April 1951 he taped sheets of tracing paper into a single roll some 120 feet long, fed it into his typewriter, and in about three weeks typed the first draft of On the Road. Publishers turned it down for six years; Viking finally brought it out in 1957, heavily edited.

Its place in literary history is as the founding novel of the Beat Generation. Kerouac had tossed off the word in the late forties for a generation worn out and hollowed by respectable postwar America; he later insisted that "beat" also meant beatific, that the people most worn down were closest to God. Nearly everyone in the book has a real model: Sal is Kerouac himself, Dean is Neal Cassady, Carlo Marx is Allen Ginsberg, Old Bull Lee is William S. Burroughs.

The story

Part One · Summer 1947: a red line on the map

Sal Paradise lives with his aunt in Paterson, New Jersey. He has just split from his wife and just come through an illness he doesn't want to talk about. Setup ①All he will say is that it had something to do with that miserable break-up, and with his feeling that everything was dead. That's where the book starts: with a man who thinks the world has gone out.

Then Dean Moriarty arrives. He's barely twenty, fresh out of reform school, and has come in from Denver by bus with his new wife Marylou; he answers the door in his shorts. He steals cars compulsively, and he spent his boyhood in thirds: a third in the pool hall, a third in jail, a third in the public library. Setup ②His father is a wino on Denver's Larimer Street, and Dean grew up knocking around the streets and flophouses with him. Dean talks like a machine gun and agrees with everything: yes, yes, I know exactly what you mean. Setup ③What he says he wants from Sal is to be taught how to write.

Sal soon sees that Dean's interest in writing is the same as his interest in women, cars and jazz: a wish to swallow life whole. Sal falls for the wild, open-country energy in him. The New York intellectuals around Sal are all murmuring their self-disgust; Dean looks like someone who just walked out of the sun. Dean goes back to Denver. In July, Sal sets out after him with fifty dollars.

The way he sets out tells you who he is. He has spent months dreaming over a map at home and chosen Route 6, which runs from one coast to the other, Setup ④meaning to follow that single red line all the way. Instead he spends a whole day in the rain at the foot of Bear Mountain, north of New York, where almost nothing is heading west. A passing driver asks him why on earth he's trying to go west from here. He slinks back to the city by bus, takes another bus to Chicago, and only there starts hitching for real.

Then the American interior. He wakes in a cheap hotel by the railroad in Des Moines with the sunset turning the room red, and for a few seconds has no idea who he is: a man on the dividing line between the East of his youth and the West of his future. He catches the flatbed and crosses the Nebraska night with a crew of hoboes. In Denver, Dean is too busy to bother with him: juggling Marylou and a new lover, Camille, while sitting up all night with Carlo Marx making "complete confessions" of every thought in their heads. Sal's role in Denver is to follow and watch.

He pushes on to San Francisco and his old school friend Remi Boncoeur, takes a job as a night guard at a workers' barracks, and joins Remi in stealing from the cafeteria; a dinner Remi has carefully arranged to impress his stepfather is wrecked by Sal, who leaves Remi humiliated in front of his family. Heading south, Sal meets Terry on a bus, a Mexican girl just escaped from a husband who beat her, with a little boy in tow. They pitch a tent in the California cotton fields and live a few weeks like man and wife. Sal is a slow, bad picker and can barely earn enough to eat. In October the air turns cold, and he knows he can't support her and doesn't mean to stay. They agree she'll come to New York later; they both know she won't.

He hitches and starves his way back east, stands for a while in the crowds of Times Square feeling like a man back from another planet, and gets home to his aunt's, who tells him that Dean was there a few days ago looking for him, and left again for San Francisco. Sal crossed the continent to find Dean, Dean crossed it to find Sal, and they passed each other on the way. It is the book's first note.

Part Two · Winter 1948–49: a new Hudson and a road left behind

Christmas, and Sal and his aunt are at his brother's in Virginia. A brand-new 1949 Hudson, caked in mud, pulls up outside: Dean has driven it straight across from San Francisco, with Marylou and his friend Ed Dunkel aboard. Dean is married to Camille by now, with a newborn daughter; he has spent his savings on the car, left mother and baby, and picked Marylou up again. He says he has to go, because he has to go.

They haul the aunt's furniture from Virginia to New York, throw themselves into a New Year's run of parties, and then Sal, Dean and Marylou drive south. Dean talks the whole way: we know time, we know everything is fine, there's nothing to worry about. His hands fly around; the wheel is an instrument.

They reach Algiers, across the river from New Orleans, to see Old Bull Lee: an oddball in a ramshackle house full of guns and junk who lectures his children on how American bureaucrats have ruined everything, while his wife Jane gets through the nights on Benzedrine. Ed's wife Galatea, whom he abandoned along the way, is there waiting for him. Sal looks at Bull and sees a man who has seen straight through America and is trapped inside it anyway.

Then Texas, Arizona, the money running down; they pawn things and pick up hitchhikers for gas money. Under the Texas sun Dean strips and drives naked, and Marylou and Sal strip too, three people in the front seat with no clothes on crossing the scrubland. They are among the happiest pages in the book.

As soon as they hit San Francisco, Dean drops Sal and Marylou outside a hotel on O'Farrell Street, Setup ⑤says see you later, and drives back to Camille. Sal has no money; Marylou soon goes off with a nightclub owner. Sal wanders the city hungry, picking up cigarette butts. Outside a fish-and-chips shop the woman behind the counter gives him a frightened look, and in his hunger and dizziness he suddenly believes she was his mother in some earlier life and he the wayward son back from jail, begging at her door. In that moment, he says, he reached a point of ecstasy where life and death, past and present all folded together, like a light passing through him. Then he walks back out into the street and buys a bus ticket east.

Part Three · Spring and summer 1949: the Holy Goof, and a Cadillac at a hundred and ten

Sal saves a little money and goes to live in Denver for a while, trying out a settled life, and is horribly lonely. One lilac evening he walks through the city's Black and Mexican neighborhood and wishes he were one of them, feeling that the best the white world had given him wasn't ecstasy enough. (This is the passage that later drew the most fire; see "Controversy.")

He goes on to San Francisco. Dean's thumb is in a bandage: in a fight with Marylou he hit her on the forehead and broke his own thumb. Camille, pregnant with their second child, has had enough and throws them both out. Sal takes Dean to Galatea Dunkel's, where several women round on him and list his sins one by one: you only ever think of yourself, you leave your wife and children at home, you have no sense of responsibility to anyone. Dean stands in the middle of the room, ragged and unshaven, and says nothing in his own defense. He just giggles, rubs his hands, looks around.

Right then Sal gives him a name: the Holy Goof. He decides that Dean is "beat" in the deepest sense, not depraved but emptied out by life until only something pure is left, the root of the beatific. On the spot he tells Dean: come to New York with me, and later we'll go to Italy. Dean says yes.

They spend whole nights in the little jazz joints of San Francisco. Dean yells "Go!" from the floor, climbing phrase by phrase after the tenor player, and says the man has "IT": the moment when time stops and the whole of a life is blown into a single second. IT is Dean's religion and the thing this book keeps chasing: something that only appears at top speed, in hunger and chaos, and vanishes the moment you stop.

They pick up drive-away cars from travel bureaus and head east. In Denver Dean steals one car after another for the fun of it and they have to flee; then they deliver a Cadillac limousine to Chicago, and on the Midwestern highways Dean pushes it to a hundred and ten. By the time it reaches its owner it is a wreck. Payoff ② ↑During those Denver days Dean also goes round the cheap bars and barbershops of Larimer Street asking after his father, and nobody knows where the old wino has gone. The backstory dropped in passing in Part One has turned into a direction: maybe Dean isn't running to anywhere at all, but looking for a father who is never where he was last seen. He doesn't find him, and he never will.

Back in New York, Dean meets Inez at a party and is living with her almost overnight; soon she's pregnant too. Now he has a pregnant woman at each end of the country, and an ex-wife in Marylou.

Part Four · 1950: the end of the road is Mexico

Sal comes into some money and plans to go to Mexico alone. Dean hears and immediately decides to come: he wants a quick Mexican divorce from Camille so he can marry Inez. With Stan Shephard, a friend from Denver, they cross the border at Laredo in a battered 1937 Ford.

Mexico goes to all three of their heads. Dean says the people here don't suspect you, don't guard against you; they just look at you. In a town called Gregoria a young local named Victor gets them high on a joint as thick as a cigar, then takes them to the town brothel, where the mambo is so loud the walls shake and Dean dances all over the room. At night they drive through the tropical jungle, too hot to sleep; Sal climbs onto the roof of the car and lies there with insects settling all over him and feels he has dissolved into the dark. On the mountain road, little Indian girls hold up crystals for sale, and Dean takes off his wristwatch and gives it to one of them.

Sal says they have finally reached the magic land at the end of the road. Payoff ④ ↑The red line of Route 6 from Part One gets its real ending here. Sal always imagined the road as a straight line from one shore to the other, with an answer waiting at the far end. The actual road wound for four years, five trips, half a continent, and its end wasn't on the West Coast but south of the border. There was no answer there either; only a fever.

In Mexico City Sal comes down with dysentery and burns with fever until he's barely conscious. Dean has his divorce papers. He stands by the bed and says he has to go. Through the fever Sal sees only a figure walking out the door. Payoff ⑤ ↑This is the second time Dean has left Sal in a strange city. The first time, in San Francisco in Part Two, Sal was hungry and could still turn it into a vision. This time he's too sick to stand, and afterward can only admit that Dean was a rat, then add that he had to understand the impossible tangle of Dean's life, how he had no choice but to leave. Sal forgives him, but for the first time Sal has looked at Dean through those women's eyes. Payoff ① ↑The book began with an illness: a man who thought everything was dead, brought back to life by Dean. It turns on an illness too: the same Dean leaving him sick at the end of the road. The man who cured him and the man who left him are one and the same.

Dean goes back to New York, marries Inez, and promptly gets on the road back to Camille in San Francisco.

Part Five · The corner of Seventh Avenue

Part Five is only a few pages long. In New York Sal meets Laura, the girl he feels he has been looking for all his life, and they plan to move to San Francisco. He writes to tell Dean. Dean turns up in New York early, and he's a wreck: ragged clothes, staring eyes. Payoff ③ ↑The man who talked like a machine gun in Part One and turned up wanting Sal to teach him to write can now barely get a sentence out; he nods, gestures, stammers "yes, yes." Language has flowed from Dean to Sal the whole book, and now the current runs backward: Dean is the one who can't speak, and Sal is the one who writes it all down. The book itself is the homework from that writing lesson.

Sal has no money to go with him now. That evening his old friend Remi Boncoeur pulls up in a Cadillac to take Sal and Laura to hear Duke Ellington at the Metropolitan Opera. Dean asks for a lift part of the way; Remi refuses. He wants nothing to do with him. Sal doesn't press it. In a moth-eaten overcoat he brought especially for the cold of the East, Dean walks off alone, rounds the corner of Seventh Avenue, and is gone. In Part One it was Sal who humiliated Remi in front of his stepfather; here Remi is at the wheel and Sal is sitting on the respectable side. A character's second appearance has switched Sal's allegiance.

The last paragraph jumps far ahead in time. Sal sits on an old, broken-down pier on the Hudson as the sun goes down, thinking of all that raw land rolling westward, of everyone dreaming out there on the road, of how nobody knows what will happen to anybody beyond the forlorn rags of growing old. He thinks of Dean Moriarty, he even thinks of Old Dean Moriarty, the father they never found. He thinks of Dean Moriarty.

The fuse

Looking back, the fuse of this book isn't any one departure but that illness on the first page. A man who believes everything is dead needs someone to prove the world is still alive, and Dean is someone who proves it nonstop, with speed, with women, with all-night jazz. Sal isn't chasing Dean; he's chasing the evidence in Dean that the world is still alive. That's why every trip has to repeat: ecstasy, exhaustion, someone left behind, back to his aunt's, off again. There is only one way for the loop to end: Sal finds other evidence (Laura, a future he can settle into, this book), and Dean changes from the man who saved him into a figure in an old overcoat disappearing round a corner. The four years the book covers are one long convalescence, and the day Sal gets well is the day the friendship ends.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion I · The hotel in Des Moines: fifteen seconds of not knowing who you are

Part One. After a full day on the bus, Sal falls asleep in a cheap hotel by the tracks in Des Moines. When he wakes the sun is setting, a train whistle is blowing outside, old wood creaks, and for about fifteen seconds, staring at the ceiling, he cannot remember who or where he is. He isn't frightened; he just feels like a stranger, a ghost. Then he thinks: I am halfway across America, on the dividing line between the East of my youth and the West of my future.

It works because Kerouac turns a point on the map into a point in a life without spelling the idea out. Nothing happens here: no Dean, no jazz, no girls, just a man waking in a strange room. Every ecstasy that follows rests on these fifteen blank seconds: what people on the road may want most is this temporary release from having to be themselves. The pacing is very slow, and in a novel famous for speed it reads like a deep breath, tuning the instrument for all the acceleration to come.

Slow motion II · Galatea's living room: the accused says nothing

Part Three. The women take turns accusing Dean, and every charge is fair: he walked out on a pregnant wife, he broke his own thumb, he hurts everyone who comes near him. The camera doesn't cut to Dean's face to plead his case; it stays behind Sal's eyes as Sal watches this man stand in the middle of the room taking the abuse, giggling, tapping his foot as if to music only he can hear.

The technique here is an asymmetry of viewpoint. Kerouac gives all the arguments to the women and all the interpretation to Sal. The charges we hear are concrete and checkable; Sal's defense ("Holy Goof," "the root of the beatific") is abstract and religious. The gap between them is the most honest thing in the book. It doesn't whitewash Dean. It just shows you that one man can be a bastard in some eyes and a saint in another's, and that both are describing the same facts. Critics often call this a boys' book in which women are scenery; yet in exactly this scene the women's words are left standing on the page, and later, in Mexico City, Sal says the same thing himself.

Slow motion III · The corner of Seventh Avenue, and an old pier

The last two pages. Dean leaves; the camera doesn't follow him, it just watches from a distance as the moth-eaten overcoat turns the corner into the cold wind. Sal sits in the Cadillac, where it's warm and a concert is waiting. Then the narration leaps years ahead to the riverside, the sun going down, the whole of America spreading out in the dusk.

The power comes from a sudden pulling back of the tense. For three hundred pages the book has been a present-tense sprint, and the reader has been carried along at Dean's speed. In the last paragraph the narrator gets out of the car and sits down in "afterwards." Only then do you realize that all the revelry was being looked back on from this pier, that every "Go!" had an echo in it. The last sentence's repetition (thinking of Dean, of his father, of Dean again) is like a man calling the same name across the water. Kerouac doesn't have Sal weep or repent. He just lets a name be said three times, and that is the elegy.

The core device: a roll of paper, and a man walking behind

The book's most famous device is that roll of typing paper. Kerouac wanted to write the way jazz musicians improvise, a method he later called spontaneous prose: no outline, no going back to revise, blowing straight ahead like a saxophonist on a long phrase, with sentences broken by breath rather than grammar. The roll meant he never had to stop to change sheets, and not stopping was the point. The immediate spark came from Cassady himself: at the end of 1950 Cassady sent him a rambling, uninhibited letter dozens of pages long, written in one rush, and Kerouac decided that this was how Americans actually sounded.

An honest note belongs here: "written in three weeks" is true; "never revised" is a myth. Beneath the scroll lay years of notebooks and journals; between 1951 and 1957 he reworked it repeatedly, and his publisher cut hard, swapping real names for pseudonyms and removing the franker sex and anything libelous. In 2007 the original scroll was published with the real names, and it reads rougher, messier and more direct, which also shows how carefully the published version had been shaped. "Spontaneous" is better understood as a stance and a rhythm than as a construction log.

More important than the scroll is where the narrator stands. Sal is not the hero. He is the one who follows the hero. In Part One he tells us his habit: all his life he has trailed after people who interest him, the ones who are mad to live, mad to talk, who want everything at once. That position shapes the whole book:

  • Dean is never written from the inside. We never know what he's thinking, only what he does and says. So he can be a saint or a bastard; the blank left for the reader is his charisma.
  • The structure is a loop, not a growth curve. The five parts are almost five variations on one tune: departure, ecstasy, exhaustion, someone left behind, back to the aunt. The novel never presents the loop as progress; it just repeats it until the narrator himself stops.
  • The joyride is told from the position of the elegy. The Sal who writes all this down is already sitting on that pier. So even in the happiest passages, the sentences often carry a premature nostalgia: he is missing things while they are still happening.

Put those three together and you see why a book taken as a manifesto for hitting the road leaves you hollow at the end: the life it celebrates is being told by a man who has already left it.

What it forces you to think about

One · Is a friend worth it if he brings you back to life but is sure to walk out when you fall ill?

Dean gave Sal the most vivid years of his life. Dean also abandoned him twice in strange cities, once hungry, once sick. Plenty of people have someone like this: every day with them is brighter than ordinary days, and you can't count on them to pick up the phone.

The strongest case for "worth it": reliability and aliveness rarely grow in the same person, and you can't judge fire by the standards of insurance. Dean never lied to Sal about what he was. Sal knew, and Sal chose. If Sal had only befriended dependable people, he would have stayed at his aunt's believing everything was dead, and he would never have written this book. Wanting to split someone into the part that revives you and the part that wounds you, and keep only the first, is its own kind of greed.

The other side is just as strong: "that's just how he is" is the favorite amulet of everyone who hurts people. Sal's "understanding" sounds generous, but it excuses a man from what he owes. The women were right: Dean's freedom was paid for by others, and Sal was one of the people paying; he just preferred to write the bill as poetry. If a relationship only holds while you are healthy, flush and free, is it friendship or consumption?

Kerouac's answer is in the ending: Sal chooses both. He forgives Dean, and then he lets him go at the corner. The person in your life who brings you back to life: how many times are you planning to forgive them?

Two · Is a life of pure burning still freedom if the bill goes to someone else?

Over the course of the book Dean goes through three marriages and several children. He loves them, and he leaves them again and again. Camille waits in a small house in San Francisco with a baby on her hip while he chases IT on the road.

The strongest case for Dean: a society that presses everyone into the mold of "responsible" loses the very things that make life worth living. Postwar America was turning into suburbs, installment plans and nine-to-five, and Dean refused to be tamed. The fire in him is exactly what this book, and generations of readers after it, borrowed from him. When people today talk about opting out of the rat race, taking off, living as themselves first, there's a little Dean underneath. And he never pretended to be a good husband; his irresponsibility was at least honest.

The strongest case against can be written on Camille's behalf: freedom is not a property of one person; it is a distribution. Dean could leave whenever he liked because someone always stayed behind to change the diapers and pay the rent; his IT needed someone else's everyday to stand on. That isn't honesty, it's shifting the cost onto people who aren't in the room. Behind almost every ecstatic scene in the book, just off camera, is a woman who was left. The modern version: when someone drops everything to chase a dream, who is looking after their parents?

Where does Kerouac stand? While writing, plainly with Dean; but he didn't cut a single word of the women's charges. Ask yourself: the kind of freedom you envy, who is paying for it?

Three · Does growing up mean betraying someone on some street corner?

At the end Sal gets into Remi's Cadillac and lets Dean walk off alone. He has no money to go with him, he has Laura now, he's on his way to a concert: every reason is a fair one.

The strongest case for Sal: people who refuse to leave their youth end up eaten by it. By Part Five Dean has collapsed; he can't speak and barely knows where he's going. One more trip with him wouldn't save Dean; it would only drag Sal down too. Sal needs a place from which to write all this, and that place can only be off the road. Everyone, at some point, chooses a steady job, a partner, a life that isn't a party every night. That isn't betrayal; it's the price.

The strongest case for Dean: all Sal had to say that night was "Remi, give him a lift." Not hit the road again, just a short ride. Sal didn't say it, because speaking up for Dean in that car would have meant admitting he had once been on Dean's side. Growing up needn't mean betraying anyone, but when people cross over to the respectable side, they often shut their former selves out of the car on the way.

Kerouac answers in the last paragraph: he gives Sal no defense, only a seat on a pier and a name to think about over and over. Have you ever had a corner like that, where there was no falling-out, you just didn't speak up that day?

Motifs: what Kerouac kept writing about

Kerouac saw nearly all his novels as one big book he called the Duluoz Legend: one man's life from a Lowell childhood to a mid-life crack-up, under a different alias in each volume, always himself. He kept returning to three things.

First, the sacred in the outcast. He was a devout Catholic, raised to believe that those who suffer are closest to God, so to him hoboes, drunks, car thieves and jazz players all wore halos. The "Holy Goof" is the loudest version of that obsession. Second, mother and brother. When he was four, his nine-year-old brother Gerard died of illness, which he later wrote about in Visions of Gerard; he lived with his mother nearly all his life, and the "aunt" who is always at home waiting for Sal in On the Road is based on her. Sal has to come back to her after every trip; the book's loop has a fixed center. Third, collapse after ecstasy. In The Dharma Bums (1958) he turns to Buddhism, trying to put meditation in place of speed; Big Sur (1962) is about a famous writer crushed by drink and fame in a cabin by the sea. From On the Road to Big Sur is like watching the same man step out of the car and walk to the edge of a cliff.

On the Road is the brightest point on that line: he still believes the road leads somewhere, and already half knows it doesn't.

Controversy and criticism

"That's not writing, that's typing." Truman Capote's jibe is the most quoted, and it stands for a view common in literary circles at the time: the book is long-winded, loose and repetitive, the same ecstasy written out five times, the sentences often a stream of mood. Even admirers concede that some stretches read like a drunk talking. On the other side, Gilbert Millstein's review in The New York Times on publication day called the book's appearance a historic occasion, and made Kerouac famous almost overnight.

Women as roadside scenery. Almost all the women in the book are "girls," sized up, pursued, then left where they stand; Camille, Marylou, Inez and Terry barely get lives of their own on the page. The real women later wrote their own: Cassady's wife Carolyn Cassady published the memoir Off the Road (1990), and Kerouac's former girlfriend Joyce Johnson wrote Minor Characters (1983), a title that is itself a reply.

Romanticizing Black and Mexican lives. In the Denver lilac passage, Sal wishes he were Black because the white world isn't "ecstatic" enough. In a 1961 essay James Baldwin called the passage absolute nonsense, and offensive nonsense at that, and said he would hate to be in Kerouac's shoes if he ever read it aloud from the stage of the Apollo Theater in Harlem. The core objection: envying other people's poverty and suffering as somehow "more real" is a privilege only a white man could afford. The book's descriptions of Mexicans as innocent and unguarded are often discussed in the same frame.

Glamorizing irresponsibility and drugs. After publication, many young people hit the road in its image, and Kerouac was cast as the spokesman of rebellion. The irony is that he didn't care for the role: late in life he kept his distance from the sixties counterculture and drifted to the political right, and in 1968 he appeared, visibly drunk, on William F. Buckley's television show and distanced himself from the hippies. He died the following year, at forty-seven, of internal bleeding brought on by years of heavy drinking.

Crossovers

  • Anthropology · Turner's liminality: Victor Turner found that people in the middle stage of a rite of passage shed their status and meet as equals, forming a bond he called communitas; the hitchhikers on the flatbed have exactly that shape. Where it differs: in a rite, elders lead you back; Sal has to walk back alone, and Dean never comes back.
  • Neuroscience · Berridge's wanting and liking: Kent Berridge distinguished the "wanting" that drives us to pursue from the "liking" we feel on getting, two systems that can come badly apart. Sal's ecstasies happen almost entirely in transit, almost never on arrival. Where it differs: the letdown on arrival in the book is tied to other people's tears.
  • Philosophy · Pascal's diversion: In the Pensées Pascal says all of humanity's misery comes from being unable to sit quietly in a room, so we keep busy to avoid looking at our condition. Dean's speed is diversion to the letter. Where it differs: Pascal wants us to stop and face God; Sal thinks God is in the car at a hundred and ten.
  • Sociology · Hochschild's second shift: In The Second Shift (1989) Arlie Hochschild documented women coming home from work to a second round of housework, which is where men's leisure came from. Camille's little house is the other half of Dean's road. Where it differs: she studied couples under one roof; Dean doesn't even come home to the roof.
