Novels, Deep-Read · Shi Nai'an · No.43

Water Margin

Shi Nai'an (or Shi Nai'an and Luo Guanzhong) · c. 14th century · 100-, 120- and 70-chapter recensions

中文 →

Outside Cangzhou it has snowed all day. Lin Chong, arms instructor to the imperial guard, walks back to the fodder depot through the drifts with a spear on his shoulder and a wine gourd hooked over its point. When he left, the ramshackle hut was already creaking under the weight of the snow. By the time he returns, it has collapsed.
So he carries his bedding half a mile to a mountain-god temple, pulls the door shut behind him, wedges a large stone against it, and eats cold beef with cold wine. Around midnight the fodder depot goes up. Firelight comes through the gaps in the temple door.
He is about to run out and fight the blaze when he hears three men talking, leaning against the door on the other side. They are discussing how to be sure he burned inside. They are discussing how to claim the reward from Marshal Gao when they get back to the capital.
The patience he has been spending for half a book runs out here. He pushes the stone. The door opens.

This page contains complete spoilers — who gets driven into outlawry and how, how the amnesty is finally negotiated, how many of the hundred and eight come home from the Fang La campaign, who sends Song Jiang the poisoned wine, and what he does to Li Kui before he dies. The premise of this site is that everything worth arguing about in this book happens in the last thirty chapters; stopping at "the heroes take their seats" means reading half a novel. If you want to keep the suspense, read it first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

A street loafer with a gift for kickball becomes the marshal in charge of the entire imperial guard, and from that day the powerful can requisition anything by name — your wife, your rank, your good name — with the paperwork in perfect order; one hundred and eight people, each driven separately to the end of the road, gather in a marsh in Shandong and raise a banner reading Act in Heaven's Stead, beating back three imperial armies; then their leader takes them all into an amnesty and sends them to fight for that same court, and twenty-seven come back; and at the end he drinks the poisoned wine the emperor sent him and calls in his most loyal brother to drink it too, in case that brother should rebel and spoil the word loyalty.

Where it sits

The authorship is a mess. The conventional attribution is to Shi Nai'an; some editions credit "compiled by Shi Nai'an, edited by Luo Guanzhong"; and almost nothing verifiable is known about Shi Nai'an himself. What is certain is that no one sat alone in a study and wrote this: it grew out of two centuries of storytelling stalls. Song and Yuan prompt-books, Yuan drama cycles and the anonymous Old Incidents of the Xuanhe Period already contain a roster of thirty-six names and an early version of the birthday-convoy robbery. The historical basis is thin to the point of comedy: in the History of Song, Song Jiang leads a band of raiders in the Huai region around 1119–1121, is defeated by the official Zhang Shuye, and surrenders. Two or three years, a few dozen men. The novel expanded those few lines into a nation's memory of its own outlaws.

It stands with Romance of the Three Kingdoms as one of the two foundations of the Ming novel, running in the opposite direction: the Three Kingdoms is about the court, this is about the bottom of society; that book writes a semi-classical chronicle prose, this is the first long work to put living spoken vernacular on the page. In the late Ming, the critic Jin Shengtan (1608–1661) cut the book off at seventy chapters, deleting the entire amnesty and everything after it, and appended a dream in which all one hundred and eight are executed together. His edition dominated the market for three hundred years, so generations of readers knew a Water Margin with no ending at all. In English, Pearl Buck's 1933 All Men Are Brothers (a title taken from the Analects) was the most influential and the most disputed; Sidney Shapiro's 1980 Outlaws of the Marsh is the standard complete version today.

The story

Act one · The rot starts at the top: a footballer becomes marshal

Before the novel proper there is a prologue. A plague is raging, and Marshal Hong Xin is sent to Dragon-Tiger Mountain to fetch a Daoist master to pray it away. He fails to find the master, but on the mountain he comes across a hall sealed shut, its doors layered with the seals of generations of Daoists: the Hall of Suppressed Demons. A monk begs him not to open it. Hong Xin is a court official; being told no by a country monk is an affront, so he has it opened. Inside is a stone stele over a deep pit, and out of the pit comes a black vapour that bursts into a hundred-odd streaks of gold and scatters in every direction. The monk goes white: thirty-six Heavenly Spirits and seventy-two Earthly Fiends, one hundred and eight demon lords, and you have just let them out.seed ①

Then the book begins, and the first thing it does is not to introduce a hero but to trace the career of a scoundrel. In the capital there is a broken-down loafer named Gao Qiu, good for nothing except an uncanny foot for kickball. Sent to deliver something to Prince Duan's residence, he arrives while the prince is playing, and when the ball comes loose he returns it with a flourish. The prince keeps him. Months later the prince becomes emperor — Huizong — and within half a year Gao Qiu has gone from hanger-on to Marshal of the Imperial Guard, commanding the standing army. On his first day he takes the roll and notices that an instructor named Wang Jin is absent: Wang Jin's father once beat him with a staff. Wang Jin flees the capital that night with his aged mother, and on the road takes a pupil — a tattooed boy called Shi Jin.

Act two · Two lives snapped: Lu Zhishen and Lin Chong

In Weizhou, Shi Jin meets an officer named Lu Da, the one man in the book who never once acts out of a private grievance. Drinking upstairs in a tavern, he hears crying next door and sends for the source: a singing girl, Jin Cuilian, was taken as a concubine by a butcher known as the Lord of the West, on a contract for three thousand strings of cash that was never paid — and now that his wife has thrown her out, the butcher wants the money back. None of this is any business of Lu Da's. He empties his purse on the spot, escorts father and daughter out of the city himself the next morning, sits in their inn long enough to be certain they are clear of the place, and only then goes looking for the butcher — first running him ragged (ten catties of lean, minced fine; ten of fat, also minced; ten of gristle, minced too), and only when the man's temper breaks does he hit him. Three punches, and the butcher is dead. Lu Da had meant to give him a beating. He prods the body, says loudly for the benefit of the crowd that the man is shamming and they will settle this later, and walks away fast. The detail is worth keeping: he has no sense whatever of being a killer. He has merely discovered how strong he is.

On the run he is placed in a monastery on Mount Wutai and given the religious name Zhishen. He drinks, he wrecks things, the monastery cannot hold him, and the abbot writes him a letter of introduction to the Great Xiangguo Temple in the capital. At parting the abbot gives him four lines: rise at a forest, prosper at a mountain, flourish at water, halt at a river.seed ② He does not understand a word of it and takes it anyway. At Xiangguo he is set to guard the vegetable garden; a gang of local toughs comes to bully him and ends up in the manure pit; next day they throw a conciliatory feast, and while they are eating, a crow will not stop cawing in the willow above them. Lu Zhishen walks over, tightens his belt, and pulls the willow out of the ground by its roots. In the crowd stands an officer who came to watch: Lin Chong, arms instructor to the imperial guard.

Lin Chong is the most complete destruction in the book. He has rank, reputation and a wife he loves — he lacks nothing, and therefore has everything to lose. The trouble begins when someone takes an interest in his wife: Marshal Gao's adopted son accosts her at a temple festival, and when Lin Chong comes running, his raised fist stops in the air the moment he recognises whose son this is. That fist, not thrown, is the watershed of the whole novel: Lu Da killed a man for a woman he had never met; Lin Chong lowered his hand for his own wife.

What follows is a procedure. First his good friend Lu Qian invites him out drinking and has his wife lured to Lu Qian's upstairs room; it fails. Then a man appears in the street selling a fine sabre. It really is a fine sabre. Lin Chong loves weapons, and pays a thousand strings for it. The next day Marshal Gao sends for him: the Marshal has a blade too, and would like to compare them. He follows the servants inward, gate after gate, and walks into the White Tiger Hall — the military council chamber, where carrying a weapon is a capital offence. The Marshal appears on the dais, and one sentence settles it.

On the road to exile in Cangzhou, two escorts try to finish him in Wild Boar Forest and Lu Zhishen comes out from behind a tree. Before setting off Lin Chong did something that is painful to read: he wrote his wife a deed of divorce and asked his father-in-law to find her a better match.seed ③ His reasoning is spelled out — I may not come back, and she should not be tied to a dead man. It is the most decent form of love he can imagine.

And then the snow at the top of this page. The warden moves him to the fodder depot in order to burn him alive in it; the snow crushes the hut and drives him to the temple, and so keeps him alive. Behind the door he hears the whole truth: Lu Qian is here on Marshal Gao's orders and has already been paid for it. The sabre, the White Tiger Hall, the two escorts and this fire are four movements of one hand. Lin Chong opens the door, kills three men, and walks off through the snow into a different life.

Act three · Yellow Earth Ridge: one bucket of wine rewrites seven lives

Another line opens in Shandong. The governor of Daming is sending his father-in-law, the grand councillor Cai Jing, a birthday present worth a hundred thousand strings of cash — the birthday convoy. Last year's was robbed on the road, so this year he picks his most reliable man: Yang Zhi, descendant of a famous general, with a blue birthmark across his face, who wants one thing out of life, which is to win back his family's name through service. Yang Zhi disguises the treasure as merchants' bales and marches at midday, because bandits work the hill roads in the afternoon.

On Yellow Earth Ridge the heat stops them and they collapse into a pine wood where seven date merchants are already lying in the shade. A man comes up the ridge carrying two buckets of wine on a pole, singing. Yang Zhi forbids his men to buy. The date merchants buy a whole bucket and drink it off in plain sight, and nothing happens to them. The wine seller turns to go; one of the merchants tries to cadge a free ladle from the second bucket and is chased down and robbed of it; another steps in to smooth things over and, in doing so, gives the ladle one more stir in the bucket — and that is when the drug goes in. The soldiers, having watched strangers empty an entire bucket unharmed, break; Yang Zhi himself relents. Half a bucket later, fifteen men go down one after another and lie there watching the seven tip out their dates, load the treasure and wheel it away, unable to say a word.

The seven are Chao Gai, Wu Yong, Gongsun Sheng, Liu Tang and the three Ruan brothers, plus Bai Sheng with the wine. They were not driven to Mount Liang; they went out and took it — structurally this matters enormously, because it means the skeleton of Liangshan is not made of injustice alone. Half of it is appetite. When the case breaks, a county clerk named Song Jiang gets word to Chao Gai first, and the seven fight their way to the marsh.

The chief of Mount Liang at that time is Wang Lun, a failed examination candidate with a small mind. When Lin Chong first came up, Wang Lun demanded a token of allegiance: go down the mountain within three days, kill someone, bring back the head. That rule is the thesis of the entire book — to come through this door, first do something you can never come back from. When Chao Gai's party arrives and Wang Lun again tries to move them along, Wu Yong does not argue; he only catches Lin Chong's eye, and Lin Chong stands up and stabs the man dead.

Settled at last, Lin Chong sends for his family. The messenger returns with two facts: her father held out for the better part of a year against pressure to remarry her to Marshal Gao's son, and she hanged herself six months ago.payoff ③ ↑ That deed of divorce was the most considerate act of Lin Chong's life; he meant it as an exit for her, and it stopped nothing — in this country a man out of favour does not even own the right to give something up. The cruelty of the writing is in the delivery: his decency, his restraint and his love all come to nothing, and the nothing is reported in half a sentence, with no scene at all.

Act four · Wu Song: how a hero walks into being a killer

The most famous stretch of biography in the book starts with a bowl of wine. The tavern below Jingyang Ridge flies a sign reading three bowls and you don't cross. Wu Song drinks eighteen and goes up with a staff, and meets the tiger at the edge of a thicket. His first blow hits a tree and the staff snaps. What follows is this novel at the peak of its powers with physical action: he gets both hands on the animal's head, forces it down into the mud and hits it fifty or sixty times with his right fist. When it is over he is finished too, sitting on a rock getting his breath back and thinking that if a second one came now, that would be the end of him. The tiger fight ends in exhaustion, and that is why it outclasses every set piece of martial-arts fiction that came after it.

It makes him a county constable, and in the same county he finds the elder brother he lost years ago: Wu the Elder, a tiny man who sells steamed cakes and has been handed a beautiful wife, Pan Jinlian, by a local grandee who wanted her out of his house. The moment Wu Song leaves on official business, the teahouse keeper Dame Wang brokers an affair between Pan Jinlian and Ximen Qing, an apothecary; Wu the Elder catches them, takes a kick to the chest, and is finished off in his sickbed with a packet of arsenic. The coroner, suspecting, quietly keeps two blackened bones that would not burn.

Wu Song comes back and the first thing he does is not to kill anyone. It is to file a complaint. He assembles the coroner and a fruit-seller who witnessed the affair, and takes the bones and the testimony to the magistrate. The magistrate has taken Ximen Qing's money, and rules that the body has been cremated, the evidence is insufficient, and the case will not be heard. This is the hinge of the entire novel: a man who respects the law exhausts every legitimate channel and is told, explicitly, that the road is closed. He walks out, buys wine and meat and paper offerings, invites his neighbours, bolts the door, lays a knife on the table and makes one of them write down what is said — what he wants first is not blood, it is a deposition. Only when he has it does he act: he kills Pan Jinlian, runs Ximen Qing down in a tavern on the bridge, and carries both heads to the magistrate to turn himself in.

After his exile to Mengzhou, it curdles. He takes back a tavern district for his patron Shi En, thrashes the strongman who had seized it, and is drawn into the local officials' feuds; the garrison commander pretends to promote him, then plants stolen goods in his room on a festival night. He is run over by legal procedure a second time. On Flying Cloud Bridge, four men are waiting to kill him. He kills all four, stands on the bridge and thinks for a moment, and turns back toward the city.

That night at the Mandarin Duck Pavilion is the coldest passage in Chinese fiction. He kills the groom in the stable, goes upstairs and kills the three men he came for, and then he does not stop. Maidservants, wives, attendants, the girl who had been used as the bait — everyone alive in that compound that night, fifteen people, household and servants alike. His blade notches and he takes another. When it is done he tears a strip from a corpse's robe and writes eight characters on the whitewashed wall in blood:

"The killer is Wu Song, who slew the tiger."— the Mandarin Duck Pavilion, ch. 31 (quoted from the original, in translation)

Notice that in the whole passage the author does not offer a single comment. He does not say Wu Song has lost his mind, or regrets it, or is enjoying it. He records one action after another, like a ledger. The rest of the work is done by the reader: for the first half you are entirely on Wu Song's side, and you stay on his side until you notice you have followed him into a night of killing maidservants, and that the book never once suggested you get off. Afterwards Wu Song takes the disguise of a lay pilgrim, two monk's knives at his belt, and joins Lu Zhishen and Yang Zhi on Twin Dragon Mountain — the three most substantial men in the book, ending up on the same hill.

Act five · Song Jiang: the hand that gathers a hundred and eight

Song Jiang is the contested centre of the book. He cannot fight, has no skill at arms, and is short and dark; his one talent is giving money away and bowing low — everyone who comes to him gets a purse, and he calls himself "this petty clerk" to everybody. It is not a small talent: a hundred and eight people are gathered by that hand. His turning point is also private: his kept woman, Yan Poxi, finds the pouch containing a letter to him from Chao Gai on Mount Liang and tries to blackmail him with it, and he kills her.

On the run he meets Wu Song for the first time at Chai Jin's manor, and in this same stretch commits the ugliest act in the book. To recruit Qin Ming, a government commander, he sends men out in Qin Ming's armour on Qin Ming's horse to burn and kill outside Qingzhou for a night; the prefect responds by executing Qin Ming's entire family. The next day Song Jiang tells him to his face exactly what was done, apologises, and offers him Hua Rong's sister in marriage. Destroy every road back, then be kind to him — this is Song Jiang's recruiting method, and the book uses it more than once. Later, to acquire the officer Zhu Tong, he lets Wu Yong send Li Kui to murder a prefect's four-year-old son, splitting the child's head in two. Zhu Tong chases Li Kui in tears, cannot catch him, and goes up the mountain.

He is slow to go up himself. Lured home by a forged report of his father's death, he is arrested and exiled to Jiangzhou, and there, drunk on the Xunyang Tower, he writes a poem on the whitewash whose last line boasts that he could laugh at Huang Chao for being no man at all.seed ④ Huang Chao was the late-Tang rebel who burned the capital to the ground and never took an amnesty. A man with loyalty permanently in his mouth, drunk, writes that even Huang Chao was not ruthless enough. A minor official copies the poem down and denounces him; he is sentenced to death; Mount Liang storms the execution ground and carries him off. At that point he has nowhere else on earth to be.

Then comes expansion: three assaults on Zhu Family Village, the breaking of the chained cavalry, and the recruitment of Lu Junyi, the richest man in Hebei. That episode says everything about Liangshan's methods: Wu Yong enters the city disguised as a fortune-teller and leaves an acrostic poem on Lu Junyi's wall whose first characters read "Lu Junyi rebels". Once the rumour is running, his steward and his wife denounce him to be rid of him, his property changes hands, and it is his servant Yan Qing who pulls him off the execution ground. The greatest fighter in the empire is delivered to Mount Liang by a poem and his own household staff.

Chao Gai dies at Zengtou Market, a poisoned arrow in the face with the name of the enemy commander cut into the shaft. His last words: whoever takes the man who shot me becomes chief of Liangshan. The man is taken by Lu Junyi, and the chief is still Song Jiang — after a scene of courteous refusals back and forth, courteous enough to make your teeth ache.

Then the climax. Seven days and nights of rites are held before the Hall of Loyalty; on the last night the sky splits, a shaft of gold drives into the earth, and they dig up a stone tablet covered on both faces with tadpole script — thirty-six Heavenly Spirits on one side, seventy-two Earthly Fiends on the other, exactly matching the names and the seating order of the hundred and eight, with four characters across the head of the stone: Act in Heaven's Stead.payoff ① ↑ The hundred and eight streaks of black vapour Marshal Hong let out of the sealed hall come down, seventy chapters later, onto each of these names. What makes the stroke so good is that it is not an explanation but a verdict: the reader has spent seventy chapters watching these people be forced up the mountain step by step and is on the verge of indignation when the author says, casually, that they were demons all along and it was written on a rock before any of it happened.

At the chrysanthemum banquet that autumn, half-drunk, Song Jiang has a new lyric of his sung, and it reaches the line about longing for the emperor to send down a decree of amnesty. Wu Song puts down his bowl first and says that with amnesty today and amnesty tomorrow, his brothers' hearts are going cold. Li Kui's eyes go round and he kicks the table over:

"Amnesty, amnesty — amnesty be damned!"— Li Kui at the chrysanthemum banquet, ch. 71 (quoted from the original, in translation)

Song Jiang, enraged, orders him executed, and is talked out of it. The quietest line is also the deadliest, and it is Lu Zhishen's: the whole court is crooked and the emperor sees nothing, and it is like this black cassock of mine — you can wash it to death and it will never come clean. Amnesty will not do.seed ⑤ The three who object hardest are precisely the three men in the book who never calculate anything for themselves.

Act six · After the amnesty: from the marsh to Fang La, from twenty-seven men to a cup of poison

Two approaches from the court collapse: the first decree is written so arrogantly that Li Kui tears it up on the spot. Then the grand marshal Tong Guan attacks twice and loses; then Gao Qiu comes in person with a fleet, is captured alive, and is untied, seated in the place of honour and escorted home with every courtesy — after which he does not honour a word of what he promised. Three military victories cannot buy one negotiation; the book is not remotely naive about the court. What finally works is a back channel: Yan Qing goes around Gao Qiu and Cai Jing entirely, reaches Emperor Huizong in person through the famous courtesan Li Shishi, and lays the case before him; and Marshal Su comes to the marsh with a real pardon.

Then the campaigns. Against the Liao, not one of the hundred and eight dies — clearly deliberate on the author's part, letting the reader believe for a while that the amnesty really has bought a future. (The 120-chapter recension inserts two further campaigns here.) And then Fang La, the other great rebellion, in the south. That campaign ends Liangshan. Zhang Shun dies in a volley of arrows at a Hangzhou water gate; Qin Ming dies on an enemy halberd; Dong Ping and Zhang Qing die at a mountain pass; one after another, and the author no longer gives anybody a death worth the name — in the first seventy chapters each man got a whole chapter to arrive; now they are struck off like entries in a ledger, and the drop itself is the author's opinion. It is Lu Zhishen who takes Fang La alive.

Passing through Hangzhou on the way home, Lu Zhishen is resting at Liuhe Temple when he hears the Qiantang bore in the middle of the night and starts up, having never heard a tidal wave, thinking it is war drums. A monk tells him it is the tide, on the eighteenth of the eighth month, and that it never fails to keep its appointment. He remembers the second set of lines his old abbot gave him: at the tide, be whole; at the appointed sign, be still.payoff ② ↑ He calls for paper and brush, writes a short verse, has water heated, bathes, changes his robe, sits down in a meditation chair and does not move again. The verse ends:

"The tide comes up the Qiantang; today at last I know that I am I."— Lu Zhishen's death verse, ch. 119 (quoted from the original, in translation)

This is the high point of the book. For a hundred and nineteen chapters everyone has been busy not being himself — Lin Chong being the instructor others required of him, Song Jiang being the loyal subject others expected, Wu Song being an instrument of his brother's revenge — and only Lu Zhishen, who spent his life killing people and setting fires, states plainly at the end who he is, and then goes.

The others scatter. Wu Song loses an arm in the campaign and stays at Liuhe Temple as a monk; Lin Chong is paralysed by a stroke, and Wu Song nurses him for six months until he dies; Yan Qing leaves a letter of resignation urging Lu Junyi to come away with him, and when Lu Junyi refuses, shoulders a load of gold and vanishes; Li Jun feigns illness to be left behind, and later puts to sea and becomes king of Siam — the only man in the book who genuinely escapes this logic is the one who pretended to be sick.

Twenty-seven return to the capital to receive their appointments. Song Jiang is made a regional commissioner at Chuzhou, Lu Junyi another at Luzhou, Li Kui is posted to Runzhou. Gao Qiu and his allies are not finished: they put mercury in Lu Junyi's imperial banquet, and on the journey back he falls from his boat and drowns; then a flask of imperial wine arrives at Chuzhou. Song Jiang drinks, tastes what is in it, and thinks immediately of one thing — Li Kui is at Runzhou, and the moment he hears his brother has been poisoned, he will rebel. He sends for him, gives him wine, and only when the cup is empty tells him what was in it. Li Kui does not curse him. He says only that he served his brother in life and will be a small ghost in his brother's retinue in death. This is the last act of loyalty in the book and also the last murder in it, and they are the same act.payoff ④ ↑ The man who wrote drunk on the Xunyang Tower that Huang Chao was no man at all spent his life doing the exact reverse: Huang Chao burned the capital, Song Jiang burned his own brothers; Huang Chao never took an amnesty, Song Jiang begged for one for twenty years. The man he sneered at is the man he could not be.

Wu Yong and Hua Rong have the same dream, of Song Jiang calling them, and hang themselves together at his grave outside Chuzhou. Huizong learns the truth in a dream of his own, posthumously ennobles Song Jiang and builds a temple where statues of all one hundred and eight stand in good order.payoff ⑤ ↑ The three men who fought hardest against the amnesty at the chrysanthemum banquet end thus: one dead in the lotus position in a temple, one one-armed and refusing to go back to the capital, one poisoned by his own sworn brother. Every one of them was right, and the price of being right was to be buried by this book in as few words as possible.

Put the six acts together and the fuse of the whole novel is a foot — a broken-down loafer catches an emperor's eye and is made marshal of the imperial guard. From that day the rule is: those above may requisition anything by name, your wife, your rank, your good name, and every step of it comes with paperwork. Wang Jin runs, Lin Chong is destroyed, Yang Zhi's career ends on a ridge in the heat, Song Jiang's letter falls into the wrong hands, Lu Junyi's household is dismantled by a poem. Everything bloody that follows is the after-shock of that one kick. And the hardest thing to say about it is this: the same logic that forced all of them up the mountain eventually collected them again, unchanged — the amnesty does not betray the book's premise, it is that premise arriving at its final form.

Three slow-motion replays

1 · Snow at the mountain-god temple: how one snowfall both kills and saves

The craft here is that every object works in two directions at once. The ruined hut is the warden's malice (a posting designed to be fatal) and Lin Chong's salvation (he only leaves because it collapses). The stone against the door is something he set there against the wind, and the reason three men later cannot get in and have to say everything standing outside it. The wine gourd on the spear point is the last small comfort of his daily life, and the only weapon in his hand when the door opens. Not one coincidence is written anywhere; all the luck grows out of the physical properties of things.

What matters more is timing. Before this scene Lin Chong has swallowed three humiliations: the fist he did not throw at the temple festival, the defence he did not make in the White Tiger Hall, and the moment in Wild Boar Forest when, tied to a tree, he asked Lu Zhishen not to kill the escorts. The author pushes the detonation back and back, until Lin Chong hears through a door that the men intend to collect a reward for him in the capital — only when he hears the word "reward" with his own ears does patience become finally meaningless. So the push on the stone is not the peak of his anger; it is the end of his hope. That is the snow's third function: it turns the world white and silent, so that three men's voices are the only sound in it.

2 · Yellow Earth Ridge: locking the reader inside Yang Zhi's eyes

The robbery runs a full chapter, and not one word of it tells the reader who the seven date merchants are. The point of view is bolted to Yang Zhi: we resent the heat with him, suspect the porters with him, interrogate the strangers with him, weigh again and again with him whether the men may buy that wine. Every judgement he makes is correct — guard the afternoon, not the morning; watch strangers drink first and then decide — he does the job better than anyone else could, and loses anyway. Only after the men are down and the treasure is gone does the narration step back and explain, in one cool paragraph, how the drug was administered and who these seven people were.

Classical vernacular fiction almost never does this. It means the author could tell the difference between suspense and the reader's active participation: you have to be fooled alongside Yang Zhi so that the reveal gives you two feelings at once — admiration for Wu Yong and pity for Yang Zhi. And note how well the props are chosen: summer, noon, a hill road, thirst. The trick requires no moral weakness at all, only a bodily need — fifteen trained soldiers are broken by being thirsty.

3 · The Mandarin Duck Pavilion: the author says nothing, and you find you cannot get off

Look at the sentence lengths: the passage is built of very short action clauses, one movement after another, no subordination, no interiority, no atmosphere — closer to stage directions than to prose. The blade notches and he takes another; that is a note about tooling, not about feeling.

It is exactly this flatness that produces the moral vertigo. Had the author written "by now Wu Song's eyes were red with killing", the reader would step ashore at once and start judging. He does not write it, so the reader stays on the boat. By the time you register that the girl was only bait used by her master, Wu Song has killed her too, and you read it without a stumble. This is the most powerful and most dangerous thing about Water Margin: it does not argue you into sympathy with violence, it lets you enjoy it first and then lets you notice what you were enjoying. And enjoyment has momentum: having cheered him for killing Ximen Qing, you ride the same feeling into cheering him for killing a maidservant — once pleasure has been authorised by a sense of justice, it stops checking its object. Every online pile-on that feels richly deserved starts from a real injustice too; the loss of control is always at step two. The eight bloody characters on the wall close the technique off: Wu Song signs the work because he still holds one conviction — what I did I own, and I am not a thief. Nearly every tragedy in this book compresses into that sentence: they all believe they are the party in the right, and most of the time they actually are.

The book's central device

A machine called "being forced"

The first seventy chapters are not structured as a novel but as a chain of novellas: Shi Jin hands off to Lu Zhishen, Lu Zhishen to Lin Chong, Lin Chong to Yang Zhi, Yang Zhi to Chao Gai, Chao Gai to Song Jiang, Song Jiang to Wu Song, each man's story delivering the next man onstage as it ends. The technique comes straight from the storyteller's pitch: one life per evening, a new one tomorrow, so the audience never tires.

What makes it a book rather than a collection is that every bead on the string holds a different version of the same event: a person who still wants to live decently has his options removed one at a time. That is what the table above is for — the means differ every time (power, law, accident, indifference, rumour), so seventy chapters never grow monotonous; the conclusion is identical every time, so after seventy chapters the reader holds a conviction: this country has no other exit.

The device inverts: the victims become the machine

The real depth of this book is not the slogan about officials driving the people to revolt. It is that it dares to show Liangshan becoming that same machine. Qin Ming's wife and children are killed by Song Jiang's stratagem. The four-year-old in front of Zhu Tong is killed by Li Kui. Hu Sanniang's family is wiped out by Li Kui after they surrender, and she is then given by Song Jiang in marriage to a notorious lecher, without a single line about whether she consents. Lu Junyi, Xu Ning and the surgeon An Daoquan are all tricked up the mountain. In other words: people whose choices were taken from them, once they have power, take other people's choices first, using the same vocabulary — this is for your own good, brother. A book in praise of rebellion that is simultaneously a book about how rebels turn into the thing they rebelled against — that is why it has lasted six hundred years.

The stone tablet: a rock for offloading responsibility

Nailing the hundred and eight to a roster of star-spirits closes the narrative circle, but as thought it is a retreat. If these people were demons by birth, then Gao Qiu hounding Lin Chong to ruin and a magistrate refusing Wu Song's complaint drop to the status of scenery in a fate that was fixed anyway: the state need not take full responsibility, and neither need the outlaws. In fairness this is not simply cowardice — a book that asks its readers to sympathise with rebels needs an amulet if it is to survive in circulation. But read it clearly: this novel is at its strongest where it is sociological and at its weakest where it is fatalistic, and the two are tied to the same rock.

"Act in Heaven's Stead" already contains the ending

The four characters on that yellow banner have been read as a rebel slogan for centuries. They are the most conservative sentence in the book. Heaven, in this vocabulary, is never abstract justice; it is first of all the Son of Heaven. To act in Heaven's stead means: the emperor himself is good, the evil is in the ministers around him, and we have not come up the mountain to replace him but to clean his house for him. Once that premise is granted, the amnesty is not a betrayal but the logically necessary terminus — having conceded that you act on his behalf, on what grounds do you refuse when his decree tells you to come home?

Li Kui is the only one who punctures this. More than once he bellows that they should march on the capital and put Song Jiang on the throne. He is the only character in the book who actually wants a revolution, and he is written as an unreasoning brute, and he dies at the hands of the man he was loyal to. That is not a lapse on the author's part. It is his heaviest stroke.

What it forces you to think about

Question 1
Is the amnesty a betrayal, or the only road out?

Take Song Jiang's side first, and put it more strongly than his defenders do. Liangshan is not a state; it is a provisional organisation supplied by robbery, legitimated by a rock, and passing power by a scene of ritual refusal. It has beaten three imperial armies, and it will not beat the tenth — however rotten the Song dynasty is, it is an empire of tens of millions, and the marsh is a marsh. It has no production, no registers, no arrangement for the next generation; ten thousand people on that mountain have to eat after each campaign, and what they eat is taken from the peasants around them. Worse, it has no future tense: when Chao Gai dies, one vacancy requires a whole performance — so what happens after Song Jiang? He can see more than most readers credit him with: he holds a hundred and eight lives, plus their families, their names, and whether they will be buried in their ancestral ground. The amnesty is the only version of a decent ending he can win for them — not cowardice, but a leader facing a room with no exits and deciding to spend his own reputation to buy his brothers a standing.

Now the other side, at full strength. His counterparty is the very group that forced these men up the mountain in the first place; Gao Qiu was captured once and courteously sent home, and Cai Jing and Tong Guan are never touched at all. You cannot argue that the system is so rotten it left you no choice but outlawry, and then stake everything on that system's credit. And so it goes: three campaigns spend their lives, and of the twenty-seven who reach the capital, the two most loyal are poisoned. What is more, the thing being traded away is other people's lives, and only one person decides. Three men objected in public at the chrysanthemum banquet, and his answer was an order to execute one of them. He fought for their standing; he never once asked whether they wanted it.

There is a distinction that is easy to slide past: trading with the system to save a group of people and trading with the system so that your own choices are ratified by history look identical from outside. Which is Song Jiang? The book declines to say, and leaves two fingerprints: the poem on the Xunyang Tower, and the last cup given to Li Kui. If it were only about his brothers, Li Kui could live. There is exactly one reason he must not: alive, he would ruin the word loyalty.

Bring it forward. Any group outside an institution, once it reaches a certain size, meets this question — do we let ourselves be absorbed? Absorption brings resources, legitimacy and long-term safety, and the price is handing over the thing that made people believe you in the first place. And the hard part is this: people who are being absorbed never feel they are surrendering. They feel they are scaling up.

Question 2
What exactly was wrong with Lin Chong's patience — how long should anyone endure?

Defend him first, until nobody can answer it. Each individual act of endurance is correct: not throwing the punch at the temple, because striking the Marshal's son would make his wife a widow by nightfall; not defending himself in the White Tiger Hall, because arguing in that room adds to the charge; asking Lu Zhishen not to kill the escorts, because killing them would end any chance of going home. Every step is spent protecting something that is being taken from him — wife, rank, good name, the possibility of return. Have him flip the table at the first insult and he loses all four immediately. Patience is not cowardice; patience is the only technique available to a person with something to lose.

The counter-argument: every concession lowers the cost of the aggressor's next move. Gao's son suffers nothing the first time he is interrupted, so the second time he has the woman lured to a private room; Gao Qiu learns that one manufactured charge can remove an instructor, so he manufactures a fire. Lin Chong believes he is protecting those four things; what he is actually doing is publishing a price list — this man's floor can be lowered one more notch. By the night at the temple he has lost all four before he acts, and the force of his explosion lands in a vacuum: the three men he kills buy back none of the things he was trying to keep.

So is the answer "break earlier"? No. The real question is harder: at what point can you tell whether this concession is a buffer or the first step down a slope? The person inside it cannot get that information; all Lin Chong ever has is "perhaps once more and it will pass". This is why the character is still uncomfortable to read — frozen out by a boss, escalated against a little at a time by a counterparty, tested at the edges over and over by an intimate partner, most of us are being Lin Chong, with excellent reasons. If one usable rule can be taken from it: endurance only works while it is buying time; once each new attack costs the other side nothing, endurance has stopped being a strategy and become a delay. His error was not enduring. It was never counting: in this whole procedure, what has the other side lost so far? The answer is nothing.

Question 3
The radius of brotherhood: may you destroy people outside the circle for the sake of those inside it?

Qin Ming's wife and children; the four-year-old in front of Zhu Tong; Hu Sanniang's family; the maids and grooms in the Mandarin Duck Pavilion; the spectators Li Kui cuts down at the Jiangzhou execution ground. They have one thing in common: none of them is inside the circle.

Put brotherhood at its strongest first, because it is nothing so simple as savagery. In a world where the state itself is the predator, the only social contract available to ordinary people is a private promise: Lu Zhishen empties his purse for a father and daughter he has never met, Chai Jin shelters every fugitive who reaches his gate, Dai Zong risks execution to carry a letter. It works — it holds up a secondary order, one you can trust with your life, exactly where the state has failed. It moves people to act faster than abstract justice ever will, because it has names and faces.

Then the invoice. The power of a private promise comes precisely from its closure: because it is owed only to the inside, it can be unconditional — and the other face of unconditional is that costs outside the circle can be externalised without limit. Li Kui is the pure form of the logic: his loyalty to Song Jiang has no impurities in it, and because it has none he kills outsiders without hesitation, the four-year-old being merely one step in a job his brother needs done. He is not the corrupt version of brotherhood. He is its limit case.

This one comes up several times a day, minus the blood: covering for the team by pushing blame onto a partner; protecting a friend by staying quiet about something plainly wrong; a company externalising the cost onto its users for the sake of "our people". "Our people" is a phrase with an adjustable radius, and all the cruelty happens outside it. There is only one useful question: the last thing I did for "our people" — who paid for it, and did that person appear anywhere in my thinking?

Recurring obsessions, and the books this one produced

Since the authorship is in doubt, change the question: what does this book keep returning to? Three things, and they fight each other. One is the obsession with roads being closed: its despair about institutions is intensely concrete, always located in procedure — a document, a roll call, a verdict that has been paid for. Two is an appetite for the visceral: fists, blades, big bowls of wine; nearly every aesthetic high point is a matter of bodily force, which makes it the least refined and the warmest of the great Chinese novels. Three is nostalgia for order: having exposed the court completely, it cannot let go of the word loyalty, and in the end walks all one hundred and eight men back inside the institution to die. None of the three will yield, so the book is permanently split — it teaches you to rebel and it urges you to go home.

Its descendants are livelier than most books' own careers. The Golden Lotus branches directly off the Wu Song chapters: keep Pan Jinlian and Ximen Qing, let Wu Song come back a few years later, and you have the first long Chinese novel about domestic life and appetite — the pivot of Chinese fiction from romance to social realism was snapped off a limb of this tree. In the early Qing, Chen Chen wrote Later Water Margin, in which the survivor Li Jun really does found a kingdom overseas. In 1847 Yu Wanchun wrote Quelling the Bandits, continuing from the seventy-chapter version and having the imperial army exterminate all one hundred and eight — a novel important enough that someone wrote a whole novel to destroy it, which is rare in any literature. A further branch runs into martial-arts fiction: the ideal of the "great knight-errant" in Jin Yong is a redefinition of Act in Heaven's Stead, taking Heaven away from the emperor and returning it to ordinary people.

Criticism and controversy

  • The women. The hardest thing to defend. Named women fall into roughly three groups: the adulteresses (Pan Jinlian, Yan Poxi, Pan Qiaoyun, Lu Junyi's wife), all butchered in detail far exceeding anything given to male villains; the fighters (Sun Erniang, Gu Dasao, Hu Sanniang), written as effectively sexless; and instruments. Hu Sanniang's family is annihilated by Li Kui after surrendering, and she is then handed in marriage to a man known for lechery, with not one line about her will. "A product of its time" does not cover this — The Golden Lotus, from the same tradition, is far more attentive to what women's lives actually were.
  • Indiscriminate killing. Li Kui cutting down rows of spectators at the Jiangzhou execution ground; a four-year-old murdered to recruit Zhu Tong; Song Jiang staging a massacre in Qin Ming's name and thereby getting the man's whole family executed. These are not the villains' acts, they are the protagonists', and the book supplies almost no moral comment on them. Readers who take Water Margin for a story about justice have usually stopped around chapter forty.
  • Contested values. The argument about the amnesty has run since the Ming: the critic Li Zhi praised the book's loyalty; Jin Shengtan judged Song Jiang a hypocrite and deleted everything after chapter seventy. The most widely known modern intervention came in 1975, when Mao Zedong's remarks on Water Margin were published in the People's Daily and elsewhere — including the line that the book's merit is that it shows capitulation, and that it should serve as negative teaching material so the people can recognise capitulationists — and a nationwide campaign of criticism of the novel followed. Stated here as fact, without judgement — but it does show that a six-hundred-year-old novel was still being used as a field of contemporary political argument very recently.
  • Which book you read. "I've read Water Margin" can mean three different books: the hundred-chapter version (with the Liao and Fang La campaigns), the hundred-and-twenty-chapter version (with two more campaigns), and Jin Shengtan's seventy chapters (no amnesty, no ending). Because Jin's text was dominant for three centuries, the Water Margin in many readers' heads stops at the heroes taking their seats. What you think of this novel depends very largely on which of them you read.
  • Bans and translations. It was repeatedly proscribed under the Ming and Qing as a book that "teaches banditry". In English, Pearl Buck's title All Men Are Brothers drew objections at the time — Liangshan is rigidly hierarchical, with the seating order settling precedence, which sits awkwardly with the phrase; Shapiro's Outlaws of the Marsh is closer to the sense, though it too is abridged. Chinese and English readers discussing this novel are, strictly speaking, not discussing the same book.

Ten sentences that stay

1. The first villain in this book is not a bandit but a street loafer made marshal — from beginning to end it argues that the rot starts at the top.

2. Every one of Lin Chong's concessions was correct, and together they cost him everything he was trying to keep.

3. Wu Song filed the complaint first. The hardest line in the novel is not written in blood; it is a magistrate saying the evidence is insufficient and the case will not be heard.

4. The token of allegiance is the book's thesis: to come through this door, first do something you can never come back from.

5. What broke fifteen trained soldiers on Yellow Earth Ridge was not a stratagem but thirst — the best cons need only a bodily need.

6. The author offers no comment on the Mandarin Duck Pavilion, because comment would let you off the boat; he wants you to notice on your own what you were enjoying.

7. "Act in Heaven's Stead" already contains the amnesty: Heaven is first of all the emperor, and if you act on his behalf, what grounds have you to refuse his summons?

8. The deepest layer is not officials driving people to revolt; it is people whose choices were taken turning round and taking other people's.

9. Song Jiang calls Li Kui in to share the poison because a name matters more than a man — the book says this out loud exactly once.

10. Of the hundred and eight, only Lu Zhishen states in the sound of the tide who he actually is, and then sits down. The rest spend their lives being the person somebody else required.
