Novels, Deep-Read · Juan Rulfo · No.40

Pedro Páramo

Pedro Páramo · Juan Rulfo · 1955

中文 →

A man is walking downhill at noon in August. The road is white. On either side the ground is too hot to hold anything living. The town he is going to is called Comala, and his mother told him on her deathbed that it is green there, that the wind smells of ripened grass, that in the mornings the mist hangs over the roofs like gauze.
He keeps descending. It keeps getting hotter. The man driving the burros ahead of him says: down there, señor. That's it.
And he sees it: a town with no lit window anywhere, half its houses fallen in, nobody in the street, hot as the back of a griddle left on the fire.

This page contains complete spoilers — including the fact that Juan Preciado dies halfway through the book, that everyone in Comala is already dead, who puts the knife into Pedro Páramo, and the last sentence of the novel. The premise of this site is that everything worth discussing lives past the ending. If you want to keep the suspense, read the book first — it is barely more than a hundred pages, an afternoon's work. This page will wait.

In one sentence

A man keeps a promise to his dying mother and goes to a town called Comala to collect a debt from the father he has never met — only to discover on the way that his father died years ago, that the whole town died with him, and that he is going to die there too; the second half of the book is that man lying in a grave, listening to Comala's dead assemble, in fragments, the one story they have: how their town was built by a man's greed and then cancelled by the same man's grief.

Coordinates

Juan Rulfo (1917–1986) came from the state of Jalisco in western Mexico. His father was killed when he was small; his mother died a few years later; he was raised in an orphanage in Guadalajara. Writing was never his job. He sold tires, worked as a travelling salesman, worked for a government river-basin development agency, and spent years at the National Indigenist Institute editing books. He was also a superb photographer — empty ground, broken walls, crosses, people made small by the landscape around them.

He published two works of fiction in his life: El Llano en llamas (1953), a book of stories, and Pedro Páramo (1955). Then nothing. Thirty more years, no third book.

It sold slowly and the early reviews were not warm. Almost all of its standing was conferred retroactively by the writers who came after it. Gabriel García Márquez said in public more than once that this book hit him decisively and that he could very nearly recite it; Susan Sontag wrote the introduction to the 1994 English translation. In the filing system of literary history it sits under "the source of magical realism" — a label that fits it badly, for reasons taken up below. A more accurate description: a hundred-and-something-page novel that pushes one idea, the narrator is already dead, further than anyone had pushed it.

The story

Act One · A man goes looking for his father

Dolores Preciado is dying. She makes her son Juan Preciado promise one thing: go to Comala, find the man called Pedro Páramo — his father — and take back what is owed to the two of them. Not ask. Take. That man forgot her for a lifetime, and she wants her son to make him pay for it.

She gives him something else at the same time: a picture of a town. Her Comala is green, it smells of ripe wheat on the plain, it has mist in the early morning, it has rain. She says it over and over, and he learns it by heart. Seed ①

After she dies, Juan sets out. He did not really want to go — by his own account he agreed the way you agree to things, and what actually moved him were the daydreams that kept coming back. On the road down he meets a muleteer, Abundio Martínez. Seed ② They talk. Who are you going to see? Pedro Páramo. The muleteer laughs: then I'm his son too. Everybody around here is.

Then comes the line that sets the key of the whole book:

"He is living bile."— Abundio, describing Pedro Páramo, opening pages (Spanish: un rencor vivo; English versions render it very differently)

And then: he's been dead for years.

The town is below. The heat comes up out of the ground. There is nothing green anywhere — no grass, no mist, no living person. The street is empty enough that Juan can hear his own footsteps come back off the walls. Someone points him to a house: go to Eduviges Dyada.

Eduviges opens the door and the first thing she says is that his mother told her he was coming. Juan stops — his mother is dead, and nobody carried any message. Eduviges says, quite calmly, yes, she was just speaking to me. Seed ③

She also tells him an old story. On the night Pedro Páramo married Dolores, the woman in his bed was Eduviges. Dolores was indisposed and asked her to take her place for the one night, and she did. Which means that the owner of the room Juan is now sitting in came within one night of being his mother.

Then Damiana Cisneros appears, an old servant from the Media Luna, the Páramo estate, come to take him in. As they walk he cannot stop himself from asking: are you alive? The question is barely out of his mouth before she is gone and he is standing alone in an empty street.

He is starting to understand where he has walked into. There is no living person in this town. Everyone he has met is dead, and they either do not know it or do not consider it worth mentioning. The air is full of murmurs — half-sentences, weeping, conversations leaking out of walls and out of rooms with nobody in them.

He is taken in, finally, by a brother and sister. The brother is Donis; the sister has lived with him as his wife for years. She tells Juan a bishop came through once and would not absolve them, and that her sin has grown on her like a crust. The room has no window. It is like being inside a steamer. The brother goes out and does not come back.

That night the murmurs close in. Not as a figure of speech — in this book murmurs have weight. Juan cannot get air. He goes out into the street to find some and the street is thicker with them. He goes down.

And the novel does not tell you. It simply carries on, into other people and other decades, as though nothing had happened.

Act Two · Two people in a grave

Pages later, in the middle of a conversation, the reader is finally let in on it: Juan Preciado is lying in a grave, and there is someone lying next to him, an old beggar woman named Dorotea. She asks how he died. He says the murmurs suffocated him, that fright killed him. She corrects him: you're dead all right, but don't flatter yourself that fright did it — there was no way back from the moment you walked in.

This is the structural cut of the whole novel. The first-person narrator dies, and the first person does not stop. Everything that felt wrong in the first half — the empty street, the answers that don't match the questions, the time that never lines up — is explained here, all at once: what you have been reading is the memory of the dead.

Dorotea tells him her own story. When she was alive she went around the town with a bundle in her arms, telling anyone who would listen that it was her baby. Everyone laughed at her. Seed ⑤

And in the grave next to theirs lies a woman who never stops talking — about her husband's body, about the sea, about a deep hole she was once lowered into. Her name is Susana San Juan. Dorotea tells Juan: the person you're listening to is the reason this town died.

The rest of the book is those voices, coming up through the ground, assembling one man's life.

Act Three · How a man in debt became the weather

Back up several decades. A boy sits in his family's outhouse doing nothing, his head entirely full of a girl — Susana San Juan. Her hair. The way she looked in the water. An afternoon they spent flying a kite. His grandmother calls him in to eat. It is the only moment in the book where Pedro Páramo resembles an ordinary person.

Then his father, Lucas Páramo, is killed at a wedding by a bullet that was not meant for him. What is left is the Media Luna and a mountain of debt.

The estate's manager, Fulgor Sedano, comes to the young heir expecting to have to listen to nonsense. Instead Pedro asks two questions. Who do we owe the most to? — the Preciados; their daughter is called Dolores. Then go and ask for her hand.

That is the man's entire method, demonstrated once and never varied: turn a debt you have to pay into a marriage you get to make. From that moment Fulgor is his. The wedding happens, the debt evaporates, Dolores has a son and is then sent away — packed off to her sister's, never to return. She raises the boy elsewhere and spends the rest of her life telling him how green Comala is.

The remaining land goes the same efficient way. Where a boundary is disputed, the man disputing it is invited to talk, and if the talk fails he is not seen again. One passage does it very lightly: Fulgor reports that somebody won't give up his land, Pedro says do what you have to, and in the next fragment the man is gone. Rulfo never writes the killing. He writes the minute before and the year after, and leaves the frame between them empty — empty in the particular way of a place where people have stopped expecting it to be filled in.

He wants the land and the water and the people, but not in order to be rich. There is exactly one thing he wants, and it is named San Juan.

Act Four · Miguel, the priest, and how a town loses absolution

Pedro acknowledges one son: Miguel Páramo. Miguel is seventeen, rides a horse called the Colorado, goes out at night, forces himself on girls, kills a man, comes home for breakfast. His father's attitude is not indulgence, it is bookkeeping: something happens, it gets paid for, it gets buried, next.

Miguel dies when the horse throws him. The horse comes back to the estate alone.

And now the moral engine of the book starts. The parish priest is Father Rentería. Among the girls Miguel assaulted was Rentería's own niece, Ana; the town believes Miguel also killed the priest's brother. It is under those conditions that Pedro Páramo puts a handful of gold coins into his hand and asks him to do what needs doing for Miguel — the rites, the absolution, a clean burial.

Rentería takes it. Seed ④

The problem is not that one transaction. The problem is that once the accounting exists it becomes the town's constitution: those who can pay can be absolved, and those who cannot, cannot. A woman's daughter dies and she has no money for the mass, and the priest shakes his head. Eduviges was a suicide, and under the rules of the time she could not receive what she needed, and he made no exception for her — so she stays in her room, waiting for a guest who may never arrive. Payoff ③ ↑ What makes "your mother told me you were coming" so cold is not that a ghost said it. It is that the waiting is her sentence, and the man who sentenced her is the one who took the money.

Rentería cannot live with it. He goes to the neighbouring town of Contla to confess — one priest kneeling in front of another. The priest of Contla hears him out, offers him no comfort and no way around it, and refuses him absolution. Payoff ④ ↑ The beauty of the move is this: the only time in the entire novel that anyone follows the rules exactly, the rules are being used to convict the man who sold them. He spent a career trading absolution, and when he needs to buy some, the counter is closed.

The same passage quietly explains the book's basic premise — why Comala's dead all stay put, muttering underground. The town is not haunted. The town is collectively unabsolved. The murmurs are not atmosphere. They are unpaid accounts.

Act Five · Susana San Juan, the thing that could not be bought

Many years later Susana comes back. Her father, Bartolomé San Juan, brings her to Comala looking for work and walks her straight into Pedro's hand. Pedro has waited most of his life for this. He has land now, and money, and men with guns, and he can simply have her brought to him. He tells Fulgor that Susana San Juan will be living at the Media Luna. As for her father — the novel never shows you how Bartolomé dies. It only makes sure you know that Pedro wanted him gone, and then he was gone.

Susana moves in. She does become his wife. And for the first time in his life Pedro Páramo buys something and receives nothing, because Susana has lost her mind.

Her madness is not the quiet kind. She talks all day, and what she talks about is the body of her dead husband Florencio — in detail, physically, without any shame at all. She lies in that house and Pedro sits outside the door listening to his wife describe another man. He owns every single thing in this town except the man inside her head.

There is a deeper hole in her memory. As a child her father lowered her on a rope into a mine and told her to bring up whatever she found; what she found in the dark was a skeleton, while the voice above her kept telling her to bring it up. Her mind has been hanging on a rope in a hole since she was a girl — and the estate Pedro spent forty years assembling does not reach that far down.

When she is dying, Father Rentería tries to get her to repent, to say the words that would let her be sent off properly. The words she says are not those words. They are still about the body, the sea, the man.

Then the bells. On the day of her burial the bells of Comala ring, and go on ringing, far past what the occasion calls for. People in the surrounding villages hear them and assume something big is happening in Comala, and they come. More and more of them. Vendors come. Entertainers come. Drinkers come. The deepest grief of Pedro Páramo's life is celebrated in his own town as a fair.

He sits in his chair and watches it. And then he says the sentence the whole book rests on:

"I will fold my arms and Comala will starve to death."— Pedro Páramo, after Susana's burial (English translations word it differently)

And he does it. He stops renting land, stops lending, stops hiring, stops attending to anything. He does nothing at all — he simply stops. The fields go back to scrub, the people leave, and the ones who cannot leave die. That green Comala with the mist and the rain, the one his mother described to her son all her life Payoff ① ↑, turns out not to have been an old woman's filter or a boy's illusion at all: it really existed, and it was cancelled by one man's bereavement. The oven Juan walks into is the corpse of his mother's town. What makes this hard is the direction it runs: a page of tender nostalgia at the front of the book is retroactively converted into a death certificate.

Act Six · The revolution gets bought; the last knife does not

The Mexican Revolution reaches the district. Armed men ride into the Media Luna to settle accounts with Pedro Páramo. He sits them down and asks who they are fighting for and how much they need. He gives them money, and he gives them men — he sends one of his own, El Tilcuate, to join up, officially as a recruit and actually as his ear. Fulgor, the manager, is killed in the disorder.

After that, the war reaches Pedro as periodic briefings from El Tilcuate: we're with these ones now, now those ones, now so-and-so is winning. In this book the revolution is not a historical turning point. It is a line item. Even Father Rentería finally picks up a rifle and joins the Catholic rising (the Cristero War, 1926–29, between the Church and the government). The country changes hands; Comala goes on emptying at the same rate.

Dorotea, down in the grave, settles her own account too. The bundle she carried all her life had no child in it. She says she later dreamt she went to heaven and was told there: you were never given a son. Payoff ⑤ ↑ The other half is worse. The children she really did handle while alive were other people's daughters — she was the one who found girls for Miguel Páramo. The woman going from door to door with an empty bundle, saying she has a baby, is the same woman delivering other women's babies to be ruined. Rulfo puts the most pitiable and the most sordid thing in the book inside one person, and does not comment.

The end. Pedro is old and mostly emptied out, sitting in a chair by his door. A man comes weaving up: Abundio Martínez, the muleteer, deaf now, and very drunk, because his wife has just died and he does not have the money to bury her. He has come to ask for some. He probably does not entirely know what he is doing. There is a knife in his hand, and he uses it. Payoff ② ↑

This is the man from the first act. The one who walked the narrator into the book is the one who ends it — and the order is reversed: he kills Pedro Páramo first, and gives Juan Preciado directions on the road afterwards. He also said that everyone around here is Pedro Páramo's son, so the man who kills this father is one of the many sons he never acknowledged, doing it not out of revenge but for funeral money. (Both figures are named Abundio Martínez, and they are generally read as the same man, though some readers have noted that the details do not perfectly align.)

Damiana hears and starts screaming. Pedro pushes himself up and tries to take a few steps.

"He crumbled as if he were a heap of stones."— the last line of the novel (Spanish: se fue desmoronando como si fuera un montón de piedras)

In Spanish there is one more layer. Pedro is the stone; páramo is the barren waste, the wasteland. The man's name has been telling you how he would die, and what he would leave behind, since the first page.

Put the six acts end to end and the whole novel runs off a single fuse, and the fuse is not an atrocity — it is a boy in an outhouse thinking about a girl. Pedro Páramo swallows land, lends money, marries his largest creditor's daughter, kills the men in his way, buys off a revolution and raises an animal for a son, and all of it is instrumentation toward one end: getting Susana San Juan. He gets her, and finds another man living in her head. She dies, and he cancels an entire town as her grave goods. Comala was not destroyed by tyranny. It was destroyed by the backwash of a love that was never returned — and Juan Preciado is just a stranger who walks into that backwash forty years later.

Who is talking, and are they dead

The book's hardest quality is that it never signals. Here is the first half laid out — note the last row.

Three slow-motion replays

1 · The death of Juan Preciado: killing your narrator without disturbing the reader

The standard method is to give the moment a marker: he fell, everything went black, end of chapter. Rulfo gives none of them. Heat, a room with no window, murmurs pressing in from every side, a man going outside for air — and then the narration slides off, into other decades and other mouths. No blackout, no chapter break, not one full stop weighted more heavily than the others. The truth leaks out pages later inside a piece of small talk: he is in a grave, and an old woman is talking to him.

What makes this good is not the reversal. It is that the reader goes through exactly what Juan went through: he too walked around Comala for a long time before working out that he was among the dead. Getting lost in the narration and getting lost in the town are the same act of getting lost. Plenty of novels claim that form is content. This one does it, and does it with almost no effort — purely by declining to say.

2 · The refusal at Contla: a confession run backwards

The conventional way to stage a confession is a sinner on his knees and a priest behind the grille. Rulfo moves the priest to the kneeling side.

Rentería says what he has done: taken the money, waved the Páramos through, shaken his head at people who could not pay. His self-defence is genuinely strong, and it is the same one used by everyone who stays inside a rotten institution: this parish is destitute, who exactly is keeping the church open? If I refuse, the next man takes it anyway and sleeps better than I do. The priest of Contla does not engage with any of it. He does not argue, does not scold, does not lecture. He just does not absolve him. The one time in the whole novel that someone applies the rules strictly, they are applied to the man who spent a lifetime bending them.

The technique worth stealing is that Rulfo does not comment, at all. He does not tell you Rentería is pitiful, and he does not tell you he is contemptible. He shows him getting up, walking out, going back to his own town and continuing to be its priest. No punishment and no release — just turning up for work the next morning, which is worse than either.

3 · The rope in the mine: madness written as grammar

Susana's sections are the hardest and the best writing in the book. A mad woman is usually done from outside: her eyes are unfocused, her answers don't match. Rulfo does not stand outside. He hands over her language directly, and deletes the boundary between tenses — the rope from thirty years ago and the bedsheet of this moment occupy the same sentence; her father's voice calling down and her husband's body occupy the same paragraph. The well itself is written with extreme economy: the rope, the lamp, the dark, the thing her foot touches, then the skeleton. Not one adjective in the passage is doing the reader's fear for them.

Why that is the right choice: madness is not fundamentally absurd content, it is time refusing to queue. Describe the symptoms for a chapter and the reader sees a case file; let her talk for two pages with the order collapsed and the reader has spent two pages inside her head. Which is precisely the place Pedro Páramo can never get to. He can buy the surface of this town. He cannot buy that well.

The central device: turning "dead" from an event into a position

There is no science-fictional premise here to be tested, but there is a very precise machine. It has three rules.

Rule one: everyone is speaking from after their death, and nobody announces it. The words "ghost" and "spirit" do not appear, and no character is assigned to explain. Death is not a plot event in this book; it is a narrating position. Where other novels choose first person or third, this one chooses from the grave.

Rule two: because the position is a grave, time does not queue. The seventy-odd fragments have no chapters, no numbers, no dates, and swap person freely. This is not avant-garde decoration — memory underground has no sequence. Whoever remembers something says it, and gets talked over by the weeping from the next plot. The reader's confusion is not a side effect; it is the experience being transmitted.

Rule three: the mechanism that keeps them there is religious, and a landlord has bought it. The dead are stuck in Comala for a specific reason: they were not absolved. Who gets absolved is decided by Father Rentería, and Father Rentería's budget is decided by Pedro Páramo. The supernatural in this book has an administrative process, and the process has a price list. That rule is the core of the machine and the single biggest difference between this novel and the magical realism it supposedly fathered.

Which brings up the label. Pedro Páramo is enshrined as the source of magical realism and is itself barely magical: no woman ascends into the sky, no rain falls for four years, there are no yellow butterflies. Its metaphysics is the ordinary common sense of rural Catholicism — purgatory, absolution, masses said for the dead — and in that world the dead speaking is not a marvel, it is expected. García Márquez turned this material into something festive and enormous. Rulfo's original is dry, short and withholding. Read backwards through One Hundred Years of Solitude, the book loses half its cold.

And, honestly, which parts are literary need rather than logic: the rules are not consistent, and Rulfo has no interest in making them so. Murmurs suffocate a living man, which no coherent ghost-physics would permit. Some of the dead know they are dead and some have no idea. Juan and Dorotea converse, while other dead can only talk past each other. Treat it as a system to be reasoned about and the book falls apart in your hands. It is not after consistency; it is after a pressure it can hold for a hundred and twenty pages. This is a poem-shaped novel, not a premise-shaped one — knowing which ruler to use on it is most of the job.

What it makes you think about

Question 1
The debt your mother sent you to collect — is it your debt?

Juan Preciado did not choose this trip. He was given an instruction: go and take back what was taken from your mother. He has never met the man, never spent a day in Comala, and not one peso of that account was ever taken from him. He goes. He dies there.

Take the mother's side first, at full strength. A debt nobody ever comes to collect is a second robbery. Time has always been the perpetrator's best weapon — live long enough, and if the next generation declines to take up the matter, the thing done simply zeroes out. Every reckoning, trial, reparation and oral history rests on descendants pressing claims on behalf of the dead; if each generation says "this has nothing to do with me," the victim's silence becomes the final verdict. Dolores is not sending her son for money. She is sending him so that man does not get to win this cheaply.

Now the other side, at full strength. Juan never manages to say the line — the man he came to collect from died years ago, and the only thing left to inherit is the poison. What his mother handed him was not a task but a position: the position of the wronged. He has never seen Comala, yet he was issued a full set of memories, feelings and grievances about it, and he walked into a place that killed him carrying them. And the green Comala was already gone. What he inherited was not even a real debt — it was a hallucination about a past.

Nearly every family has a small version of this: your parents haven't spoken to a relative in thirty years — do you keep not speaking to them? A people was wronged; how many generations should carry the invoice? Memory is a necessity; whether the right to press a claim is heritable is a different question. Rulfo declines to answer, but leaves one uncomfortable observation: everyone stuck in place in this book has the same symptom — they cannot stop going back over it.

Question 2
"I'll do nothing" — can inaction be an atrocity?

Pedro Páramo does not destroy Comala with a gun. He has killed men, but killing takes out individuals. What takes out the whole town is him sitting down in a chair and folding his arms.

His side first, and it deserves to be argued better than his defenders argue it. He signed nothing. Nobody is obliged to go on provisioning other people indefinitely; ceasing to rent land, lend money and hire hands is the withdrawal of giving, not the commission of theft. And consider what had just happened to him: on the day the only person he ever loved was buried, his town heard the bells and turned up for a fair. A man treated that way by his own town at the worst hour of his life, and thereafter refusing to do that town any more favours, is emotionally intelligible. Classifying "I will no longer help you" as an atrocity slides somewhere frightening: it implies that any act of giving silently converts into a lifetime contract.

Now the other side. That defence assumes the two parties began as equals and that what he provided was a kindness. In fact he spent forty years moving every field, every water source and every alternative livelihood in the district into his own name. When a man has manufactured the dependency, his "stopping" is not neutral — it is the closing move of the taking. Fill in all the wells in a village and then announce that you are no longer delivering water and that this is your freedom, and that is not passivity. And he knew exactly what would follow: what he said was that Comala would starve, not that Comala would find its own way.

The everyday versions are more common than they look: a manager who stops advocating for their reports, a department that quietly ends a subsidy, a person who makes no scene at all and simply stops replying. Each of them can say they did nothing. Whether not-acting counts as acting depends on what you did before — that is the most usable thing this book gives you.

Question 3
A good man who knows he is doing harm — is he more forgivable than a bad man, or less?

Father Rentería is the only character in the book with a conscience, and he does far more damage than Miguel Páramo.

For him first. His situation is real: the parish is destitute, the church has to keep running, and all the money in the district is in one man's hands. Every compromise he makes has arithmetic behind it — take the coins and the church stays open and the poor still have somewhere to go; refuse, and he is replaced by someone more accommodating, and the only thing improved is his own record. "If not me, someone worse" endures as an argument because it is frequently true. And he has not gone numb: he goes to confess, he asks for absolution, and after being refused he goes back and continues being a priest — a form of persistence with no reward attached. Miguel, by contrast, is at least not pretending to be anything.

The other side is harder. His conscience is precisely what makes him useful. A shameless priest cannot sell absolution, because nobody believes him; Rentería can sell it because the town sincerely regards him as their priest. What he supplies is not a rite but legitimacy, and only clean men manufacture legitimacy. The cost is the other half of the price list: the people who cannot pay are turned away. Eduviges is held in place because he signed off on it, and by this novel's own metaphysics, the administrative responsibility for Comala becoming a field of murmurs that can neither stay nor leave is his. His sin is not accepting the gold. It is that he turned "who deserves peace" into something with a price, and then convinced himself it was temporary.

The live version of this is the question facing anyone employed by a compromised institution: stay and improve it, or leave and keep your hands clean? The book gives no answer, but it does hand you something concrete to check — since you stayed, has the price list gotten shorter or longer?

Question 4
Is Susana's madness the one thing she kept, or are we making a ruin sound noble?

There is a reading that is moving, and popular. Pedro Páramo bought the land, the water, the people, the priest and eventually the revolution, and at the end he got Susana's body too — and could not get into her head. She spends her days describing another man's body with nobody able to stop her. In a world where one man owns everything, her madness is the only territory left off the register. As she dies, the priest asks her to say the correct words and she says her own instead. She wins, at the price of her mind.

But be careful saying it out loud. Calling a woman's destruction "sovereignty" is an extremely comfortable reading — comfortable because it excuses the reader from looking at what was done. What was done: as a child her father lowered her into a mine to feel for a corpse; as an adult her husband died; then the man who had her father killed took her into his house as his wife. She did not withdraw. She was broken. There is no joy of freedom in her delirium, only a return, over and over, to the same hole. To say she won is to say she came out of the transaction with something — and she came out with nothing.

The trap is not confined to literature. We routinely translate someone's collapse into clarity, their withdrawal into wisdom, their exit from every relationship into freedom at last. Sometimes it is. More often it is that we would rather not admit a person was damaged. The novel refuses to close either end: it makes Susana's voice unbearably beautiful and simultaneously itemizes exactly how she came to be this way, and then leaves you to decide what to call it.

The obsession Rulfo kept returning to

He wrote two books, so the recurring material is unusually easy to see.

First, fathers and sons, always as a question of who gets claimed. In El Llano en llamas there is a story in which an old man, tied up, begs his son for help — and the officer who has ordered his execution is the son of a man he killed decades earlier. In another, a father carries his grown, gunshot son on his shoulders all night, cursing him as he walks; the son is a bandit. In this novel it becomes the entire architecture: a son walks into town to collect from his father, who is already dead — and the man who killed that father is another son he never acknowledged. Rulfo's fathers and sons never reconcile. They only settle accounts.

Second, land as a lie. El Llano en llamas opens with peasants walking across the plot the land reform has awarded them; nothing will grow on it, it is hard as slate. Here the lie points the other way: Comala was not always bad. It was good, and then a man cancelled it. The green his mother described was real. It is simply no longer there.

Third, a dead village that talks. The story "Luvina" is almost a rehearsal for Comala: a village up in the wind where the young have all gone and only the old and the dead remain, described across a whole evening in a bar by a man who came back from it to a man about to go there. Read it next to this novel and you can watch a writer take the atmospheric pressure of a short story and hold it for the length of a book.

And then the silence — thirty years after 1955, no third book. He gave a famous account of it himself: the stories all came from his Uncle Celerino, and Uncle Celerino died, so there was nothing left to write. That is a joke, and also a shield. The more honest guess may be that Pedro Páramo is a book with no next move after it. He kept working — editing, photographing, writing for film (The Golden Cockerel came from that work and was published as a book many years later). He just did not write another novel.

Criticism and controversy

Told straight, the book has some real problems, and the criticisms have weight:

  • It is hard, and whether the difficulty earns its keep is genuinely disputed. No chapters, no dates, person switching without warning, seventy-odd fragments cutting in front of each other — a first reading almost inevitably loses the thread. The defence is that form is content (this page has made that argument). The objection is that some of the payoff from the jigsaw work the reader is forced into comes from the confusion itself rather than from the picture assembled. There is also a technical doubt: Rulfo is said to have cut a great deal of material at the final stage, and how much of the present ordering is design and how much is the residue of cutting cannot be established from outside the text.
  • Where the women stand. Nearly every woman in the book is defined by her relation to Pedro Páramo: Dolores married to erase a debt, Eduviges substituting for a wedding night, Susana taken, Damiana serving, Dorotea procuring for his son. Susana is the only character with a fully realized interior, and that interior is written as delirium and is heavily sexualized. Critics see a pattern: women are property, objects of appetite, or mad. Defenders answer that the one consciousness that man can never occupy is deliberately hers. The argument is unresolved.
  • Does the myth absorb the history? The book is about rural power after the Mexican Revolution — how a local strongman (a cacique) controls everything through land, marriage, violence and the Church, and how the revolution itself is simply purchased. That is sharp political content. But it has been argued that once all of it is housed inside a ghost town and an eternal curse, very specific historical responsibility becomes easy to read as fate: Pedro Páramo stops looking like the product of a system and starts looking like weather.
  • Indigenous people are almost absent. Rulfo worked for years at the National Indigenist Institute and photographed extensively in that context, but his fiction is essentially a rural mestizo world with very few indigenous characters. This has been raised repeatedly, with responses running from "it accurately reflects the makeup of the particular region he wrote about" to "it remains a conspicuous absence."
  • Translation costs a great deal here. The novel's power depends heavily on the speech rhythms of rural Jalisco — short clauses, ellipsis, abrupt stops, sentences abandoned midway. There are at least three English versions in circulation (1959, 1994, 2023) and they differ substantially. Readers in different languages are not, at sentence level, discussing the same book — especially in the passages that work through tone rather than information.

Ten sentences to take away

1. This is not a story about a man looking for his father. It is a story about a man inheriting a grievance and dying inside it.

2. The hardest technical move in the book: the narrator dies at the midpoint and the first person keeps going — the second half is being told by a man in a grave.

3. The green Comala his mother described was not an old woman's filter. It existed. A man cancelled it after his wife died.

4. Pedro Páramo killed a number of people, but the act that destroyed the whole town was sitting down, folding his arms, and doing nothing.

5. The supernatural here has an administrative process: the dead stay because they were not absolved, and absolution has to be paid for.

6. Father Rentería's sin is not that he took the gold. It is that he turned "who deserves peace" into a price list.

7. Susana's madness is the one thing Pedro could not buy — but calling that "her victory" concedes that she got something out of the transaction.

8. The revolution is not a historical turning point in this book. It is a line item in the Media Luna's accounts.

9. It is called the source of magical realism and it is hardly magical at all: no marvels, just the common sense of a rural Catholic world in which of course the dead talk.

10. What stays with you is the last verb. He does not fall and he does not expire — he crumbles, into a heap of stones, which is what his name has said all along.
