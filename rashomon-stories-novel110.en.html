Novels, Deep-Read · Ryūnosuke Akutagawa · No.110

Rashōmon and In a Grove

Rashōmon · In a Grove (Yabu no naka) · Ryūnosuke Akutagawa · 1915 / 1922 · two short stories

中文 →

The ladder is narrow. He flattens himself against it like a cat, holds his breath, and inches up one rung at a time.
There is light up there, wavering, making the cobwebs on the ceiling flicker.
Nobody honest lights a fire in a place like this, at an hour like this. One hand rests on the hilt at his hip; the other, without his noticing, has drifted up to the big festering pimple on his right cheek.
It is still raining. One more rung and he will see what is crouching under that flame.

This piece contains complete spoilers — what the old woman on the Rashōmon is doing to the dead, whose clothes the servant walks away with, how the samurai in the grove actually died (three people give three answers), and the hand in the dark that pulls out the knife. The two stories together run to a few dozen pages. The endings are the whole point; without them there is nothing to think about.

In one line

Two stories, seven years apart. In the first, a servant who has just been dismissed climbs into a city gate and strips an old woman bare using the very argument she used to excuse herself. In the second, a samurai lies dead in a bamboo grove, and the bandit, the wife, and the dead man's own ghost each step forward to confess — all three of them say: I killed him. They cannot all be telling the truth.

Where it sits

Ryūnosuke Akutagawa (1892–1927) studied English literature at Tokyo Imperial University and wrote short stories his whole life; he never finished a novel. "Rashōmon" appeared in the journal Teikoku Bungaku in November 1915 and was barely noticed. The next year "The Nose" won him a letter of praise from Natsume Sōseki, and his career began. "In a Grove" (Yabu no naka) came out in the magazine Shinchō in January 1922, in the middle of his working life. Five years later, at thirty-five, he killed himself with an overdose.

Both are old wine in new bottles — or rather, new wine in old ones. Their raw material comes from the Konjaku Monogatarishū, a late-Heian compendium of more than a thousand Buddhist tales and strange anecdotes; Akutagawa took them apart and put twentieth-century things inside. In 1950 Akira Kurosawa fused the two into the film Rashomon, and the word entered English: the Rashomon effect, when everyone tells the same event differently and nobody can settle it. But the film is the film. This piece is about the stories, and the woodcutter who adopts an abandoned baby, the warm ending — none of that is in Akutagawa.

The story

Act One · Rashōmon: a man with nowhere to go in the rain

The Heian age is ending and the capital has rotted. Earthquakes, whirlwinds, fires and famine have come one after another. The great southern gate of Kyoto, the Rashōmon, has fallen into ruin; no one maintains it, and people have taken to throwing unclaimed corpses into its upper storey. At dusk, crows circle the roof in flocks, pecking at what lies inside.

A servant — a genin, the lowest kind of household drudge in an aristocratic house — sits on the stone steps under the gate, out of the rain. A few days ago the master he served for years let him go; the house itself is going under. He has nowhere to go. Apart from him there is only a cricket on one of the huge pillars, its red lacquer peeling. He sits staring at nothing and fingering the big pimple on his right cheek.Setup ①

His situation reduces to one simple problem: how to live tomorrow. Either he starves at the foot of some mud wall and gets thrown up into this gate like a dog, or he becomes a thief. He has reasoned his way halfway there — if he isn't going to be choosy about means, that is the only way. But every time he arrives at "so I'll have to steal," he stalls, and cannot take the step.

It grows cold. Wind slips between the pillars; at some point the cricket has gone too. Looking for somewhere to sleep, he notices the ladder up to the gate tower. Up there, at least, there are only the dead.

Act Two · The light upstairs: a man suddenly feels righteous

Halfway up the ladder he sees the light. At the top he raises his head: the floor is strewn with bodies like clay dolls, mouths open, arms flung out, dumped where they fell.

Among them crouches someone alive — a small, scrawny, grey-haired old woman, monkey-like, in a robe the colour of cypress bark, holding a pine torch in her right hand and peering at the corpse of a woman with long hair. Then she wedges the torch into a gap in the floorboards, takes the dead woman's head in both hands, and, like a mother monkey grooming her young for lice, begins to pull out the hair, one strand at a time.

The servant's fear vanishes. What floods in is loathing — of the old woman, and then of every kind of evil. If anyone asked him now whether to starve or turn thief, he would choose starvation without a second thought. The story drily adds, in effect, that his hatred of evil was burning as brightly as the torch stuck in the floor. He doesn't even know why she is pulling hair; he is in no position to judge good or evil. It is just that pulling hair from the dead, on a rainy night, on the Rashōmon, seems unforgivable in itself.

He leaps out, sword in hand. She jerks back; he throws her down on the bodies. Then he realises her life is entirely in his grip — and the blazing loathing quietly cools, leaving the slack satisfaction of a job done. He changes his tone: he is no officer, just a passer-by; tell me what you are doing and I won't harm you.

Act Three · The logic of a robe

Her answer is simple. She is pulling out hair to make wigs.

She can see this has not satisfied him, so she hurries on. Pulling hair from the dead may be wicked, yes. But every corpse up here deserves it. This woman, for instance: in life she cut snakes into lengths, dried them, and sold them as dried fish to the guards at the palace barracks, and the guards said it was good fish. If the plague hadn't taken her she'd be selling it still. I don't think what she did was wrong — she'd have starved otherwise, she had no choice. And so I don't think what I'm doing is wrong either: I'd starve otherwise, I have no choice. She understood having no choice. She would forgive me.Setup ②

The servant sheathes his sword and listens coldly, left hand on the hilt. And the right? Still fingering that inflamed pimple. As he listens, a kind of courage rises in him — exactly the courage he lacked down at the foot of the gate, and pointing in exactly the opposite direction from the one that sent him leaping at her. Starve or steal is no longer a question. Starving has been pushed out of his mind altogether.

When she finishes he asks, mockingly, whether that is really so. Then he steps forward, takes his right hand off the pimple,Payoff ① ↑ grabs her by the collar and says: then you won't hold it against me if I take your clothes. I'd starve otherwise.Payoff ② ↑

These two moments are the bones of the story. The pimple has been there from the first page: he touches it while brooding under the gate, and again while listening to her excuse. The pimple is his hesitation — something small and private, on a young man's face, under his own hand. When the hand comes away, he is no longer a man who is still thinking. Akutagawa never writes "he decided"; he writes a hand. And "I'd starve otherwise" is a blade the old woman hands him herself. She uses the argument to acquit herself; he uses the identical argument to strip her. Her defence is returned to her word for word.

He tears off her robe, kicks her back onto the corpses as she clutches at his legs, and with the cypress-bark robe under his arm takes the steep ladder down into the bottom of the night. The last line of the story is a handful of words: where the servant went, no one knows. This line was a revision; when the story first appeared, it ended by saying outright that he was hurrying through the rain into the city to commit robbery. With the change, the door shuts behind the reader.

Act Four · Four people outside the grove

"In a Grove," seven years later, has no narrator and not a single "he thought" or "she felt." It is seven statements, the first four given to the kebiishi, the Heian office that handled policing and trials.

A woodcutter: this morning, cutting cedar in the hills behind the Yamashina road, in a thicket of bamboo and thin cedars, he found a man's body. Pale blue hunting robe, the soft cap of a samurai, lying face up. A single sword wound in the chest; the bamboo leaves around him stained dark red. The bleeding had stopped; a horsefly was stuck to the wound. No sword, nothing of the kind — nothing at all;Setup ③ only a length of rope at the foot of a cedar, and a comb. The grass and fallen leaves all around were trampled flat. A horse? No horse could get in there; a stand of bamboo cuts it off from the road.

A travelling priest: yesterday around noon, on the road from Sekiyama to Yamashina, he passed this man walking beside a woman on horseback. Her hat had a hanging veil; he could not see her face, only the colour of her clothes. The man wore a long sword and carried a bow and arrows.

A hōmen — a constable, the kind often recruited from released convicts: the man I caught is the notorious bandit Tajōmaru. Last night he was groaning on the stone bridge at Awataguchi, evidently thrown from a horse. He had a long sword on him, and a bow and arrows — the very set the dead man had. The horse was grazing past the bridge. If he killed the man, someone had better also ask where the woman went.

An old woman — the dead man's mother-in-law: the dead man is my son-in-law, Kanazawa no Takehiro, twenty-six, a samurai of the provincial office in Wakasa, a gentle man who made no enemies. My daughter is Masago, nineteen. She is as strong-willed as any man,Setup ④ and has never known any man but Takehiro. A small oval face, a mole at the corner of her left eye. Yesterday she set out with him for Wakasa.

Act Five · The bandit's confession: twenty-three passes

Tajōmaru confesses at once. I killed him. I don't know where the woman went. You're going to give me the ultimate penalty anyway; I won't hide anything.

He met the couple yesterday afternoon. A gust lifted her veil, he saw her face, and in that instant he decided to have her — even if it meant killing the man, though better not to if he could help it. He spun a tale: in the hills over there he had dug up an old burial mound full of mirrors and swords, hidden the haul in the grove, and would sell it cheap. The man was tempted. Tajōmaru led him deep into the thicket, jumped him, tied him to a cedar and stuffed his mouth with fallen bamboo leaves.

Then he went back and told the woman her husband had suddenly fallen ill. She followed him in, saw her husband bound to the tree — and whipped a small dagger from her robe and came straight at him.Payoff ④ ↑ He knocked it away, in the end had his way with her, and did not kill the man.

He meant to leave. But she clung to his arm, frantic, crying in broken words: either you die or my husband dies — one of you must. To be seen in my shame by two men is worse than death. Whoever lives, I'll go with him. He looked into her eyes, where something seemed to be burning, and in that moment he wanted her for his wife. So he cut the rope and told the man to take up his sword. They crossed swords twenty-three times; on the twenty-third, Tajōmaru's blade went through the man's chest.

When he turned around, the woman had gone. He took the man's sword and his bow and arrows and went back to the road, where the horse was still grazing. He ends with swagger: that's my confession; my head will hang from the chinaberry tree sooner or later, so give me the harshest penalty you have.

Look back at the fourth statement. When the mother calls her daughter "as strong-willed as any man," it reads like a grieving parent speaking well of a missing child — a line of character description. Only when the bandit mentions the dagger pulled from her robe does the phrase bite into a concrete act. Akutagawa is sly here: he lets two statements from strangers agree on a detail, and so the reader starts to believe the bandit. That is precisely the kind of trust the next statement is built to dismantle.

Act Six · The woman at Kiyomizu, the dead man through a medium

The sixth statement is headed "The Confession of a Woman Who Has Come to Kiyomizu Temple." Masago tells another story.

After the man in blue had his way with me, she says, he looked at my bound husband and laughed in mockery. How my husband must have suffered. I ran to him, and the man kicked me down. And at that moment I saw my husband's eyes. There was neither anger nor grief in them — only a cold contempt. Remembering it still makes me shudder.Setup ⑤ I cried out and fainted.

When she came to, the man was gone. Her husband was still tied to the cedar, his look unchanged — contempt, with hatred underneath. She told him: after this I can't go on living with you; I mean to die. But you have seen my shame, so I cannot leave you alive either. She found her fallen dagger. His mouth was packed with leaves and he could make no sound, but she read the shape of his lips: kill me. As if in a dream, she drove the dagger through the pale blue robe into his chest.

She fainted again. When she opened her eyes he was dead, a thread of late sunlight falling through the bamboo onto his grey face. Choking back sobs, she untied the rope from the body. And then? She wanted to die. She put the dagger to her throat; she threw herself into a pond at the foot of the hill. She failed both times. What should someone do who cannot even manage to die? Her confession dissolves into weeping.

The seventh and last statement is "The Story of the Dead Man's Spirit, Told Through a Medium." Takehiro himself comes back, speaking through a shrine medium's mouth.

After the bandit had his way, the ghost says, he sat down and began to console my wife: you've been defiled, you'll never get on with your husband now — why not be my wife? It was love that drove me to it. I tried with my eyes to tell her: don't believe him, whatever he says.Payoff ⑤ ↑ But my wife, sitting on the bamboo leaves, raised her face as if entranced. I had never seen her so beautiful. And she said to the bandit: take me with you, anywhere.

That was not all. At the edge of the grove her face changed; she pointed at me where I was tied and screamed at him: kill that man. As long as he lives I can't be with you. She screamed it again and again. The ghost says the words still roar at him like a gale, threatening to blow him into the bottom of darkness. Even the bandit went pale. He kicked her down onto the leaves, folded his arms, and asked me: what do you want done with this woman? Kill her, or spare her? Just nod. — For those words alone, says the ghost, I would forgive the bandit his crime.

While I hesitated, my wife shrieked and fled into the depths of the grove; the bandit lunged and missed even her sleeve. He picked up my sword and my bow and arrows, cut my rope in one place, muttered that now it was his own neck at stake, and vanished.

I heard someone weeping. Working at the rope, I listened — and realised the weeping was my own. I rose from the foot of the cedar and saw, glinting in front of me, the dagger my wife had dropped. I picked it up and drove it into my own chest. Something tasting of blood rose into my throat, but there was no pain at all. My chest went cold; everything grew quieter; in that sky behind the hills not a bird was singing, only a lonely light lingering at the tops of the cedars and the bamboo.

Then someone came creeping toward me. I tried to see who, but darkness had closed in around me. That someone — that invisible hand — gently drew the dagger from my chest. Blood flooded my mouth again, and I sank for ever into the darkness of the chūu, the Buddhist in-between, the dim passage between death and rebirth.Payoff ③ ↑

This is the coldest payoff in the story. In the very first statement the woodcutter says there was no sword, nothing at all, and we read it as a gap in an inventory: the killer took the weapon, of course. In the last statement the gap is filled: someone pulled the blade out before he had finished dying. Who? The bandit had gone; the wife had fled. Akutagawa does not say a word. He simply moves the knife from the first page to the last — and so many readers turn back to the most modest, least noticeable witness, the first one to speak, the one who said there was nothing. And look again at the eyes at setup ⑤: a single glance read two ways by two people. The wife saw contempt; the husband says he was sending a warning. One look, two opposite events, in two confessions.

The fuse

Put the two stories side by side and they run on the same fuse: not the famine, not the gust that lifted the veil, but a person at the end of their rope needing a reason they can say out loud. The servant lacked not a meal but a justification, and the old woman handed him one. Tajōmaru, Masago and Takehiro lack not the truth but a version of themselves they can live — or die — inside. So the servant goes down the ladder into a night where "no one knows," and the three in the grove each walk into a story of their own making. The sentence, the mouths — those were the real weapons. The actual knife was taken away by a hand no one saw.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion one · The narrator interrupts himself

Early in "Rashōmon" there is an odd pause. The narrator has written that a servant was waiting for the rain to stop under the gate. A few paragraphs later he comes back to correct himself: the author wrote "waiting for the rain to stop," but in truth, even if it stopped, the man had nowhere in particular to go. Better to say that a servant who had lost his place was trapped by the rain, with no idea where to turn.

Why does this land? Because it lifts the act of "waiting" off the character. The rain is no obstacle; it is just a decent-looking excuse for having no road. The narrator quietly hands the reader the whole story's theme in advance: this man is not blocked by weather but by himself. And when the last line comes — "no one knows" where he went — the question of where he can go returns, except that now he has somewhere to go and we are not allowed to follow.

Slow motion two · A hand leaves a face

The servant's turn takes less than a page and is written almost entirely through the body: breath held, sword gripped, hand on the hilt, fingers on the pimple. The finest touch is the righteousness: Akutagawa says it burned as brightly as the torch. The torch is wedged in the floor, the only light in the scene; it lets him see the old woman, and it makes him look upright. But a torch burns down, and this one is a bundle of pine lashed together on the spot. One image says two things at once: his indignation is genuinely hot, and genuinely short.

Slow motion three · The hand in the dark

The dead man's statement is the only death in the story written from the inside: the blade goes in, there is no pain, only cold; the world goes quiet, no birdsong, a little light in the treetops. The rhythm is pressed down almost to stillness — and then someone approaches and pulls the knife out.

Why is this so frightening? Everyone has been telling a story that serves them, the ghost included; yet at the very moment he finishes and is about to sink into darkness, a detail breaks in that he can neither explain nor use. It is the only thing in seven statements that serves no speaker, and so it feels the most true. Akutagawa places the one shard of "objectivity" in the mouth of the witness least capable of objectivity — a dead man.

The idea, taken seriously: a machine that won't give an answer

"In a Grove" is often treated as a puzzle, and it has every part a puzzle needs: a body, a weapon, suspects, testimony, physical evidence. But the machine is designed to do the opposite — it uses the parts of a detective story to build a device guaranteed not to produce a solution. Take it apart.

Layer one: the four outside witnesses supply hard constraints. The man carried a sword and a bow; the woman rode a horse; the man died of a single wound to the chest; at the scene there were a rope, a comb, trampled leaves, and no blade; the bandit was caught carrying the dead man's bow and arrows. Any "true" account has to satisfy all of these.

Layer three: every version rubs against the constraints somewhere, but never fatally. If they fought twenty-three passes, why only one wound? Perhaps the earlier strokes were all parried. If Masago stabbed him with her dagger, where is it? She fainted, she tried to kill herself; it could have been lost. If the ghost stabbed himself and someone pulled the blade out, that neatly explains why the woodcutter found none — and opens a new question: who pulled it, and why say nothing? Every clue can be swallowed by at least two versions, so no clue can decide between them.

Layer four: the medium removes even the foundation. In detective fiction the dead are the only ones who cannot lie; they have nothing left to gain. Akutagawa lets the dead man speak, and his version is just as self-serving. Worse, he speaks through a medium — is this the dead man, the medium, or the medium shaping what her listeners want to hear? Even the last witness comes to us through someone else's mouth.

Which links hold? "People reassemble memory and narrative to protect their dignity" holds very well. Which link is there because the story needs it? All three rushing to confess is an extreme case. In real-life Rashomon situations people usually push blame outward; Akutagawa reverses it so that everyone pulls blame inward. That is not a statistic about human nature but a design: if even a confession can be self-flattery, no testimony carries automatic credibility.

And the inevitable question: who really did it? Around 1970 the critic Mitsuo Nakamura went so far as to argue that the story's unresolved truth was itself an artistic failure, and the novelist Shōhei Ōoka wrote in rebuttal — a fairly well-known exchange in Japanese letters. What is striking is that almost every proposed solution can be made self-consistent, and that is exactly how the machine works: it turns every reasoner into an eighth witness. In Japanese, yabu no naka — "in the grove" — has since become an idiom for a truth that cannot be known.

What it makes you think about

One · "I'd starve otherwise" — how much can that excuse?

The old woman says: the snake-seller would have starved otherwise, so she wasn't wicked; I'd starve if I didn't pull hair, so neither am I. The servant adds one more link to the chain. At which link would you stop?

The case for the old woman at its strongest: this is not sophistry; it is the only honest ethics a famine allows. Morality at such a time is a luxury of the fed. A man sitting hungry under a gate who demands that others "starve rather than do wrong" is, in fact, demanding that others die. The snake-seller poisoned no one; the guards enjoyed their fish. The dead do not need their hair.

The case for stopping at its strongest: once that waiver is issued, nobody can call it back. The servant uses that same piece of paper to strip the woman who signed it, and his case is far less desperate than hers — a strong young man taking the only robe from a skinny old woman on a cold night. The logic of "no choice" has one fatal feature: it never checks whether there really is no choice. It only passes downward, and always toward someone weaker.

Where does Akutagawa stand? He passes no sentence, but he makes the servant's "righteousness" and "courage" both look absurd: one lasted minutes, the other was borrowed. Bring it home: layoffs because "everyone is cutting costs," cheating because "everyone else does," a business that "couldn't survive otherwise" — when you hear the sentence, ask who it finally lands on.

Two · Three people, three stories: insist on one truth, or admit it's "in the grove"?

Suppose you are the magistrate and must close the case. Or suppose you are the one "hearing both sides" of a family quarrel, an accident inquiry at work, a broken friendship. Three accounts, each complete, each moving, each contradicting the others.

The case that the truth exists and is worth pursuing: Takehiro really did die, once, in one definite way. "Everyone has their own truth" sounds generous but is a second abandonment of the dead and the best possible reward for liars — if every version is equally valid, the best storyteller always wins. Refusing to judge is not humility; it hands judgment to whoever has power. And the story itself leaves a crack that points somewhere: the knife that was pulled out, the woodcutter who said there was nothing.

The case for "in the grove": the three need not be lying. A woman who has been raped, a man forced to watch it, a bandit who has just killed — what they say afterward is memory, shame and self-protection boiled together, and each may believe their own account. Insisting there must be one true version usually means declaring two people liars, and the one declared a liar is often the one with least power. Admitting the truth is out of reach lets you return to what can still be done: what this woman needs, what the bandit deserves for the crimes that are certain — robbery, rape.

Akutagawa clearly leans toward the second, but he left the knife, as if deliberately leaving a door open for the first. Bring it home: next time two friends tell you completely different versions of the same night, will you rush to rule on who is right — or first admit that you, too, are only the eighth witness? Both choices cost something; they just cost different people.

Three · Is a lie that saves your dignity still a lie?

Tajōmaru would rather tell what followed the rape as an honourable duel than admit a woman's words made him go pale. Masago would rather say she killed her husband than admit she wanted to leave with the bandit — if that is what happened. The ghost would rather say he took his own life than admit he died at someone else's hand. Each confesses to the heaviest crime in order not to confess the deepest shame.

The case for these "lies": people are not recording devices; they live by a story about themselves. A woman who has been raped, if she cannot say "what I did, I did out of shame," how does she go on living at Kiyomizu? Keeping a self you can survive inside is sometimes a necessity, the same kind of need as the servant's need for a meal — only the hunger is somewhere else.

The case against: dignity always has someone paying the bill. Tajōmaru's prowess needs a duel that perhaps never happened; Takehiro's honour needs his wife to be the one who betrayed him; Masago's purity needs her husband's look to be contempt. Each saves themselves by putting someone else in an uglier position. A dead man returns through a medium, and the first thing he does is accuse his wife — not even death relieved him of the need.

Akutagawa excuses no one. He simply stands the three versions side by side and lets you see how well each fits its wearer. Bring it home: the "back when I…" story you tell most often — which version is it? And who, in it, have you put in the unflattering spot?

The obsession: what Akutagawa kept writing

Akutagawa returned to two obsessions. The first: how thin human decency is, and how much it depends on circumstance. In "The Nose" (1916), a high priest with an absurdly long nose finally shrinks it, only to be laughed at more than ever — people, he discovers, do not pity misfortune so much as feel cheated when someone escapes it. In "The Spider's Thread" (1918), the Buddha lowers a strand of spider silk to save a robber in hell; the moment the robber shouts "This is mine — get off!" it snaps. The servant on the Rashōmon is the earliest and barest link in this chain.

The second: the distance between art, narration and truth. In "Hell Screen" (1918), a painter watches his own daughter burn alive so he can paint a burning carriage on a screen of hell; when it is done, he hangs himself — paying with everything real for one "true" image. "In a Grove" moves the problem into narration itself: it is no longer an artist sacrificing something for truth, but everyone who opens their mouth making art.

In his last works, Kappa and Spinning Gears (both 1927), Akutagawa barely borrows ancient costumes at all; he writes a modern man who is nervous, hallucinating, doubting everything. Seen from there, "In a Grove" looks like a story from halfway along the road: the tale is still whole, but the single fact behind it has already caved in.

Criticism and controversy

"Borrowed plots." Most of Akutagawa's best-known stories rework the Konjaku Monogatarishū, the Uji Shūi Monogatari and other old collections, or adapt Western works. Critics have called him a brilliant adapter who wrote from cleverness rather than lived experience, his characters placed like pieces on a board. Defenders reply that the changes are the creation: in the Konjaku version, the man who climbs the gate is a thief who came to the capital expressly to steal; Akutagawa made him an unemployed servant still hesitating. In the Konjaku grove tale the robber ties up the husband and assaults the wife before his eyes but kills no one, and the wife berates her husband for being useless — Akutagawa added the death and the seven mouths. Scholars also often point to possible influences on the many-voiced structure: Ambrose Bierce's "The Moonlit Road," in which a dead man likewise testifies through a medium, and Browning's long poem The Ring and the Book.

Masago. In all three versions the woman is defined by a male gaze: in the bandit's she is fierce and makes two men fight over her; in her husband's she is a traitor; in her own she is someone shattered by one look from her husband who cannot even succeed at dying. Some readers see this as the story's insight — her situation is defined by men. Others argue that setting a rape victim's testimony on an equal footing with her attacker's and then announcing that the truth is unknowable is not a neutral stance. Both readings have force, and the story will not choose for you.

Is "unknowable" deep, or a trick? That was the gist of Nakamura's complaint: if the author himself has no answer, the openness is merely a clever device rather than knowledge of the world. The objection has never been fully put to rest.

Eclipsed by the film. Kurosawa's film is so famous that what many people remember as "Rashomon" is the movie: the gate as a frame, the grove as the plot, plus a fourth version witnessed by the woodcutter (a clumsy, ugly fight) and an abandoned baby who is taken in. The film grants human nature a final flicker of hope; neither story does. Translations also vary — English versions appear as "In a Grove" and "In a Bamboo Grove," among others — so it pays to mind the translation when arguing over particular wording.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · Bartlett's reconstructive memory: In Remembering (1932), Frederic Bartlett had English subjects retell a Native American folk tale again and again; with each retelling it drifted toward their own culture's expectations. Memory is rebuilt, not replayed — the same shape as the three confessions. The difference: Bartlett's subjects revised without meaning to; in the grove at least some of it is deliberate, and the story refuses to tell you how much.
  • Sociology · Goffman's face-work: Erving Goffman argued that people constantly maintain the image others hold of them, and that losing face can hurt more than many material losses. Confessing to murder rather than to humiliation is exactly that trade. Unlike Goffman's small everyday repairs, this face is patched with a life.
  • Law · R v Dudley and Stephens: In 1884, shipwrecked English sailors killed and ate a dying cabin boy; the court refused to accept necessity as a defence to murder. "I'd starve otherwise" is that defence in embryo. The difference: a court has to stop the chain somewhere; on the Rashōmon there is no court.
  • Philosophy · Nietzsche's perspectivism: Nietzsche's notebooks contain the often-quoted remark, roughly, that there are no facts, only interpretations. "In a Grove" is often taken as its illustration. Unlike it, the grove does hold a fact — a dead man and a missing knife; the story lets interpretation bury it, not abolish it.

Ten lines to take away

① He wasn't waiting under the gate for the rain to stop; he was waiting for a reason he could believe.

② Righteousness like a torch jammed in the floorboards: burning bright, and not for long.

③ A hand leaves a pimple, and a man leaves his hesitation.

④ The first person the old woman's waiver was used against was the old woman.

⑤ "No one knows" where he went — and the door shuts behind the reader.

⑥ In the grove, all three confess to the killing, because confessing to murder is cheaper than confessing to shame.

⑦ One glance: the wife read contempt, the husband says he sent a warning.

⑧ Even the dead, given a voice, tidy themselves up before they speak.

⑨ The only detail that speaks for no one is the hand in the dark that drew out the knife.

⑩ Every part of a detective story is present, assembled into a machine that will not give an answer — and every reader becomes the eighth witness.
