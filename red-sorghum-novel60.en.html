Novels Deep Read · Mo Yan · No.60

Red Sorghum

A novel assembled from five novellas · Mo Yan · 1986 first part / 1987 complete

中文 →

The fog moves along the ground, smearing the sorghum leaves into one wet dark-green mass. A fourteen-year-old boy called Douguan pushes after a man into the deep rows, the black earth of Gaomi Northeast Township sucking at his shoes. The man is his father, and also the local bandit chief everyone calls Commander Yu. They are going to the bridge over the Black Water River to ambush a Japanese convoy.
Nobody tells the boy that by the end of the morning he will see his mother lying in this sorghum; that most of the men walking with them will be on the ground by noon; that before the autumn is out he will be fighting the village dogs for the same patch of field.
The sorghum does not care. It rustles in the fog, all the way to the horizon.

This page contains complete spoilers — how Grandma dies, what happens to Uncle Arhat's skin, what the ambush at the bridge actually achieves, the "strange death" of Second Grandma, and the last stalk of pure-breed red sorghum. This site's conviction: everything worth discussing lies past the ending. If you want the suspense, read the novel first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

A grandson retells his family's unofficial history in a voice that keeps saying my grandpa, my grandma, my father: his grandmother was traded at sixteen by her own father for a big black mule and married off to a leper; on the road home from the wedding she went into the sorghum with one of the men who carried her sedan chair; that man murdered her husband's family, took over their distillery, raised a bandit band, and in 1939 ambushed a Japanese convoy with homemade weapons — after which his grandmother bled to death in the sorghum, the band was destroyed, the corpses were eaten by dogs; and decades later the grandson comes home to find that the pure-breed red sorghum is extinct and the fields are full of hybrid stock.

Coordinates

Mo Yan (born 1955; the pen name is a pseudonym, his given name is Guan Moye) comes from Gaomi in Shandong province. The novella Red Sorghum appeared in the journal People's Literature in 1986; together with four more — Sorghum Wine, Dog Ways, Sorghum Funeral and Strange Death — it was published in 1987 as the novel Red Sorghum Clan, known in English simply as Red Sorghum. Zhang Yimou's film of the first two novellas won the Golden Bear at Berlin in 1988. Mo Yan received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2012.

Its slot in literary history is easy to find: the loudest single noise made at the point where China's mid-1980s "root-seeking" movement collided with its avant-garde. Mo Yan has openly named his two masters — Faulkner's Yoknapatawpha and García Márquez's Macondo — and has also said he had to get away from those two furnaces. What he built instead was Gaomi Northeast Township: a place assembled out of his own memory and his own invention, and the site of nearly everything he has written since.

As for why the book went off like a bomb in 1986: before it, the Chinese war-of-resistance story was told by regular forces, with protagonists who had organization, political consciousness and discipline. Red Sorghum hands that role to a gang of bandits — a sedan-bearer who has killed, stolen a woman, and pissed into a vat of wine, leading a mob of illiterate farmers with knives and a homemade cannon against Japanese trucks. And the narrator is his grandson, who keeps calling him my grandpa. The posture is itself the argument.

The story

Act One · 1939, a column in the fog

The novel cuts in at the middle. Before dawn on the ninth day of the eighth lunar month, 1939, a heavy fog lies over Gaomi Northeast Township, and Yu Zhan'ao walks his forty-odd men toward the Black Water River. One carries the flag, two haul the homemade cannon, several have brought kitchen knives from home. One of them is fourteen years old: Douguan, "my father," Yu Zhan'ao's son. The novel's famous first sentence fixes that date and that age before anything else happens, and calls the boy a bandit's offspring — the book tells you exactly what it thinks of its own heroes in its opening breath.

The target is a Japanese truck convoy. The intelligence came from Detachment Leader Leng, who runs the other armed group in the district, the one flying Nationalist colors; Leng has promised to meet them at the bridge. Yu half believes him.

The march is written long and slow, and that is the craft of it: in fog you can see nothing, so men identify each other by voice and smell, sorghum leaves cut their faces, dew runs down their collars. Seed ①Sorghum is never scenery in this book. It is written as something warm-blooded, breathing, capable of bleeding — thousands of stalks crowded together, red going on black, like a standing field of blood. Men walking through it are walking inside a living body.

Something happens in the fog that ought to be terrifying and is instead played for laughs. Wang Wenyi, the nervous one, starts howling that his head has been shot off, hands full of blood, sobbing. When they feel for it, a stray bullet has taken a piece out of his ear. The others laugh at him. That laugh matters — it teaches you, before you have seen a real death, that death here can be funny. A few hours later the same gesture repeats: a man calls out, goes down, and nobody laughs.

Before they set off, Yu gives one instruction: have the food brought out to the bridge. So "my grandma," Dai Fenglian, picks up a basket of flatbreads she has just baked and starts into the sorghum. Seed ②She is the band's commissariat and its actual center of gravity — the distillery is hers, the grain is hers, and half these men are here because of her.

Act Two · Sixteen years old, the price of a black mule

To understand where this band came from, go back sixteen years.

A man named Shan Tingxiu ran a sizable sorghum-wine distillery in Gaomi Northeast Township. He was rich and he had one son, Shan Bianlang, who had leprosy. In that time and place leprosy was the end of the line; no family would give him a daughter. So the Shans named a price high enough to work: one big black mule. Seed ③

The man who took the mule was Dai Lianfang, a shrewd small trader, and the father of "my grandma." He tied his sixteen-year-old daughter into a bridal sedan chair and traded her for livestock. Every drop of blood in this book flows out of that transaction.

Four men carried the chair. The lead bearer was Yu Zhan'ao, early twenties, unmarried, a hired laborer. Local custom required the bearers to "jolt the chair" — to shake the bride until she was sick, which counted as both festivity and warning. They jolted and they sang, and the songs were filthy. Inside, the girl sat scarlet-faced with a pair of scissors hidden against her chest: she had already decided that if the leper touched her she would stab him first and herself second.

On the road it went wrong. A masked highwayman stopped the chair with a gun and walked the bride into the sorghum. The bearers lay flat and kept their heads down — all except Yu Zhan'ao, who followed and killed the man with his bare hands, and only afterward discovered that the terrifying gun was junk and the robber a small-time nobody.

This was not Yu Zhan'ao's first killing. Seed ④The novel supplies his adolescence later: his widowed mother took up with a monk from the local temple, the village talked, and the teenage Yu finished the monk off. His mother hanged herself soon after. He is not a good man pushed into outlawry. From the beginning he is a man who removes whatever is standing in his way — a disposition that is about to be used again.

Three days later came the customary first return to the bride's parents. On that road Yu Zhan'ao appeared again, took her off the path, donkey and all, and carried her into the sorghum. The novel writes the scene large and without apology: he tramples a circle flat, beats down a nest in the stalks, spreads his coat on the ground. This is the book's true beginning — not a love story, but two people standing in a crop field issuing themselves a permit. "My father," Douguan, is conceived in that trampled circle.

When she got back, the Shans, father and son, were dead. Payoff ④ ↑Cleanly done. The whole township could guess who; nobody could prove it. There was an inquiry and it went nowhere. And so a sixteen-year-old widow took possession of an entire distillery. She did not weep and she did not go home to her parents: she called the workmen into the yard and announced that the place would open as usual tomorrow.

Act Three · Eighteen Li Red, the wine a full bladder made famous

What follows is the most alive stretch in the book. How does a sixteen-year-old girl hold a yard full of veteran distillery hands? Through one man: Uncle Arhat, the Shans' old foreman, whose word carried in that yard. He could have taken the cash and gone. He stayed and held the place together for her. Exactly what the two of them were to each other is something the novel never settles, and never quite drops either.

The distillery reopened. But what actually made the brand called Eighteen Li Red is an episode nobody could print on a label: Yu Zhan'ao, drunk, walked over to a vat and urinated in it. The hands went white and said nothing. The batch came out better than usual — stronger, rounder. Uncle Arhat worked out what had happened and quietly kept doing it. The most famous wine in the district is made with piss, and Mo Yan writes it both fragrant and rank, with no attempt to clean it up. That is the ethic of the whole novel: the good thing and the filthy thing are frequently the same thing.

The household prospered. Three dogs grew up in that yard — one red, one green-black, one black — fed on meat, big, and feared by everyone. Seed ⑤

Yu Zhan'ao moved in around this time, but not to run a business. He raised his own band and swallowed the district's real bandit power, a man called Spotted Neck, who had earlier kidnapped "my grandma" and held her for ransom. After that everyone called Yu "Commander." The command consisted of a few dozen men, guns mostly taken from somebody else, and discipline enforced by fist.

Nor was the house peaceful. Yu later took a second woman, Lian'er — "Second Grandma" — set her up in a separate compound, and had a daughter by her, Xiangguan. "My grandma" responded in character: no scenes, no weeping; she simply divided the household and ran her own. Nobody in this book is morally clean, least of all the two people it loves most.

Act Four · A mule, a spade, and a man's skin

Around 1938 the Japanese arrived, and they arrived in a specific form: road construction. They wanted the Jiao-Ping highway driven through Gaomi Northeast Township, so they conscripted men, requisitioned draft animals, and set up a work site. Uncle Arhat was taken along with the livestock — and among the requisitioned animals were the big black mules that had come into the family with the Shan property.

What happened on the work site needs no elaboration: beatings, hunger, a whip. Uncle Arhat got away one night, and then could not make himself leave. He kept thinking about those mules. So he went back in the dark with a spade in his hand.

The mules no longer knew him and kicked at him. He swung the spade, once, twice, and crippled them where they stood. Payoff ③ ↑The seed for this was planted in a bride price sixteen years earlier: that animal was what turned a girl into merchandise, then followed her into this household, and has now become the last thing anyone in it cannot let go of. What Uncle Arhat is destroying is not really two mules — it is whatever the Japanese have taken out of this family's hands, even though the thing itself was the family's original shame. The move is beautiful because it is not exactly hatred: it is a hired man's unaccountable sense of ownership over animals that were never his.

He was caught. The Japanese wanted an example, herded the township onto the work site, produced the local butcher, Sun Five, and had him flay Uncle Arhat alive in front of everyone.

This passage is the most famous thing in the book and the most attacked. Mo Yan does not cut away and he does not comment. He writes Sun Five's shaking hands; he writes the knife's route, which piece comes off first and what it sounds like; he writes the man on the ground still cursing while it happens, and the curse changing shape; he writes the crowd going quiet, then vomiting, then breaking and running. He also writes that Sun Five lost his mind afterward. This is not spectacle, it is a question: is a man forced to hold the knife a murderer?

The novel does something sly here as well. It produces the written record — the county gazetteer, the commune history, the official martyr's account — and sets it alongside what the family says at home. In the official version Uncle Arhat is a clear-featured anti-Japanese martyr; in the household version he is an old foreman who could not stop thinking about two mules and had something unspoken with his employer's widow. Neither version will yield, and the novel declines to choose for you.

Act Five · Half a morning at the bridge

After the flaying, revenge requires no persuading. Yu Zhan'ao takes his men to the bridge over the Black Water River to ambush the Japanese convoy — which is the march through the fog in Act One.

The tactics are crude: rakes buried in the roadway with their iron teeth up to burst the tires, men hidden in the sorghum, a rush once the trucks stop. Leng's detachment does not come. The homemade cannon fires once and then blows up, throwing the father and son working it flat. When the Japanese machine gun opens, the sorghum goes down in swathes like mown grass, and so do the men. It is not a battle; it is a massacre with one piece of luck in the middle of it. The convoy really is stopped, several Japanese soldiers really are killed, and almost all of Yu's forty-odd men are gone by the end. The people who finally get up and set fire to a truck are farmers who cannot hold a formation.

In the middle of it, "my grandma" comes into the sorghum with her basket of flatbreads. Payoff ② ↑A machine-gun round comes through the stalks and into her chest. She goes down and the bread scatters. The move works because of how ordinary it is: she is not shot charging, she is shot bringing lunch — carrying the homeliest object in the book, and a basket of bread turns out to be exactly as soft as a person in front of a bullet. She lies in the sorghum a long time before she dies, and the interior monologue of that dying is the best writing in the novel (see the next section).

When it is over, Leng's detachment arrives and collects the battlefield at leisure, carrying off the Japanese weapons. Yu Zhan'ao stands among the dead holding his son. Later the Communist-led Jiao-Gao regiment will want to absorb him, and want his guns; so will others. He never joins anyone, and that, not the Japanese, is his real tragedy: there is no establishment he can enter.

Act Six · Dog ways, a funeral, a strange death, and the last pure stalk

Dog Ways comes next, the cruelest of the five novellas, and not one second of it made it into the film.

Dozens of bodies lie in the sorghum with nobody to collect them. The village dogs go hungry, then exploratory, then organized — they divide the work, set ambushes, take turns, and treat the dead as a sustainable food supply. Three lead the packs: the red one, the green-black one, the black one. Payoff ⑤ ↑They are the three from the Yu family yard. The seed was planted in the warmest possible place: those dogs were raised on this household's leftovers, and they are now eating this household's corpses by exactly the same logic. The dogs have not changed; only the source of the food has. Yu Zhan'ao and fourteen-year-old Douguan fight a running battle against their own animals in the sorghum until the ammunition is gone, and the boy is wounded in it. By this point the line between people and beasts has been rubbed until it is no longer visible.

Then Sorghum Funeral. Years later, Yu Zhan'ao gives "my grandma" a burial so lavish it is almost indecent: paper horses and paper servants, musicians, monks and Taoist priests, the whole township turning out to watch, the scale of it like an argument with somebody. A man who could not keep her alive spends everything he has on her funeral — in this book the deepest feeling almost always arrives in the least dignified form.

The darkest of the five is Strange Death, which belongs to Second Grandma, Lian'er. Japanese soldiers break into her compound; her daughter Xiangguan is killed on the spot; she is raped and left broken. She does not die at once. She lingers for days, half lucid, raving, and the country people decide she is possessed by something unclean and bring in someone to "treat" her. Yu Zhan'ao arrives too late for any of it. What is strange about this death is not the dying but the fact that the woman has to be explained, and humiliated, one more time before she is allowed to finish. This novella is the book's own most complete refutation of its own wildness: blood and guts save nobody, and they save women least of all.

The novel closes on the narrator. "I" am a grandson of this family, educated in the city, home again in Gaomi Northeast Township, standing in front of fields of hybrid sorghum — short, dense, lodging-resistant, high-yielding, poor-tasting, grey-green, standing in tidy rows. The old kind, the red going on black that moaned in the wind, is extinct. Payoff ① ↑The living, bleeding sorghum of Act One has become, by the end, a crop that is technically superior and biologically mediocre; and the narrator is sent off to do one thing: find a single stalk of the pure breed. The move is beautiful because it hands the book's verdict to a plant — the sentence the novel cannot quite say in its own voice, "we are smaller than our grandparents," gets said by what is growing in the field.

The story is told; now wind the causes into one fuse. The fuse is not the arrival of the Japanese. The fuse is a wine trader named Dai Lianfang, around 1923, marrying his sixteen-year-old daughter to a leper in exchange for one big black mule. The daughter wanted to live, so there was a trampled circle in the sorghum; the circle needed cleaning up, so the Shans died; their deaths produced a distillery, a famous wine, and a yard run by a woman; in that yard was an old foreman who would not leave; the foreman could not abandon the mules that had bought her, so he went back to the work site with a spade, and so he was skinned; the skinning produced the ambush at the bridge, and the basket of bread scattered in the sorghum, and a field of bodies for the dogs. In the end that mule is what peels the skin off Uncle Arhat.

Three slow-motion replays

1 · Jolting the chair: a comedy staged entirely in the body

Run it again: August sun coming through the curtain of the sedan chair, the inside like a steamer basket. Four barefoot bearers deliberately pitch the chair like a small boat in chop, singing something obscene, stealing looks at the embroidered shoe showing under the curtain. The girl inside is nauseous, sweating through her veil, a pair of scissors pressing against her ribs. Yu Zhan'ao is one layer of cloth away from her, close enough that she can smell him.

Why it lands: the point of view is pinned inside the chair. You are not watching a folk custom, you are in the hot box being shaken. Mo Yan mixes the motion sickness, the sweat, the fear and a first flicker of something else into one sensation — here, desire and travel sickness are the same physiological event, which is precisely his claim: the body starts working before morality does.

And note where it sits. The two cruelest scenes in the novel (the flaying, the dogs) are still ahead, and the book opens with a dirty joke. First it teaches you to enjoy the body; then it makes you watch the same body opened an inch at a time. The comedy is not relief. It is an ambush.

2 · The flaying: why the camera refuses to move

Run it again: yellow dust over the work site, the township herded into a ring. The butcher Sun Five is pushed into the middle and handed a knife — his own, the one he uses on pigs. The Japanese stand at the edge and gesture. His hands will not keep still, and the knife has to go in anyway. Mo Yan writes the job as a job: where the first cut goes, which section is hardest to lift, what it sounds like coming away. The man on the ground is cursing at the start, and the cursing changes into something else. The onlookers hold their breath; then somebody vomits; then they scatter.

Why it lands. First, the scene is anchored on the man who was made to hold the knife, not the victim and not the perpetrator. Put the camera on Uncle Arhat and you have a martyrdom; put it on the Japanese and you have an indictment; put it on Sun Five and you have a question with no clean answer — is a man who is forced to do this one of the killers? Sun Five goes mad afterward, which is the novel's answer: he thinks so.

Second, the crowd. The novel spends real attention on the spectators — the position Lu Xun staked out a century ago (see this site's piece on Call to Arms), which Mo Yan takes over but heats differently: Lu Xun's onlookers are numb, Mo Yan's go numb and then come apart. And the reader is the last member of that crowd who has not run away.

Third, rhythm. The prose of this novel is a flood — piled adjectives, synesthesia, repetition — except here, where it turns spare and hard. The most extravagant writer in modern Chinese fiction goes plain at exactly the moment extravagance was available, and that contrast frightens more than any adjective would have.

3 · Grandma's death: a person leaking out of her own body

Run it again: she is not killed outright. She lies among the stalks, hearing gunfire and shouting somewhere off to the side, seeing one strip of sky with a pigeon turning in it. Blood goes down into the black soil. Consciousness starts to warp: the sedan chair, the coat spread on the ground in the sorghum, steam-filled mornings at the distillery, the man still fighting somewhere and the son who is fourteen. She argues it out with heaven — what exactly, in her whole life, was her crime? Her conclusion is: none. She loved, she took her pleasure, she lived at full weight, and she is not sorry.

Why it lands. First, this is the only time in the book that her interior is handed over whole. For hundreds of pages "my grandma" has been narrated — by her grandson, by legend, by the way men look at her. Only in dying does she get the first person back. The arrangement is cruel and exact: in that world a woman has to be nearly dead before she is entitled to a monologue.

Second, the pacing of sensation. Her dying is not a dimming, it is a brightening — the senses enlarge past realism: the sky bluer, the pigeon's wingbeats clearer, the smell of the earth stronger. The world reaches its fullest exactly as she leaves it, which is far harder to take than any elegy.

Third, what she thinks about at the end. Not the nation, not the resistance, not whether her man is a hero: she audits her own life. The politics of the whole novel hide in that detail. Mo Yan makes this sacrifice completely private, and a private death refuses to be claimed by anybody. She dies inside an anti-Japanese ambush, but she does not die for anyone. She was delivering lunch.

The book's core device: the words "my grandpa"

The real invention in Red Sorghum is not the plot. It is a first-person possessive welded onto a third-person omniscient narration. The whole novel is told by a descendant who says my grandpa, my grandma, my father. Those words look ordinary and are in fact a machine that does at least four jobs.

1. It issues a license to mythologize. The narrator is describing events from decades before his birth; he cannot have been present. But the moment they are "my grandma's," these details no longer need documentary backing — family legend has always been allowed exaggeration, deification, ancestors who are bigger than the living. So the novel can write interior monologue and ghost-talk on the same page without apologizing.

2. It builds the comparison into the grammar. Every my grandpa is also a measurement of the man saying it. The narrator keeps interrupting to insert his contemporary self: that generation could drink, fight and love; this one is pale, soft-legged and calculating. This is the famous thesis of degeneration. Note that it is a figure of speech, not an anthropological finding — the dilemmas section below takes it apart.

3. It lets incompatible versions live side by side. One event, four accounts: the county gazetteer, the commune history, an old man in the village, the family at home. The novel sets them in a row and kills none of them. That is its actual attitude to history: not folk memory overriding official memory, but the two made to face each other while you sit there uncomfortable.

4. It makes the scrambled chronology cheap. The five novellas are organized by person, not by year, and jump constantly; but as long as "I" am still talking, the reader stays oriented, because this is how anyone tells stories about their own family.

The other half of the device is the sorghum itself. It is not setting, it is the book's body: red is the crop, the blood and the wine, and the three are swapped for each other throughout the prose. The sorghum is a bed, a grave, cover, food, the raw material of the wine, and finally the vehicle of the novel's verdict. This is a novel written entirely in one color. One honest note: the method's cost is loss of control — where the adjectives pile up too thickly, the force leaks out. The critics have a point there, as the next-but-one section concedes.

What it makes you think about

Question 1
If "living wild" and "living safe" cannot both be had, which do you pick for your grandchildren?

Take the degeneration case at its strongest. The narrator's evidence is not abstract: his grandfather's generation slept in the sorghum, went at trucks with kitchen knives, killed for a woman, walked back into a guarded work site for two mules. Their feelings came in single pieces — love to the end, hate to the death, no hedging and no explanation offered. His own generation lives in cities, drinks watered wine, says two-thirds of what it means, understands everything and risks nothing. His suspicion is concrete: in the process of being domesticated, does a people lose some irreplaceable intensity of life? Bring it to now: we live longer, cleaner and better-educated than our grandparents, and how many of us have ever once gone all in on anything?

Now the other side, argued harder than its supporters usually argue it. The "wildness" was invoiced to other people, and those people are mostly silent in the ledger. Yu Zhan'ao's vitality includes murder, abduction, a private army, and two women in two compounds. "My grandma's" freedom stands on a murdered leper and a wiped-out household. Strange Death is that bill coming due: a woman raped to death and a small girl killed on the spot get no share whatsoever of the famous life-force. People who feel nostalgic for savagery are imagining themselves holding the knife, not lying on the ground. Worse is the shape of the idea itself: treating "strong," "pure-bred" and "degenerate" as the keys to history is a road that has been walked several times, and a great many people died along it.

What can be said honestly is that the novel itself cannot decide. It writes the ancestors enormous and then uses Dog Ways and Strange Death to press that enormity into the mud. Red Sorghum is not a manifesto of nostalgia; it is a man singing a hymn while privately unconvinced by it. That unease is more honest than either conclusion.

Question 2
A band that belongs to nobody wins a fight. Does it go in the record?

Yu Zhan'ao is a bandit. Nobody sent him, nobody can order him, nobody credits him afterward. Leng's detachment agrees to come, does not come, and arrives afterward to collect the weapons. The Communist-led Jiao-Gao regiment wants to absorb him, and wants his guns. He joins neither.

The case for crediting him is hard to beat: the result is the result. The convoy was really stopped, men really died, and the people who did it were farmers. Admitting this fight means admitting that resistance was not any organization's property but the reflex of people living on a piece of land. That sentence is why the book detonated in 1986 — the official register for this history had been considerably narrower.

The case against is equally hard, and deserves a full hearing: unorganized resistance is ruinously expensive and usually leaves nothing behind. Yu's band fights once and ceases to exist. No supply, no intelligence, no medical care, no next step; the motive is a private grievance and the currency is lives. Leng's "let them fight it first" is ugly, but it points coldly at something real: one magnificent suicidal charge and a resistance apparatus that can keep functioning are not the same object. And the same unregulated force that robs a Japanese convoy today can rob its own villagers tomorrow — the bandits in this book are never only doing good.

Pull it back to us: the question never goes away. Acting alone on your conscience, or joining an organization that has rules, compromises and dirt on it — which one actually changes anything? The first looks better; the second works; and most of us spend our lives choosing a mixture we can live with. Yu Zhan'ao chose the one that looks better. The novel records his courage, and records that his band stopped existing.

Question 3
Is a writer responsible for making violence this good?

The prosecution at full strength: the flaying is technically exquisite — pacing, diction, point of view, what is withheld, all of it precisely judged. And when a torture is written this beautifully, what is the reader actually getting: compassion, or an excitement dressed in fine prose? Literary history has form here: the better violence is written, the more easily it is consumed as style. Mo Yan's later Sandalwood Death is built around an execution, and the charge has followed him ever since. There is a real test in it: if what you take away is "that passage is brutally well done," then the writing has functioned on you more or less the way an interrogation video would.

The defense, also at full strength: not writing it is its own complicity. Compressing a torture into "he died bravely" serves the executioner in the end — it lets posterity imagine an indecent event decently. Mo Yan's refusal is a refusal of that decency: he will not let you skip. And his framing is not built for thrill but for accountability — it stays on Sun Five with the knife and on the township watching, which is to say it stays on how ordinary people participated. Genuinely pornographic violence points the camera at the victim's pain; this passage weights the bystanders.

To your own life: this question now arrives daily. True-crime podcasts, war photography, surveillance footage, the violent clip forwarded forty times. We say "too awful to watch" and watch to the end. The dividing line is probably this: does the depiction force you toward "what would I have done?" If yes, it is literature. If it only leaves you wanting another one, it has already turned into something else.

Question 4
Why does Grandma's freedom have to be delivered by a man with a knife?

The criticism at full strength. "My grandma" is very nearly assembled as an ideal: beautiful, bold, unafraid to want, able to run a business, capable of walking out — and then dying cleanly at the right moment without becoming anyone's burden. But every hinge of her life is operated by Yu Zhan'ao. He kills the highwayman; he kills her husband and her father-in-law; he carries her into the sorghum. Her liberation is handed to her by somebody else's violence. And her longest act of self-expression in the entire book is scheduled after she has been shot. A liberated woman who needs a man's knife to begin and a bullet to speak in full is liberated at a discount. The same line of criticism has followed Mo Yan through Big Breasts and Wide Hips.

The defense, without shortcuts. One, she is not passive: she does not scream for help on that road; she does not run home to her father when the distillery falls to her; when Yu sets up a second household she neither weeps nor fights but divides the estate and runs hers. She chooses every time; the tools available to her are simply these. Two, demanding that she liberate herself unaided is grading a sixteen-year-old girl in 1920s rural Shandong on a contemporary curve — her menu reads: marry the leper, kill yourself, or attach yourself to a man willing to act. She took the third and turned it into her own enterprise. Three, the book's own rebuttal is not located in her but in Second Grandma: inside the same logic, a woman with less luck ends up in Strange Death. The novel attaches that invoice itself.

To now: the modern form of the question is if someone else cleared the ground for your freedom, is it yours? In marriages, in workplaces, inside families, the arrangement where "somebody absorbed it so that I could be myself" is far more common than we like to admit. The novel offers no verdict. It only makes sure you notice that nobody asked the black mule's opinion either — and nobody asked the girl's.

The obsession: what Mo Yan keeps writing

Line his books up and one compulsion recurs: using a folk voice to retell the things that have already been told officially.

Big Breasts and Wide Hips (1995) loads the Chinese twentieth century onto one mother and her string of children — whoever holds power, a child goes with them, while the mother only gives birth and survives. Sandalwood Death (2001) stitches an execution together with Maoqiang, a local Gaomi opera, so that the condemned man sings himself out. Life and Death Are Wearing Me Out (2006) has an executed landlord reincarnated in turn as donkey, ox, pig, dog and monkey, watching half a century of land reform through animal eyes. Frog (2009) sets the deliveries and the terminations of one village obstetrician side by side, and is the rare Mo Yan book written in a penitent key.

Besides Gaomi Northeast Township, two concrete things run through all of it. One is hunger — he writes eating, scavenging and the smell of food at a density almost unmatched in contemporary Chinese fiction, and it is connected to what his own generation lived through. The other is the body. For Mo Yan the body is the last piece of evidence that cannot be revised. Slogans change, archives are amended, the list of heroes and villains is reissued — but a skin taken off a man, a child pushed into the world, a bladder emptied into a vat of wine are hard facts that occurred. All his excess, coarseness and overflow exist to hold that line.

Red Sorghum is the first link in that chain and the most hot-blooded. The later books are more complicated, more bitter and better at arithmetic, but none of them has this one's unreasonable vitality.

Criticism and controversy

Without flattery: a substantial part of what is said against this book and this author holds up.

  • The weight of the violence. The flaying, the corpse-eating dogs, Strange Death — these passages were argued over in the 1980s and remain the most attacked thing he has written. The charge is not "he should not have written it" but "he wrote it too deliciously." Sandalwood Death pushed further down the same road and made the charge stick harder.
  • The women. Many scholars read "my grandma" as a male projection: wild, beautiful, sexually forward, and dead at the convenient moment. Second Grandma's fate is criticized as the old habit of carrying national humiliation on a woman's body. The defense notes that she is the only character granted a full interior monologue. The argument has not been settled.
  • The degeneration thesis itself. Essentializing vitality, and using pure-breed versus hybrid as a key to history, has been criticized as a romantic and quasi-biological view of the past. The sympathetic reading is that it is the narrator's self-mockery rather than the author's program — but the novel does not install a clear brake on it.
  • Visible seams. It is five novellas joined together; characters and dates repeat and occasionally contradict, and the fourth and fifth parts are looser than the first two. A good many readers love only Red Sorghum and Sorghum Wine.
  • Stylistic excess. Adjectives, synesthesia and anaphora are laid on very thick: at best a flood, at worst a howl. It is the most imitated and most criticized thing about him.
  • The film swallowed the book. Zhang Yimou's Red Sorghum, shot in 1987 and awarded the Golden Bear in Berlin in 1988, made Gong Li, Jiang Wen and that field of red into a generational memory. But the film takes only the first two novellas, cuts Dog Ways and Strange Death entirely, and handles the ending its own way. Many people who believe they have read this book have seen only its brightest third.
  • The author himself. After the 2012 Nobel, public argument about Mo Yan concentrated on several verifiable facts: he has long served as a vice-chairman of the China Writers Association; in 2011 he took part in a commemorative project in which writers hand-copied Mao Zedong's "Talks at the Yan'an Forum on Literature and Art"; and at the Nobel press conference in Stockholm he compared censorship to airport security screening, which drew a strong reaction. Herta Müller and others publicly criticized the award; Kenzaburō Ōe, among others, had long championed his work. Facts only here; the judgment is yours.

Ten sentences to take away

1. This is not a war novel. It is a grandson telling his grandparents' story, who happens to cover a war on the way through.

2. The fuse is not the Japanese entering the village; it is a father trading his sixteen-year-old daughter for one big black mule.

3. "My grandpa" is the book's real invention: two domestic words that simultaneously deify, self-deprecate, and challenge the official record.

4. The secret of the district's most famous wine is a full bladder — in Mo Yan's world the good thing and the filthy thing are frequently the same thing.

5. The flaying is not framed on the victim but on the butcher holding the knife and the township watching: it is a problem about complicity.

6. Grandma is not shot charging. She is shot delivering lunch, with a basket of bread in her hands.

7. Her dying monologue is the one time the first person is returned to her — in that world a woman must be nearly dead to be allowed to speak for herself.

8. The dogs that eat the corpses were raised by this family. The dogs did not change; only the source of the food did.

9. Degeneration is a figure of speech, not a finding. To use it as a finding you must first pay the bill attached to Strange Death.

10. What stays with you is not the ambush but the last image: tidy rows of hybrid sorghum, and a man sent out to find one pure stalk.
