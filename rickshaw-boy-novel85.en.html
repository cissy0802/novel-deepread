Novels, Deep-Read · Lao She · No.85

Rickshaw Boy

Lao She · 1939 · 24 chapters · also published as Camel Xiangzi

中文 →

The rickshaw is new. Its black lacquer is glossy enough to show a face, the brass fittings have been polished and polished again, and the seat cushion still smells of the shop.
Xiangzi pulls it out onto the street and does not go looking for a fare right away. He walks it empty for a while, just to listen to the new rubber tires turning against the road. He is barely past twenty, tall, straight-backed, and three years of copper coins saved one at a time are now, all of them, in the two shafts under his hands.
He makes a decision: from now on, today is his birthday — his and the rickshaw's both. He never did know what day he was born. Now he has one.

This page contains complete spoilers — how each of Xiangzi's three rickshaws (and the money he hoarded in a clay jar) is lost, how Tigress dies, what becomes of Xiao Fuzi, and what kind of man Xiangzi turns into at the end. Without the final chapter there is no way to argue about whether this book indicts a city or passes judgment on a man.

In One Line

In late-1920s Peking, a young rickshaw puller from the countryside sets out to own his own rickshaw by nothing but strength and thrift; three times he nearly makes it, and three times it is taken — by soldiers, by a detective, by marriage and death — until his last hope, a girl named Xiao Fuzi, hangs herself in the woods outside the city wall, and the most decent, most driven man in Peking becomes someone who sells another man for sixty dollars and shuffles at the back of funeral processions, eyes on the gutter, looking for cigarette butts.

Where It Sits

Lao She (pen name of Shu Qingchun, 1899–1966) was born into a poor Manchu bannerman family in Peking and made his name, while teaching in London in the 1920s, with satirical novels about Peking life. Rickshaw Boy was the first full-length novel he wrote after giving up his post at Shandong University in 1936 to write full-time in Qingdao. It was serialized in the magazine Yuzhou feng (Cosmic Wind) from the autumn of 1936 and came out as a book in 1939.

Its square on the map of literature: the first modern Chinese novel written from inside the body of an urban laborer. Lu Xun had sketched a rickshaw man in a single page ("A Small Incident"), but nobody had filled an entire novel with a puller's appetite, his leg strength, his coin counts, his sweat and his illnesses, and the slow, clumsy arithmetic going on in his head. In a 1945 essay on how he wrote the book, Lao She said it started from two things a friend mentioned in passing: a puller who bought a rickshaw three times and lost it three times, and another who was pressed into service by an army and, escaping, led off three of its camels. Those two anecdotes are the skeleton. The flesh is the whole city of Peking.

The Story

Act I · A rickshaw, and a birthday

The novel's very first sentence sets the record straight: the man we are introducing, it says, is Xiangzi, not the camel. "Camel" is only a nickname. Its origin comes later.

Xiangzi came to the city at eighteen. His parents were dead and the few poor acres were gone; he arrived in Peking with a farmhand's big frame and a farmhand's stubbornness, and he chose pulling a rickshaw because it asked for nothing but strength and owed nobody anything. He doesn't smoke, drink or gamble. Foreshadow ①The narrator keeps reaching for two words to describe him: driven, and decent — a man who wants to stand on his own and keep his self-respect. In Xiangzi's own accounting both words point to a single object: a rickshaw of his own. Own the rickshaw and you stop paying daily rent to a rickshaw yard; whatever you earn is yours. Own the rickshaw and you are a man who can stand.

Three years. He saved from his food money, coin by coin, and one day he brought home the rickshaw of the opening. That was the day he gave himself a birthday. From then on, the man and the machine share a single life — nobody in the novel says it aloud, but every later page cashes it in.

Within half a year there is trouble outside the walls. Rumor says soldiers have reached the Western Hills, that there is fighting to the southwest, and pullers stop taking fares out of the city. Then a passenger offers two dollars to go out to Tsinghua — several days' earnings. Xiangzi's stomach tightens, and he goes anyway: he wants to get ahead fast, and he trusts his legs. Not far past the gate, a band of retreating soldiers stops him. The rickshaw is confiscated; he is put to work hauling their gear and trudges around the hills with them for days. Foreshadow ②The man in charge of the captured laborers is a platoon leader named Sun.

One night the troops break camp again in chaos. Xiangzi slips away in the dark, stumbles onto three camels the soldiers have abandoned, and, without thinking much, leads them off. He doesn't know the roads; he only knows roughly which way the city lies, and his thin clothes let the wind straight through. Near dawn he reaches a village and sells the three camels to an old man for thirty-five dollars — small change next to the rickshaw, but at least something clawed back from the soldiers.

He falls ill on the way back and walks into the city burning with fever, mumbling about camels. Once the story gets around, the pullers start calling him Camel Xiangzi. The nickname was handed to him and has nothing to do with his ambitions: the one windfall of his life left him nothing but a name.

Act II · The Renhe yard: savings in Fourth Master Liu's keeping

He goes back to the yard where he always rented, the Renhe rickshaw yard. Its owner, Fourth Master Liu, is nearly seventy; in his youth he ran gambling dens, lent money at murderous interest and used a knife when he had to. Now he owns sixty-odd rickshaws and lives on the rent. The person who actually runs the place — the books, the men, the vehicles — is his only daughter, Tigress (Huniu): thirty-seven or thirty-eight, broad-faced, loud, quicker at the work than any man. Old Liu cannot do without her, which is exactly why he has never let her marry. She is his bookkeeper, not anybody's wife.

Xiangzi leaves the camel money with Liu for safekeeping, rents a rickshaw and goes back to work like a man possessed: poaching fares, skipping rest, until the other pullers mutter that he is stealing their rice bowls. He cares only that the number keeps growing.

He takes a live-in job with a family named Yang, where two wives take turns ordering him about and feed him scraps. After four days he walks out without his wages and carries his bedroll back to the Renhe yard late at night. The yard is empty except for Tigress, who has set out food and liquor and pulls him in to drink. He can't hold drink and doesn't know how to refuse. After that night he feels as if something filthy has stuck to him that will not wash off: he looks down on Tigress, he is afraid of her, and above all he is afraid that this has tied him to Liu's yard.

Act III · The Cao household: the months that came closest to a good life

He gets away by taking a monthly position pulling for Mr. Cao, a teacher with some modern ideas and a kind manner. The family's shrewd maid, Gao Ma, takes a liking to the big silent man too. These are the most human months he has had in the city: light work, full meals, wages on time. He feeds his coins one at a time into a clay money jar of the kind that has a slot to go in and no way out.

Foreshadow ③Gao Ma can't bear to watch him save so stupidly. She is an old hand at small loans and at the rotating savings clubs neighbors run among themselves, and she urges him: money sitting in a jar doesn't breed. Lend it out at interest, or put it in the post-office savings bank, and you'll have your rickshaw that much sooner. Xiangzi listens, nods, and does nothing. To him, money is only money while it is in his own hand — what if a loan never comes back? What if the bank won't give it back?

One dark night, pulling Mr. Cao home, he fails to see a heap of road-repair stones and the rickshaw overturns. Cao cuts his hand; Xiangzi scrapes his knees and face. Horrified, he offers to quit and pay for the damage. Cao won't hear of it. The small kindness sinks deep. So there are employers like this.

But the yard comes after him. One winter night Tigress turns up at the Cao gate, calls him out, and tells him: she's pregnant. She has already worked out his path for him. Her father's birthday is coming in the first month of the new year; Xiangzi is to go back and help with the celebration, get into the old man's good graces, and then she will find a way to bring up the marriage. She says it hard and fast and leaves him no room at all.

His head in turmoil, he sits that night in a little teahouse on the corner. The door curtain lifts and an old puller comes in, shaking with cold, sits down, and topples off the bench — fainted from hunger. When they bring him round, his first words are that his little grandson is outside with the rickshaw. These are Old Ma and Little Ma. Xiangzi goes out and comes back with ten steamed mutton buns. The old man hardly eats any; most go to the boy.

Foreshadow ④Revived, Old Ma says something like this: in our line, don't imagine one man can make it on his own. Look at a grasshopper. On its own it can jump a good way, but a child catches it, ties a thread to its leg, and it can't fly at all. Get them in a swarm, though, and they'll strip a whole field in one pass. Then he takes his grandson and leaves. Xiangzi has just watched himself forty years on. Old Ma once owned his own rickshaw too. It didn't help.

Near the year's end, trouble comes for Mr. Cao. One of his students, Ruan Ming, fails his course and promptly denounces his teacher to the authorities for spreading "radical ideas" in class. One evening the Caos slip away in a hurry, leaving Xiangzi to mind the house. A plainclothesman blocks his way and introduces himself as Detective Sun: Mr. Cao is a wanted subversive, his rickshaw man is implicated too, so either come along or pay for your life. Xiangzi looks up and recognizes him.

Payoff ② ↑It's Platoon Leader Sun, the man who ran the laborers in the hills. In a time of chaos he wore a uniform and took the rickshaw; in a time of order he wears a plain gown and comes knocking at the door. In Act I he was just one more officer among the stragglers, a face you read past. When Lao She brings him back in a change of clothes, the point lands all at once: what robs Xiangzi is the same thing from start to finish — it wears a uniform in bad times and civilian clothes in good ones.

Payoff ③ ↑Sun asks how much he has. Xiangzi goes inside, brings out the clay jar, and smashes it in front of him. Thirty-some dollars, every coin handed over. Gao Ma had told him to lend it or bank it, and he had refused because money out of his hands wasn't safe. So the money stayed right beside him, in a jar with no way out, and a man with the authority to knock on doors took the lot in a minute. He had guarded against every trap the marketplace could set, and never thought of this one.

Act IV · The birthday feast, and the tenement at Maojiawan

With nowhere else to go, he returns to the Renhe yard. For Liu's birthday he works inside and out like one of the family. At the feast, the old man drinks, looks out over the courtyard full of guests, and grows sourer by the minute: the guests are mostly pullers, he has no son to make a show for him, only an aging daughter who keeps drifting over to that rickshaw man.

After the guests leave, father and daughter go at each other. Tigress decides to say it outright: she is carrying Xiangzi's child and she means to marry him. Liu, shaking with rage, accuses Xiangzi of using his rickshaw as cover to scheme for the family fortune, and roars at his daughter: marry him, then, and you won't get one copper from me. Tigress is her father's child and doesn't bend an inch: keep it. Everyone in that scene is cursing Xiangzi, and from beginning to end not one of them asks him what he wants.

Tigress pays for everything out of her own savings. She rents two small rooms in a crowded tenement courtyard at Maojiawan, in the west of the city, hires a bridal sedan chair, and marries herself off. On the wedding night she laughs and tells him there never was a baby: she had stuffed a pillow into the waist of her trousers. Xiangzi sits there as if he's been clubbed from behind.

Her plan is clear. Xiangzi will stop pulling — it ruins your health and it's beneath them. With her money they'll start a small business, and once the old man cools off they'll go back to the yard, which will be theirs in the end anyway. Xiangzi refuses. He can do without anything except the rickshaw: without it, he no longer knows who he is. They wrangle over this for a long time, until word comes that Liu has sold off the yard, pocketed the money and gone off traveling, nobody knows where. Tigress's calculations come to nothing.

Another family lives in the courtyard: a puller named Erqiangzi, a drinker who beats his wife when drunk. Not long before, he sold his elder daughter, nineteen-year-old Xiao Fuzi, to an army officer. With the money he bought a rickshaw; before long his wife was dead from his beatings, and the rickshaw was sold. It is Erqiangzi's rickshaw that Tigress buys cheap for Xiangzi. Pulling it always leaves a bad taste in his mouth. This was paid for with a daughter. He pulls it anyway. It is his third: the first he earned, the second never left the clay jar, and this one his wife bought.

Later Xiao Fuzi comes back; the officer has been posted elsewhere and has dropped her. There is no mother at home, the father is a drunk, and two half-grown brothers need feeding. The only thing she has left to sell is herself. Tigress lends her own room for the "business" and takes a cut.

Act V · Sun, storm, and a birth that goes wrong

That summer the heat is unnatural. At noon there's no breath of wind, the willow branches hang dead still, and the road surface softens in the sun. Xiangzi won't rest — he needs to prove he can still pull and still earn. He goes out under the full sun, gulps cold water, cramps, and pulls anyway.

A few days later the sky goes black without warning, a cold wind rips through, and then the rain comes down hard enough to sting like gravel. He runs with a fare through the downpour; at the destination the passenger doesn't add a cent. Xiangzi gets home and collapses, and he is ill for more than a month. He is never again a man who believes his strength cannot run out.

During these months Tigress becomes pregnant — for real this time. She has never done heavy work, and pregnant she sits and eats all day and grows enormous. When her time comes, the baby lies wrong and will not come out. The midwife they call can do nothing; then they bring in Second Granny Chen, a spirit medium who claims the gods heal through her, and she burns incense, kowtows, chants, and asks for money again and again. A hospital? That takes money, and there is none. Tigress labors for days and nights, and in the middle of one night she dies, the child dying with her.

A funeral costs money. Xiangzi sells the rickshaw. The third rickshaw buys a coffin.

While Tigress was ill, it was Xiao Fuzi who brought water and wiped her brow. After the death, Xiangzi finds he cares for this girl — gentle, sensible, able to swallow any hardship, everything Tigress was not. She looks at him the same way. But Xiangzi can do the sums: marry her and he takes on her drunken father and two brothers as well, and he no longer even has a rickshaw. How would he feed them? Foreshadow ⑤He tells her: wait for me. Once I've got on my feet, I'll come for you. He moves out of the courtyard.

Act VI · The end of the road

He takes another live-in job, this time with the Xia household. Mr. Xia is getting on in years; the young "Mrs. Xia" is a woman he set up separately from his real family. Before long she draws the big rickshaw man into her room. Xiangzi doesn't push her away — this time he doesn't even feel much shame, only that nothing matters. He comes away with a venereal infection and quits the job.

One day he runs into Fourth Master Liu on the street. Back from his travels, the old man wants to know where his daughter is. Xiangzi tells him: dead. Where is she buried? I'm not telling you. He turns and walks off, leaving the old man standing in the middle of the street. It is the only time in his life that Xiangzi "wins," and the way he wins is by taking an old man's last hope. He feels good about it for quite a while.

Still riding that feeling, he goes to see Mr. Cao, who is back in Peking. Cao hears him out and says: come back and pull for me. If the girl is willing, she can come too and help around the house, with room and board. For Xiangzi the sky suddenly clears. He runs back to Maojiawan.

Xiao Fuzi is gone. Erqiangzi says only that she left. Xiangzi asks everywhere, and the trail leads outside Xizhi Gate to the "White House," the lowest grade of brothel. A woman there, nicknamed Flour Sack, tells him: yes, there was a girl like that. Not long after she came, she hanged herself in the woods out back.

Payoff ⑤ ↑"Once I've got on my feet" — when Xiangzi said it, he meant it, and he was being sensible: he would not take on a whole household before he could stand. The day he was waiting for had in fact arrived: Mr. Cao had given him work and offered her a place. He calculated everything except this: someone with no way out cannot afford to wait. In Act V the line reads like a responsible promise. Looking back, it is the deadliest bit of prudence in the book.

Later he meets Old Ma again. Little Ma has died, and the old man's rickshaw has been sold.

The novel then spends a long stretch on Xiangzi's descent, layer by layer. He never goes back to the Caos. He smokes, drinks, visits brothels; borrows and never repays; pockets money friends have pooled for a gift and never delivers it; dodges work wherever he can, cheats wherever he can, and stops pulling properly. Street demonstrations are fashionable, and some organizers pay men to carry banners and shout slogans, so Xiangzi carries banners. The man arranging much of this is the same Ruan Ming who denounced his teacher, now making a living organizing pullers and taking money from whichever side pays. When someone offers a price, Xiangzi sells Ruan Ming out for sixty dollars. Ruan Ming is paraded through the streets and shot.

In the end Xiangzi's trade is walking in weddings and funerals for hire: holding a banner or a ceremonial umbrella in the procession, at the very back, inching along with his head down, his eyes on the ground for cigarette butts other people have thrown away.

Payoff ① ↑The novel's last sentence brings back the opening's two words exactly as they were — and sets them at the head of a long string. Decent, driven, full of dreams, it begins, and keeps counting until it reaches degenerate, selfish, unfortunate, and lands on its final phrase: the dead-end ghost of individualism. In Chapter One, "driven" and "decent" were the narrator's highest praise. In the last sentence, the same two words open an obituary. Lao She changed nothing about them. He just put them first in the line, with the rest of the man's fate standing behind.

Payoff ④ ↑"Individualism" appears with this kind of weight only once, at the very end, but it was thought of much earlier. Old Ma's grasshopper speech in the teahouse is its exact opposite: a lone grasshopper, however far it jumps, ends up on a thread; to survive you need the swarm. Xiangzi was the lone grasshopper all along. He despised pullers who clubbed together, wouldn't lend or join a savings circle, trusted nothing but his own legs. In Chapter Ten the speech reads like an old man's grumbling. Only on the last page does it show itself as the one alternative the book ever pointed to — and Lao She gave that alternative just one spokesman in the novel: Ruan Ming, who sells slogans for cash and is sold in turn by Xiangzi.

If this book has a fuse, it is not the soldiers, nor Detective Sun, nor Tigress. The fuse is the birthday in Chapter One: the moment Xiangzi decided the day he bought a rickshaw was the day he was born, he staked his whole sense of who he was on a vehicle. After that, every rickshaw the city took from him was a killing; every time he got back up, it was to stake that life again. After three times, with no rickshaw, there was no self left. The smoking, the borrowing, the betrayal that follow are not a good man suddenly turning bad. They are a man who is only a body now, using up the days that remain.

Three Slow-Motion Scenes

One · Leading three camels through the dark

Replay: the camp in uproar, men shouting, animals bellowing. Xiangzi edges out into the dark, and his hand meets coarse hair and warm breath — camels. He takes their leads and walks in what he hopes is the right direction. No lamp, no road. The camels go one step at a time, so he does too. He is hungry, cold, frightened, and his mind is almost empty.

The technique here is lowering the point of view to the height of the body. The narration records only the rise and fall of the ground underfoot, the cold on his skin, the shapes of hills in the distance. This is a man who does not think in words; he thinks with his body — and so the reader loses the overview too, and is left with the patch of ground just under his feet. The camels are his perfect mirror: big, silent, built to carry and to endure, plodding forward without complaint. They sold for thirty-five dollars and left him their name. The rest of his life is this night, enlarged.

Two · Ten mutton buns in a teahouse

Replay: a winter night, a small teahouse full of pullers, tobacco smoke, sweat, a kettle on the stove. The curtain lifts, cold air rushes in with an old man. He sits, says a few words, and slides off the bench to the floor. When he comes to, his first sentence is about the child outside. Xiangzi gets up, goes out, and comes back cradling a bundle of steaming mutton buns.

It is the only time in the book Xiangzi spends money on someone else — a man who feeds every copper into a jar. Lao She sets it on his most panicked night (Tigress has just told him she's pregnant), so the buns are kindness and also a half-conscious act of self-rescue: he is feeding his own future. Technically, Old Ma is a double. He doesn't move the plot. He appears exactly twice: once to give the grasshopper speech, once to say his grandson is dead. The distance between those two appearances is the whole distance Xiangzi travels from "still hoping" to "past hope."

Three · The sun and the storm

Replay: first, a sun so fierce it seems to melt people; the dust in the street is hot, even the dogs pant with their tongues hanging. Then, a few afternoons later, the weather turns in an instant. A wild wind drives the dust, and the rain smashes down; the streets become rivers in a minute. Xiangzi charges on, head down, clothes plastered to him, teeth chattering, while the passenger huddles under the rain cover and complains he's slow.

What makes these chapters terrible is that there is no villain in them. No soldiers, no detective, no Tigress, no Fourth Master Liu. What breaks Xiangzi is weather — the most ordinary torment a city can inflict on someone who lives by his muscles. The technique is duration: a single afternoon spread across pages, so the reader has to endure every minute alongside him. And that is the book's coldest argument: take away every bad person, and the job itself will still kill him. The earlier disasters took things from him. This one wears him down.

The Book's Core Device

One · Three rises, three falls: a novel built by elimination

Strip it down and the book's structure is a single repeated action: save, buy, lose; save again, lose again. Lao She took his friend's anecdote — three rickshaws bought, three lost — and turned it into a machine for eliminating excuses. Each time, a different force takes Xiangzi's rickshaw away.

The first time it's violence (stragglers seize it) — you can still blame chaotic times. The second time it's authority (a detective's shakedown) — order has returned and he is robbed anyway. The third time it's life itself (marriage, childbirth, death, a funeral) — nobody robs him at all, and the rickshaw is still gone. After three rounds, every excuse a reader might grab is gone: not bad luck, not one villain, not one bad year. What the machine sets out to prove is that in this city, a puller with nothing but strength and good character cannot hold on to a rickshaw, whatever he does.

To be honest about it: this is a novel's argument, not a sociological finding. The only person in the book who builds a fortune, Fourth Master Liu, did it not by pulling but with gambling dens, loan-sharking and a knife — the one man who got ahead here is precisely the one who never cared about being decent. That makes the argument sharper, and also more like an experiment with a carefully chosen sample.

Two · A man who can't speak, and a narrator who speaks for him

Xiangzi barely talks. His lines are pitifully few — a grunt, a nod, silence. What goes on inside him isn't so much thought as reckoning: how many coppers a day, how many months to make the total, whether to answer that remark or not. Lao She's solution is a narrator who stays close against him and says, in rich colloquial Peking speech, what Xiangzi cannot. The sentences belong to the narrator; the calculations and the grievances belong to Xiangzi. The reader is inside him and can also see the traps he can't.

In the last two chapters this narrator changes. It stops pressing close to Xiangzi, steps back, and starts handing down verdicts — degenerate, selfish, the dead-end ghost of individualism. It is the most audible break in the book's voice and the most argued-over (see "Controversy" below). One can also read it as a deliberate withdrawal: once Xiangzi has stopped thinking anything at all, the voice that did his thinking has nothing left to do but judge.

What It Makes You Think About

One · At the end Xiangzi sells a man for sixty dollars. Is that on him, or on the city?

The novel gives two answers in a single sentence: he is a child born of a sick society, and he is the dead-end ghost of individualism.

First, the strongest case that it isn't all his fault. This man did everything right that a man could do: no smoking, no drinking, no gambling; he saved, kept his word, was loyal to his employer, and handed over his own money to feed a fainted old man. Then soldiers robbed him, a detective shook him down, his wife died in front of him, and the girl he loved hanged herself in the woods. To demand that a man uprooted three times stand up straight a fourth time is itself a luxury — the kind of demand made by people on dry ground of people up to their necks in water. And Ruan Ming was a man who first betrayed his teacher and then sold revolution for profit; in selling him, Xiangzi was simply the hand through which the city's rules were carried out, this time.

The other side is just as hard: the same city, the same misery, and Xiao Fuzi sold no one, Old Ma sold no one. Xiao Fuzi sold only herself, to feed her brothers; Old Ma fainted from hunger and asked about his grandson the moment he woke. If circumstance explains everything, how do you explain those two? To lay it all on society is to say the poor have no moral agency — which is the deepest contempt you can show them. However bad Ruan Ming was, a life ended, and Xiangzi put out his own hand for the sixty dollars.

Where does the author stand? He wrote both sides and refused to let them cancel. Bring it home: when someone you know becomes, after blow upon blow, someone you don't — borrowing without repaying, lying, hurting people — do you forgive first or hold them to account first? And would you hold yourself to the same standard?

Two · Is Tigress the hunter, or another animal in a cage?

First, the strongest case that she is the aggressor. She is more than ten years older than Xiangzi, has money, family property and cunning; he is a poor young man with no way out. She opens with liquor and closes with a pillow. This marriage rests, from its first day, on a lie and on coercion — reverse the sexes and no modern reader would hesitate for a second. She also treats Xiao Fuzi's body as a source of rent and takes a cut.

The other side: Tigress is also one of Fourth Master Liu's assets. To keep a bookkeeper who costs him no wages, her father let her reach her late thirties unmarried; her competence earned her only "he can't do without her," never a life of her own. Choosing Xiangzi may be the one time in the book anybody does something not out of calculation but out of wanting. Her methods are ugly — but what other cards did she hold? In that world, how was an aging daughter whom no one courted supposed to choose a man for herself? In the end she dies in childbirth, because there was no money for a hospital, only for a spirit medium. She was killed by the same city, at the same prices, as Xiangzi.

The author's position is hard to pin down: the narration often carries a disgust at her body and her desire, yet the chapter of her death has no gloating in it at all. Bring it home: in a relationship, when the one with more money says "it's for your own good," can the one with less say no? If not, is it still good?

Three · "Wait until I'm on my feet, then I'll come for you" — prudence, or abandonment?

First, the strongest case that he did the right thing. He has no rickshaw; he has just sold one to pay for a funeral. Behind Xiao Fuzi stand a drunken father and two brothers. Marry her on the spot and they both go under — four mouths on a puller who has been seriously ill and can no longer do heavy work. The likeliest ending is that he gets dragged down too and she has to go on selling herself anyway. Not making a promise you can't keep while you can't even stand isn't coldness. It's taking responsibility for her. And he did, in the end, come back for her.

The other side: telling someone who is already drowning "wait until I learn to swim" — how is that different from not saving them? Xiao Fuzi's situation isn't "a hard life"; she is being used up day by day, and what she can't afford to wait isn't a year, it's a month. Xiangzi's prudence is the flaw of his whole life: he is always waiting for the moment when conditions are right — save enough, then buy; get on my feet, then marry her — and this city never grants the poor a moment when conditions are right. The shrewdness he used to protect himself is exactly what cost him the person he most wanted to protect.

Where does the author stand? He made Mr. Cao's offer arrive just one step too late, and that is half an answer in itself. Bring it home: have you ever told someone "once I'm settled," "once I have time," "once this busy stretch is over"? Was that person waiting too?

Motif: What Lao She Kept Writing About

One: the poor of Peking, and the "decency" they guard with their lives. Lao She's people are usually poor and care about face to the point of death. In the novella This Life of Mine, a policeman follows the rules and does his duty all his life and ends with nothing. In Four Generations Under One Roof, the alleys of occupied Peking go on haggling over etiquette and face while the country falls. Rickshaw Boy takes the motif to its cruelest point: decency is Xiangzi's only property, and the last thing the city strips off him.

Two: women forced to sell themselves, and not just once in a generation. The novella Crescent Moon (1935) reads almost like Xiao Fuzi's prequel: a girl watches her mother sell her body to keep them alive, then grows up to walk the same road. Xiao Fuzi is sold by her father, sells herself for her brothers, and hangs herself — Lao She returns to this road again and again, and always starts from "she had no other choice," never from "she fell."

Three: how a good person comes to ruin inside a bad structure. By the late play Teahouse (1957), the motif becomes three eras pressing down on a single teahouse: the proprietor Wang Lifa spends his life smiling, adapting, modernizing, and in the end hangs himself in his own teahouse. From Xiangzi to Wang Lifa, Lao She keeps asking the same question: if a person does his own small part to perfection, is that ever enough?

Controversy and Criticism

The ending, and the author's own revisions. The last two chapters' verdict on Xiangzi — degenerate, selfish, the dead-end ghost of individualism — has always been the flashpoint. Some read it as the book's strongest move, a refusal to romanticize the poor; others see a narrator who abruptly turns from sympathizer to judge and undercuts twenty-odd chapters of argument. After 1949, Lao She himself criticized the book, saying in effect that he had seen only the pullers' suffering and had failed to show them a way out. For a revised edition from the People's Literature Publishing House in the 1950s he toned down the sexual passages and cut long stretches near the end (chiefly the Ruan Ming thread and Xiangzi's final degradation). Most standard editions since the 1980s restore the original. The same book exists in a version the author cut with his own hand; which edition you read changes which Xiangzi you meet.

The English "happy ending." The first English translation, by Evan King, published in the United States in 1945 as Rickshaw Boy, became a bestseller — but the translator rewrote freely: Xiangzi became "Happy Boy," and the ending was changed so that he rescues Xiao Fuzi and the two escape together. Lao She was not pleased with it. Later translations (Howard Goldblatt's of 2010, for one) restore the original ending. A novel about a good man who gets no good ending first reached English readers with a good ending — a small footnote on what the market wanted.

The women. This is one of the most durable criticisms today. Three women decide Xiangzi's fate: Tigress traps him with desire and money, Mrs. Xia infects him, and Xiao Fuzi is the pure one he wanted and failed to save. A tigress, a temptress, a saint — women either drag men down or are the ideal men lose. Tigress's body and appetite often come wrapped in a layer of disgust; Lao She's defenders say that is Xiangzi's eye, not the author's.

The label of "individualism." Another line of criticism holds that blaming Xiangzi's failure on individualism partly lets the society of his time off the hook, and partly speaks up for a vague "collective" — when the only collective organizer in the novel, Ruan Ming, is an opportunist who betrays his teacher and peddles ideals. Lao She delivers the verdict "the individual can't make it" without supplying evidence that "the collective can." Some call that honesty; others call it a contradiction.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · the scarcity mindset (Mullainathan and Shafir): In Scarcity, the two researchers argue that chronic lack makes attention "tunnel" until only the immediate shortfall is visible. Xiangzi's mind holds a single ledger, the rickshaw fund — the same shape. Where it differs: they describe bandwidth used up; the novel shows a man staking his whole dignity on that one ledger.
  • Buddhism · upādāna (clinging): In the chain of dependent origination, craving gives way to clinging, which grips the wanted thing as "mine" and gives rise to becoming — an identity. Xiangzi taking the day he bought his rickshaw as his birthday is exactly the object turning into the self. Where it differs: Buddhism counsels letting go, but for Xiangzi the rickshaw is no illusion; it is his rice bowl, and letting go means going hungry.
  • Philosophy · Camus's Sisyphus: The boulder goes up and rolls back down, and Camus says we must imagine Sisyphus happy, because he sees the absurd clearly and pushes anyway. Xiangzi's three rises and falls trace the same loop. Where it differs: Xiangzi never sees clearly and never rebels; after the third fall he simply stops pushing. Lao She writes the man who has put the stone down.
  • Philosophy of science · Mill's method of agreement: In A System of Logic, Mill proposed that when varied cases share only one circumstance, that circumstance is likely the cause. Soldiers, a detective, then birth and death take the rickshaw; the one constant is "a puller in this city" — the very logic of the novel's elimination device. Where it differs: the method wants cases that arise on their own, and these three were hand-picked by the author.

Ten Lines That Linger

① Xiangzi made the day he bought a rickshaw his birthday, and from then on the man and the machine had one life between them.

② His only windfall ever was three camels, worth thirty-five dollars and a nickname that had nothing to do with his dreams.

③ Platoon Leader Sun took his rickshaw; Detective Sun took his savings — one man, a uniform for bad times and a plain gown for good ones.

④ He wouldn't lend his money out because that wasn't safe; it stayed in a jar at his elbow, and a man with the right to knock took all of it.

⑤ On the night of the birthday feast everyone cursed him, and nobody asked what he wanted.

⑥ There are no villains in the chapters of sun and storm, and that is what makes them so frightening.

⑦ The third rickshaw bought a coffin.

⑧ "Once I'm on my feet" was sincere and it was prudent; the one waiting for him couldn't afford to wait.

⑨ Old Ma said a lone grasshopper ends up on a thread however far it jumps — the only other road the book ever points to, offered by a fainting old man.

⑩ The two words that praised him at the start, "driven" and "decent," come back unchanged at the end, standing at the head of an obituary.
