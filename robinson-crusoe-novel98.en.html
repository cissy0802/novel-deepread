Robinson Crusoe

Robinson Crusoe · Daniel Defoe · 1719 · novel · first-person "true history"

中文 →

One day about noon, walking down to where he kept his boat, he saw a footprint on the sand. A bare foot: toes, heel, every part of it, perfectly clear. Just one.
He stood there as if struck by lightning. He looked around, listened, climbed a rise to scan the shore, came back and walked up and down — no second print, anywhere.
He ran home to his wall looking over his shoulder the whole way, and every bush was a man, and every old stump was a man. He did not sleep that night.
He had lived alone on the island for something like fifteen years, longing for another human being. Now the trace of one lay at his feet, and he was shaking with fear.

This piece contains full spoilers — how he reached the island, how many years he stayed, whose footprint it was, what became of Friday, and why he came home a rich man. Most of what this book means is hidden in what he carried home with him.

In one sentence

A restless merchant's son from York, shipwrecked on a voyage to buy slaves in Africa, washes up alone on an island off the coast of South America and spends twenty-eight years turning wilderness into an estate, terror into faith, and the native man he rescues into a servant — then sails home with a parrot, an umbrella and a pile of tarnished coins to discover that his Brazilian plantation has been making him rich the whole time. It is a survival adventure, an account book, a confession and a colonial blueprint, and all four are true at once.

Coordinates

Daniel Defoe (c. 1660–1731) was a London tradesman's son and a Protestant Dissenter who dealt in hosiery and bricks, went bankrupt, wrote hundreds of political pamphlets, was pilloried in 1703 for a satire on High Church zealots, and later worked as an intelligence agent for the politician Robert Harley. He was pushing sixty when Robinson Crusoe appeared in 1719 — his first novel.

Its place in literary history is a hard one to argue with. It is often called the first English novel (the title is contested); more precisely, it was the first book to make an ordinary man's food, clothing, tools and inner bookkeeping worth several hundred pages. Defoe's name was nowhere on the title page, which said only that it was "Written by Himself." From the start, the book pretended not to be a novel.

The story

Act One · The middle station: a third son who would not listen

He was born in York in 1632. His father was a merchant from Bremen named Kreutznaer, a name the English mangled into "Crusoe." He was the third son: the eldest had been killed fighting the Spaniards near Dunkirk, the second had vanished, and his father wanted him to study law.

All he wanted was the sea. His father called him into his bedroom and delivered a long speech: the best place in the world is the "middle station" of life, spared the ambition and envy of the great and the labour of the poor, a life that slides gently through the world. When he came to the eldest son's death, the old man wept, and he ended with something close to a curse (in substance): Foreshadowing ①if you go, God will not bless you, and one day you will have plenty of leisure to reflect on having ignored my advice, at a time when there will be no one to help you.

He was moved for a few days. Then, on 1 September 1651, he slipped aboard a ship from Hull bound for London. The first storm frightened him into vowing to go home and repent; when the wind dropped, a bowl of punch washed the vow away. The second storm, off Yarmouth, actually sank the ship, and the owner warned him it was a sign from heaven. He did not go home. He went to London.

He sailed to Guinea with a friendly captain, traded trinkets for a few pounds of gold dust and cleared almost three hundred pounds. The first venture went beautifully, which is exactly where the trouble began. On the second, his ship was taken by a Moorish corsair out of Sallee, on the coast of North Africa, and he spent two years as the captain's slave.

His chance came on a fishing trip, with only a Moor and a boy called Xury on board. He tipped the Moor into the sea and, gun in hand, told him to swim for shore; the boy swore to follow him anywhere. The two of them crept south down the West African coast for days until a Portuguese ship picked them up. Its captain was astonishingly decent: he charged nothing for the passage and paid well for the boat. Foreshadowing ②He wanted Xury too, and offered sixty pieces of eight, promising to free the boy in ten years if he became a Christian. Crusoe hesitated, and then sold him — the boy who had fetched his water, kept watch for him and sworn to stay at his side.

In Brazil he bought land and planted tobacco and sugar, and within four years he had become exactly what his father had described: a man of the middle station. When the plantation ran short of hands, he regretted parting with Xury. Then his neighbours, who had heard him talk about the Guinea trade, made a proposal: they would fit out a ship to buy slaves in Africa on the quiet and divide them up; he would put in no money, only lead the voyage, and take a share.

He was already secure, and he said yes anyway. On 1 September 1659 — the same day of the year he had run away from home — the ship left Brazil. A dozen days out a hurricane struck; the ship ran aground off an unknown coast; the crew took to the boat, and a wave the size of a mountain turned it over. The sea threw him at the beach again and again and dragged him back, until he caught hold of a rock and crawled ashore.

No one else survived. All he ever found of them were three hats, a cap and two shoes that did not match.

Act Two · The wreck: carrying a world out of a dead ship

He spent the first night asleep in a tree. By morning the tide had lifted the ship closer to shore, where it sat jammed on the rocks, almost intact.

Over the next thirteen days he made eleven trips, building rafts from spars and planks and riding the tide back with everything he could move: bread, rice, cheese, rum, the carpenter's chest, guns, powder, canvas, rope, clothes, pens and ink, three Bibles, two cats. It is the first and longest list in the book, and everything that follows depends on it.

On one trip he found money in a drawer, worth about thirty-six pounds. He laughed at it and made a speech (in substance): Foreshadowing ③you useless stuff, what are you to me? You are not worth a knife. Stay where you are and go to the bottom. Then, the book says, he had second thoughts — and wrapped it up and took it with him. That night a gale blew, and in the morning the ship was gone.

He chose a level patch under a shaded rock face, drove two rows of sharpened stakes into a half circle, left no door and came and went by a ladder he pulled up after him. Inside he pitched a tent and dug into the rock. On the shore he set up a large post and cut into it "I came on shore here on the 30th of Sept. 1659," then notched it every day, with a longer notch every seventh day and a longer one still on the first of each month. He built himself a calendar, because a man who no longer knows what day it is is not far from madness.

Once settled, he did something only a merchant would think of. He drew up his situation as a ledger, "Evil" on the left, "Good" on the right, entry against entry. I am cast on a desolate island with no hope of rescue — but I am alive, and all the others drowned. I have no clothes — but the climate is hot, and I hardly need them. He did double-entry bookkeeping on his own misfortune, and found that the Good column came out slightly ahead.

Act Three · A Bible in the fever: how an island becomes an estate

He began a journal, backdated to his landing, and kept it until the ink ran out. In it he records what he took for a miracle: a dozen ears of green barley and a few stalks of rice sprouting by his wall. He wept, convinced God had planted grain for him on a desert island — until, a few days later, he remembered shaking out a bag of chicken feed from the ship on that very spot. He admits that once he understood, his gratitude shrank accordingly.

In the summer of 1660 he went down with an ague. On the worst night he dreamed of a man wrapped in flame descending from a black cloud with a spear, saying, in substance: since all these things have not brought you to repentance, now you shall die. He woke drenched in sweat and, for the first time, truly prayed. Payoff ① ↑Lying there, he thought of his father, and the old man's words had come true to the letter. When we first heard that speech, we took it, as the boy did, for an anxious father trying to scare his son; now the whole weight of the island comes down on that bedroom. The opening scene of an adventure novel turns out to be a sermon, and the sermon isn't finished until the hero lies sick on a desert island.

Following a Brazilian remedy, he steeped tobacco in rum and drank it, then opened the Bible and found a line: call upon me in the day of trouble, and I will deliver thee. He took it as written to him. From then on he read Scripture daily and kept the anniversary of his landing, 30 September, as a fast. Little by little, "deliverance" stopped meaning getting off the island and started meaning owning his sins.

Recovered, he explored. The far side of the island was much kinder than his own: a valley of wild grapes, melons, limes and sugar cane, with cool springs. He built a "country house" there. He caught a young parrot, called it Poll, Foreshadowing ④and spent years teaching it to talk; the first thing he taught it was his own name.

For years after that, the book is all labour: firing pots, growing barley, groping his way to a loaf of bread, penning goats, milking them and making cheese, sewing clothes out of goatskin and an umbrella that folds. He felled a huge cedar and spent months hollowing a canoe big enough for twenty-odd men, only to find, when it was done, that it lay about a hundred yards from the water and he could not move it an inch. He left it to rot in the woods and drew the lesson himself: there is no folly like beginning a work before you have counted the cost.

Later he built a smaller one, rigged a homemade sail and set out to go round the island. Off the eastern point a powerful current seized him and pulled him out to sea, the shore falling away. Only then did he realise how much he loved the island: it was home, and he was about to lose it forever. A shift in the wind saved him. He staggered ashore, walked to his country house and fell asleep.

In his sleep someone was calling his name: Robin, Robin Crusoe, poor Robin Crusoe, where are you? Where have you been? How came you here? He leapt up in terror — Payoff ④ ↑it was Poll, perched on the hedge. Not one of those words belonged to the bird. They were all his own, murmured to it on countless lonely evenings, and now handed back to him intact. Teaching a parrot to talk looked like a pastime, and we forgot it; but on the evening he nearly died in the current, the only voice in the world that called his name was his own echo. The scene is funny, and cold to the bone: in six years, no human being had called him anything.

Act Four · A footprint on the sand

Gradually he came to live like a country squire, and he describes one of his dinners: Foreshadowing ⑤"my Majesty," prince and lord of the whole island, with the lives of all his subjects at his absolute command; Poll, like a favourite courtier, the only one allowed to speak to him; the old dog at his right hand; the two cats on either side, waiting for scraps. A Stoic, he says, would have smiled to see it. He also sketches his own portrait: goatskin from head to foot, saw and hatchet at his belt, gun on his shoulder, a goatskin umbrella over his head.

Then comes the footprint we started with.

He hid behind his wall for three days. He wondered if it was the Devil; he even wondered if it was his own — so he worked up the courage to go back and set his foot in it, and his foot was clearly smaller. Fear moved into his life. He built a second wall outside the first and mounted seven guns in it; outside that he planted something like twenty thousand stakes of a wood that takes root, and within a few years they were a thicket; he split his goats into several hidden folds. For two whole years he slept badly. The thing he had longed for for fifteen years became, the moment it appeared, the thing he feared most.

Later, on the island's south-west side, he found the answer: skulls, hands and feet scattered on the sand around a burnt-out fire pit. Tribes from the mainland paddled over, killed and ate their prisoners here, and left. He was sick on the spot.

For months he climbed to his lookout every day, planning an ambush that would kill twenty or thirty of them at once. But the longer he watched, the more he asked himself: by what right? These people did not know it was a crime; it was their custom; they had never harmed him. If he slaughtered them, how was he different from the Spaniards whose massacres in the Americas he had always condemned? He resolved not to strike unless they struck first. It is the clearest-headed passage in the book, and the one he himself would not keep to.

Years later, on a stormy night, he heard guns at sea. The next morning a Spanish ship lay broken on the rocks to the south-east. He rowed out and found only a half-starved dog and two drowned men. He stood there saying it over and over: if only one had been saved — just one.

About a year and a half after that, he had a dream: savages land with a prisoner, the prisoner breaks free and runs into his grove, he hides the man, and the man becomes his servant. He woke wretched, because it was only a dream.

Act Five · Friday

Around his twenty-fifth year on the island, five canoes landed at once. Through his spyglass he watched some thirty people dancing round a fire; two prisoners were dragged out and one was struck down. The other suddenly broke loose and sprinted along the beach toward his side of the island with three men behind him. A creek lay in the way; the fugitive swam it, and only two of his pursuers could swim.

He realised the dream was coming true. He ran down by a short cut and put himself between the fugitive and the pursuers. He knocked the first one down with the butt of his gun; the second was drawing a bow, and he shot him dead. The fugitive, stunned by the gunshot, came toward him a step at a time, kneeling at every step, kissed the ground, and lifted Crusoe's foot and set it on his own head — a sign, Crusoe understood, that he was now his slave forever. The man who had been knocked down began to stir; the fugitive gestured for the sword at Crusoe's belt and cut off his head with one stroke.

It was a Friday, so Crusoe named the young man Friday. The first word he taught him was what to call his rescuer: Master. Then "yes" and "no."

Payoff ② ↑At this point it is hard not to look back at the boy sold for sixty pieces of eight in Act One and feel a chill. Nearly thirty years earlier, he had a dark-skinned boy at his side who had sworn loyalty to him; he sold him, then missed an extra pair of hands in Brazil, and to replace them he boarded the slave ship that left him on this island. Now, on the island, he has someone kneeling at his feet again, and this time he doesn't sell him: he names him and teaches him to say "Master." Defoe may not have meant the parallel, but that is how the book is built: every dark-skinned person Crusoe meets ends up in the same position.

Friday learned fast and was gentle by nature. Crusoe, horrified to find him eager to dig up the two corpses and eat them, worked hard to wean him onto goat meat and bread. He taught him the Bible and the Christian God, and Friday asked a question he could not answer: if God is so much stronger than the Devil, why doesn't He simply kill the Devil, so he does no more harm? Crusoe pretended not to hear and changed the subject.

These were the happiest years of his time on the island. Friday told him of a dozen or more shipwrecked Spaniards living on the mainland, and the two of them built a new canoe to go across.

Before they could leave, the cannibals came again, and among the prisoners was a white man. The two of them took every gun they had and charged. Among the rescued were a Spaniard and an old man tied up in the bottom of a canoe — Friday threw himself on him, laughing, crying and dancing: it was his father.

Crusoe recorded his thoughts: the island was now peopled, he had subjects, he was absolute lord and lawgiver; his three subjects held three religions — Friday a Protestant, his father a pagan, the Spaniard a Catholic — and he allowed liberty of conscience throughout his dominions. He writes it with a straight face. At this moment, a castaway has already begun to think of himself as a colonial governor.

Act Six · The Governor goes home

The Spaniard and Friday's father set off for the mainland to fetch the others, after Crusoe made them swear to obey him on their return. A few days later an English ship dropped anchor offshore. A boat came in with three bound men: the crew had mutinied and meant to maroon their captain, his mate and a passenger on this desert island.

Crusoe, clad in goatskin, presented himself to the captain and named two conditions: while on the island, everyone obeyed him; once the ship was retaken, he and Friday would be carried to England free. They lured the mutineers into getting lost on the island and overpowered them in batches. Crusoe stayed out of sight and had the captain refer to him, when addressing the prisoners, as "the Governor," so that they believed the island had a governor and a garrison. Payoff ⑤ ↑At that dinner in Act Four, "Majesty" and "subjects" were a lonely man's joke for a parrot and two cats; here the joke becomes real. A governor who doesn't exist, commanding an army that doesn't exist, brings a boatload of living men to their knees. He had crowned himself twenty years earlier, and now for the first time someone took him seriously — and only after laughing do we realise that the dinner was never just a joke.

Once the ship was theirs, the ringleader was shot by the captain, and the worst of the rest chose to stay on the island rather than hang in England. Crusoe handed over his cave, tools and goats, and told them to treat the Spaniards well when they arrived.

On 19 December 1686 he left the island. By his own reckoning he had been there twenty-eight years, two months and nineteen days. He took his goatskin cap, his umbrella and his parrot, Payoff ③ ↑and he did not forget the money — which had lain by him so long, useless, that it had grown rusty and tarnished. For more than twenty years it really had been worthless; the moment he set foot on a ship, it was money again. That "second thoughts" read like a jibe when we first met it. Now we can see what it was: the one thing about this man that twenty-eight years did not change.

He reached England in June 1687, thirty-five years after leaving home. His parents were long dead. The real ending comes in Lisbon, where the old Portuguese captain produced the accounts: the Brazilian plantation had been managed in his absence all along, and when everything was settled, he was a man with more than a thousand pounds a year. He shared it out with everyone who had once helped him.

Afraid of the sea, he went home overland with Friday, and in the Pyrenees they met deep snow and a pack of some three hundred starving wolves; Friday also climbed a tree to tease a bear, had everyone in fits, and then shot it dead. Back in England, Crusoe sold his Brazilian estate, married, had three children and lost his wife — all in a single sentence. At the end he is at sea again, off to visit "my new colony," and promises more adventures. In the sequel, published the same year, Friday sails with him once more and is killed by a shower of arrows in a skirmish at sea.

Pulling the causes into a single fuse: it wasn't the hurricane that ruined him, or the Moorish corsairs. It was the moment on the Brazilian plantation when he was already secure and said yes anyway. On the island he calls it his original sin: not being content with the station God and nature had given him. But the book's slyest move is that, while preaching against this sin, it lets the very plantation he abandoned keep making money for him through all twenty-eight years of his absence. A man is exiled for restlessness, then rewarded for having turned restlessness into industry in exile; the island is a punishment and also compound interest.

Three slow-motion scenes

I · "You useless stuff": a man talking to a pile of money

Replay it: a man who has just lost all his companions, with no idea how long he will be here, in a ship that the next wave might smash, opens a drawer full of money. He laughs, curses it, says it isn't worth a knife — and then, in half a sentence, on second thoughts, takes it.

The power lies in a mismatch of rhythm: first a ringing, eloquent soliloquy, then a dry little action with no explanation and no excuse. Word and deed simply stand side by side. We laugh, and then notice that this is us: we all say money can't buy happiness, and then we put it in our wallets. That half-sentence of second thoughts is more human than the whole speech. And its echo doesn't die until the tarnished coins in Act Six.

II · Only one footprint

Why only one? A person walking across sand leaves a trail; Defoe gives us a single print — which makes almost no physical sense, and Crusoe himself can't make sense of it. But precisely because there is only one, it reads not as evidence that someone passed by, but as a sign, a question dropped out of the sky: where did they come from? Where did they go? Was it even a person?

Look, too, at how information is released. From this moment the narration sees only through a frightened man's eyes, and we know as little as he does; the answer — the cannibals — doesn't arrive for pages, and in between come two years of fear. Fifteen years of loneliness are cashed in, all at once, as terror — and only then do you realise that in all those years he had been safe, so safe that loneliness was the only enemy he had.

III · A foot set on a head

For Friday's rescue Defoe gives us a precise gesture: kneel, kiss the ground, lift the other man's foot onto your own head. The point of view is entirely Crusoe's. Friday cannot say a word his rescuer understands; all we get is Crusoe's reading: he is swearing to be my slave.

Its power comes precisely from that one-sidedness. Defoe writes it smoothly, as if a life naturally bought a lifetime of obedience. But turn the camera toward Friday and the same gesture could be terror, a plea for mercy, a courtesy of his people — and Crusoe has translated it into a contract. Few scenes in the history of the novel have become, because the author never thought to change the angle, the scene that later writers would rewrite again and again.

The book's core devices

One: pretending to be true. The preface's "editor" vouches that this is a just history of fact, with no appearance of fiction. The book is a first-person memoir with a journal inserted in the middle that retells events already narrated, doesn't always agree with the earlier version, and breaks off as the ink runs low. These clumsy repetitions and gaps are Defoe's method: real people's writing is long-winded, repetitive and full of miscounts. In The Rise of the Novel (1957), the critic Ian Watt called this "formal realism": specific dates, numbers and objects that persuade the reader this is a real man's life. The cost is that it sometimes reads like a ledger, with pages on how to fire a pot.

Two: two sets of books, laid on top of each other. One is the account of things: lists, stores, money. The other is the account of the soul: sin, repentance, providence — the Puritan tradition of spiritual autobiography, which reads every event of a life as a sign from God. The book's genius is that both accounts use the same grammar: the Evil/Good table is at once a merchant's balance sheet and a believer's examination of conscience. We can never be sure whether he is a sinner the island has ground into a saint, or a merchant who has simply entered God into the books. Defoe doesn't decide for us.

What it forces you to think about

One · The cannibals' beach: does he have the right to shoot first?

Every few years, near where you live, a group of people arrives, kills its prisoners, eats them and leaves. They have never noticed you and never harmed you. You have guns and the high ground, and you could ambush them and kill a great many at once. Should you?

First, the strongest case for yes. At intervals, people are being killed and eaten alive on that beach. That is not "cultural difference"; it is murder, and the victims are people with no way to fight back. If you can stop it and you stand aside, your non-interference is courtesy to the killers and abandonment of their victims. The two times Crusoe did act, he saved three living people. If "respecting their customs" means watching the next Friday get eaten, that respect is just an elegant excuse.

The other side is the argument Crusoe himself works out, and he thinks it through deeply: what right do I have to judge them? They don't know it is a crime, and they have done nothing to me. If I may slaughter people because they do something I find abominable, then the Spaniards had the same licence when they massacred the natives of the Americas — they too thought those people's rites were abominable. Once "I find it repugnant, so I may kill" is accepted, the first to benefit will always be whoever has more guns.

Where does the author stand? Defoe has Crusoe choose, in principle, not to attack, yet charge down the moment a prisoner is about to die — and he frames it as Providence fulfilled. He seems to say: no conquest on principle, but no standing by while someone is killed either. Except that by the end of the book, this middle path has led straight to "my new colony." Bring it home: a colleague, a neighbour, a relative does something you find abhorrent that doesn't touch you. Can you be sure when stepping in is right, and when you only want to prove that you are?

Two · A life for a lifetime: can saving someone buy them?

You save someone's life, and he kneels on the spot to show he is yours from now on. You feed him, teach him your language, protect him. He calls you "Master"; you call him by the name you gave him. Is that a fair relationship?

First, the strongest case that there's nothing wrong with it. Putting Crusoe's foot on his head was Friday's own gesture. Without Crusoe he would already have been eaten; with him he gains shelter, knowledge and a companion who genuinely cares for him. The affection between them is real: when Crusoe suggests he go home, Friday begs to be killed rather than sent away. A man in extremity trades loyalty for survival and thinks it a good bargain — who are we to tell him it wasn't? Inequality is not the same as oppression.

The other side: a promise made under the knife is not consent; it is fear. A life saved can earn gratitude, not ownership, and certainly not the surrender of one's own name. Crusoe never asks what Friday was called before; he makes the Spaniards swear obedience, makes the mutineers surrender — he applies the same logic to everyone who falls into his hands: I saved you, so you are mine. Friday's tears don't prove the arrangement fair. They prove only that people can come to love their dependence.

Where does the author stand? Defoe clearly thinks this is a beautiful relationship; his pages on Friday are full of fondness, and full of condescension. Three hundred years on, it is hard not to see the half he didn't. Bring it home: the company that sponsored your visa, the elder who got you through a hard time, the boss who championed you — at what point does a debt of gratitude turn into an invisible rope?

Three · Was his father right: the safe middle, or the sea?

Your father begs you: don't chase trouble, hold the middle and live a quiet life. You ignore him, survive shipwreck, slavery and twenty-eight years on an island, and come home richer than he ever was and a far deeper person. So — was your father right?

First, the strongest case that he was. Crusoe admits it in the end, and the admission cost him twenty-eight years. His companions drowned; the best years of his life went on talking to a parrot; and his eventual fortune came precisely from the "middle station" he abandoned — the Brazilian plantation made his money, not the island. The island gave him nothing; what made him rich was the security he hadn't managed to destroy on his way out. Treating a survivor's happy ending as proof that the adventure was worth it means listening only to the people who came back.

The other side: had he obeyed, he would have been an unremarkable lawyer in York. He would never have learned he could survive in extremity, would never have truly knelt to pray. His father's advice contained wisdom, and also something frightening: it made "not suffering" the highest goal of a life. The man reborn in fever, who suddenly loved his island while the current dragged him away from it, is not someone a safe life in York could have produced.

Where does the author stand? On the page, with the father: Crusoe confesses again and again. But the plot sides with the son: it makes the disobedient boy rich and sends him back to sea at the end. Defoe himself went bankrupt, was jailed and rebuilt his fortunes, and never spent a day in the middle station. Bring it home: a steady job, and a place you can't explain why you must go to — when your parents gave you their advice, which half did you hear?

Motifs: what Defoe keeps writing about

First, accounts. The heroine of Moll Flanders (1722) spends her life tallying her goods, her market value and her lovers; Roxana (1724) writes marriage up as a transaction. Whatever happens to Defoe's people, their first impulse is to make a list. Second, survival in extremity, and Providence. A Journal of the Plague Year (1722), narrated by a London saddler, records the weekly bills of mortality of the Great Plague of 1665 and his anxious questioning of God about whether to flee or stay. Third, the ordinary person scrambling for themselves on the margins: thief, pirate (Captain Singleton, 1720), courtesan, castaway, each telling a "true story" in the first person, each repenting at the end, none of them any poorer for it.

Robinson Crusoe is the purest link in this chain of obsessions: take away society entirely, leave one man, a heap of tools and God, and watch a modern person rebuild the books from zero.

Controversy and criticism

Colonialism and slavery: there's no getting around it. Crusoe is shipwrecked on a slaving voyage, and the book never reflects on the trade itself; he sells Xury, teaches Friday to say "Master," and finally parcels out the island as a colony. In a 1912 lecture in Trieste, James Joyce said, in effect, that the true symbol of the British conquest is Robinson Crusoe. Later writers simply rewrote the book: Michel Tournier's Friday, or, The Other Island (1967) has Friday lead Crusoe toward another way of living; in J. M. Coetzee's Foe (1986), Friday has no tongue and no one can ever know his story; Derek Walcott's play Pantomime (1978) puts master and servant on stage.

Racial description: describing Friday, the book makes a point of saying his nose was not flat like that of Black Africans and his lips were not thick — by today's lights, plain racial hierarchy. In the sequel, The Farther Adventures, Crusoe travels through China and is scathing about the Chinese, a passage rarely included in Chinese selections.

Slips: Charles Gildon published a pamphlet mocking Defoe the same year. The most cited slip: Crusoe strips to swim out to the wreck, then fills his pockets with biscuits once aboard.

Where are the women? There are almost none; his marriage, wife and children get one sentence.

Sources and adaptations: the Scottish sailor Alexander Selkirk lived alone on the Juan Fernández Islands off Chile from 1704 to 1709 and is often named as the model, but Defoe never said so, and his island lies in the Caribbean near the mouth of the Orinoco. In 1966 Chile renamed Selkirk's island Robinson Crusoe Island. Many readers know Crusoe from children's abridgments, which tend to cut the religious soliloquies and the slaving voyage. The cheerful, capable Crusoe of childhood memory is often the edited one. In Chinese, one of the earliest translations was by Lin Shu and Zeng Zonggong in 1905, which is why two different transliterations of his name are still in use.

  • Sociology · Weber, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism: Weber argued that Puritans treated methodical work and self-scrutiny as evidence of grace, and that this fed the spirit of capitalism; he even sets Crusoe against Bunyan's pilgrim. The Evil/Good ledger is that spirit on paper. The difference: Weber describes a gnawing anxiety, while Crusoe's books mostly balance with a clear conscience.
  • Psychology · loneliness and anthropomorphism: Epley and colleagues (2008) found that lonelier people more readily attribute minds to pets and gadgets — the very shape of that dinner with its "favourite courtier." The difference: Crusoe knows the parrot only repeats him.
  • Psychology · perceived control: Langer and Rodin (1976) let nursing-home residents tend a plant and make small choices; they were happier, and a follow-up found lower mortality. The notched post and the goat pens are the same self-rescue. The difference: no one arranged them for him.
  • Anthropology · Mauss, The Gift: Mauss showed that gifts create obligations, and a gift that can't be repaid creates rank. A life can't be repaid, so Friday repays it with himself. The difference: Mauss's gifts circulate between groups; this one flows only one way.

Ten lines to take with you

① His father's harshest prophecy takes twenty-eight years and a sickbed to finish.

② He first ruined himself right after he first had a truly good life.

③ The inventory of everything he hauled off the wreck is the book's first poem.

④ The speech calling money useless is beautiful; the half-sentence of second thoughts is more honest.

⑤ He drew up a double-entry ledger of his misfortune and found the Good column slightly ahead.

⑥ For his first twenty-odd years, the only voice to call his name was one he had taught a parrot.

⑦ The company he longed for for fifteen years arrived as a single footprint, and he spent two years building walls.

⑧ Asking by what right he could judge the cannibals is the book's most lucid moment, and the one he later abandoned.

⑨ The first word he taught Friday was "Master"; he never asked his real name.

⑩ The money that rusted on the island was money again the moment he boarded ship — the island changed him in many ways, but not in that one.
