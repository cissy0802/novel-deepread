Novels, Deep-Read · Luo Guanzhong · No.33

Romance of the Three Kingdoms

Sanguo yanyi · Luo Guanzhong · c. 14th century

中文 →

A recruiting notice goes up on a street in Zhuo County, and a man of twenty-eight reads it to the end and sighs.
A voice explodes behind him: a real man doesn't serve his country, he stands here sighing?
He turns and finds a butcher with round staring eyes and a beard like a wire brush.
The two go drinking at the tavern by the village gate and fall in with a third man pushing a handcart, face red as a date, a fugitive who killed a local bully five or six years ago and has been on the run ever since.
The next day the three of them lay out a black ox and a white horse in a garden full of peach blossom, kneel, and swear to heaven: we were not born on the same day, but we mean to die on it.

This page contains complete spoilers—including whether Guan Yu really lets Cao Cao go on the Huarong Road, how Jing Province is lost, whose hands Zhang Fei dies in, how Liu Bei's war of revenge ends, which windswept plain Zhuge Liang's northern campaigns stop on, and who holds the empire after a hundred and twenty chapters. Everything worth discussing here lies past the ending: if you don't know how the oath sworn in that peach garden ends up burning down three men and their kingdom with them, all you have is a very long book about battles. If you want the suspense, read the novel first.

In One Sentence

The Han empire collapses; three strangers swear brotherhood in a peach garden and vow to die together, then spend their lives contesting the wreckage with a warlord who says openly that he would rather wrong the world than be wronged by it, and with a family entrenched south of the river; fire at Red Cliffs burns the map into three pieces; then Guan Yu, repaying an old kindness, lets the enemy who should have died escape, loses Jing Province to his own arrogance and is executed at Maicheng; Zhang Fei, half-mad with grief, flogs his own men and is beheaded in his tent by two of them; Liu Bei throws the whole kingdom into a war of revenge and loses it in one night of fire, dying at the White Emperor City; which leaves Zhuge Liang holding one province against an empire, marching north six times, and dying in the autumn wind on the Wuzhang Plains—after which Jiang Wei holds on for thirty years, Deng Ai comes over the mountains at Yinping, and the last emperor of Shu surrenders and remarks that he is quite happy here and doesn't think about home; in the end all three kingdoms fall to the Sima family's Jin, closing the loop on the book's own first line about union and division.

Coordinates

Luo Guanzhong (c. 1330–1400) lived at the turn from Yuan to Ming. His birthplace is disputed, and the biographical record is almost empty—little more than one vague note by a contemporary, Jia Zhongming, who claims to have met him. This is a rare situation in literary history: the book that shaped six centuries of a nation's historical intuition comes to us from an author we know virtually nothing about.

Nor did one man invent it. The skeleton is Chen Shou's official history Records of the Three Kingdoms (3rd century) with Pei Songzhi's 5th-century commentary. The flesh comes from centuries of storytelling—the poet Su Shi records, in the eleventh century, children weeping at the tale-teller's stall when Liu Bei loses and cheering when Cao Cao does. By the Yuan there were already popular prose versions and a large body of Three Kingdoms drama. Luo Guanzhong's work looks less like invention than like general editorship: fusing history, storyteller's patter and stage business into a single long narrative with one causal spine and one moral compass.

What most people read today is not even quite his text: the standard 120-chapter version is the seventeenth-century recension of Mao Lun and his son Mao Zonggang, who rewrote the chapter titles, swapped out poems, and tightened the pro-Liu, anti-Cao slant another turn. Even the famous lyric that opens the book—the great river rolling east, heroes washed away like foam—is not Luo's; it is a poem by the Ming writer Yang Shen, moved to the front by the Maos. Its slot in literary history: the first full-length Chinese historical novel in chapters, and the only novel anywhere that has turned a nation's political instincts into common property.

The Story

Act One · Peach Garden and Luoyang: from a private oath to a hollow empire

In the last years of Emperor Ling, the Zhang brothers raise a religious rebellion; hundreds of thousands of peasants tie yellow scarves round their heads. The court has no army worth the name and posts notices calling for volunteers. One such notice, in Zhuo County, pins three men together: Liu Bei, who weaves straw sandals and claims descent from a Han prince three centuries back—a pedigree nobody can check; Zhang Fei, a butcher and wine-seller with land, money and a temper; and Guan Yu, six years a fugitive for killing a man who was abusing his power. The oath they swear in the peach garden is not about loyalty to the throne, and not about saving the country. It is private: we could not be born on the same day, but let us die on it. Setup ①

They serve well against the rebellion and are rewarded with a county post so small it comes with a superior officer looking for a bribe. Zhang Fei ties the man to a tree and beats him; Liu Bei hangs up his seal and walks away. The men who do the work cannot get the posts, and the men with the posts are collecting money—that is the book's first verdict on this dynasty. With the rebellion crushed, the real disorder begins. Dong Zhuo marches his frontier army into Luoyang, deposes one emperor, installs a boy, and lets his troops loot the capital. Cao Cao—grandson of a eunuch's adopted son, tagged in his youth with the judgment that he would be a capable minister in a peaceful age and a treacherous hero in a chaotic one—goes to assassinate him, fails, and runs. On the road he takes shelter with an old family friend, hears knives being sharpened in the back yard, assumes a trap, and kills the entire household before discovering they were preparing a pig in his honour; then he kills his host too, coming back with the wine. His companion Chen Gong asks how he can do such a thing, and he says the sentence he has been carrying for eighteen centuries:

"Better that I wrong the world than that the world wrong me."—Romance of the Three Kingdoms, ch. 4, my rendering

This is not an excuse; it is a principle announced out loud—Cao Cao is the only man in the book willing to state his own operating logic in full. Chen Gong leaves him that night. Years later Chen Gong is captured at the White Gate Tower, and Cao Cao weeps and begs him to submit, and he will not so much as turn his head. Saying that sentence bought him one life; the bill, when it came, cost him a man who could have run his campaigns for thirty years.

An alliance of warlords marches on Dong Zhuo, contributing the book's two earliest set pieces: Guan Yu killing an enemy champion and returning before the wine poured for him has gone cold, and the three brothers together closing on Lü Bu at Hulao Pass—the finest fighter alive, and the first man in the novel to make a career of changing fathers. Dong Zhuo withdraws to Chang'an, and a minister named Wang Yun runs the oldest trick in the book on him: promise the singing girl Diaochan to Lü Bu, then present her to Dong Zhuo, and let adoptive father and adoptive son destroy each other over a woman. Lü Bu kills him in open court. The Han is not rescued by any loyalist army; it is finished off, almost absent-mindedly, by a domestic quarrel. After that there is no referee left. The boy emperor flees east, going hungry on the road, and none of the warlords wants to be stuck with him—only Cao Cao sends troops to collect him and installs him at Xu. From that moment Cao Cao holds something nobody else has: legitimacy. Every order he gives can be an imperial edict, and everyone who opposes him is automatically a traitor.

Act Two · Xu: a jar of plum wine, and a horse that changes hands

Lü Bu seizes Xu Province; Liu Bei, with nowhere to go, attaches himself to Cao Cao. Cao Cao floods Lü Bu out of Xiapi, and at the White Gate Tower the great warrior, roped and kneeling, makes his pitch: give me your cavalry, keep the infantry yourself, and the empire is easy. Cao Cao is visibly tempted and glances at Liu Bei. Liu Bei says one sentence: have you forgotten how his last two adoptive fathers died? Lü Bu is strangled. Liu Bei kills him without touching a weapon, simply by reminding Cao Cao that this man's loyalty comes with updates.

Then comes the quietest and most dangerous scene in the novel. Liu Bei is lying low in the capital, growing vegetables in his back garden. Cao Cao invites him to drink: green plums, warmed wine, a cloud overhead shaped like a dragon. Cao Cao asks him to name the heroes of the age; Liu Bei offers candidate after candidate and Cao Cao knocks each one down, then points at his guest and at himself:

"The heroes of this age are you, sir, and myself. No one else."—Romance of the Three Kingdoms, ch. 21, my rendering

Liu Bei's chopsticks hit the floor. A thunderclap arrives on cue; he bends to pick them up and says the thunder frightened him, and Cao Cao laughs at a man scared of weather. That bow saves his life—but the truly hair-raising thing is not Liu Bei's performance, it is that Cao Cao says the quiet part out loud and then lets him go anyway. Soon Liu Bei finds a pretext to take troops out of the capital and never returns. It is the worst mistake of Cao Cao's life, and the form the mistake takes is wanting too badly to let his rival know he had seen through him.

Cao Cao then attacks Xu Province. Liu Bei flees to Yuan Shao; Guan Yu, protecting his brother's two wives, is cornered on a hill. Zhang Liao comes to negotiate and Guan Yu names three conditions: he surrenders to the Han and not to Cao Cao; the ladies are to be maintained properly; and the day he learns where Liu Bei is, he leaves. Cao Cao agrees to all of it and then treats him magnificently—banquets every few days, gold, silk, women, and finally Lü Bu's own warhorse, the Red Hare. Setup ② Guan Yu, who has thanked him for nothing so far, bows for the horse—because it covers a thousand li a day and he can reach his brother faster on it. Cao Cao's face changes on the spot. He bought a great many men in his life; only this one converted a gift, to his face, into escape velocity. Not long after, Guan Yu hangs up his seal, leaves the gold untouched, and rides north through the passes, cutting down the officers who try to stop him. Cao Cao's staff want to pursue. He orders the road opened. The man with "better that I wrong the world" written across his forehead here does the least profitable thing in the entire book: knowing he cannot keep this one, he insists on sending him off beautifully. That is not softness. He is buying something—a debt—and the debt comes due on a muddy road after Red Cliffs.

Then Guandu. Outnumbered and out-supplied, Cao Cao burns Yuan Shao's grain depot on intelligence brought by a defector, and the north is his. The battle is narrated with startling restraint, almost entirely as decisions: who took advice and who didn't. Yuan Shao has good advisers too; his problem is that he listens to the wrong one every single time.

Act Three · Longzhong and Red Cliffs: a plan, and a fire that burns for three days

Liu Bei takes refuge with the governor of Jing Province and sits out his forties at Xinye with no land of his own. At a banquet an enemy moves to kill him and he bolts on a horse called Dilu, a creek ahead of him and pursuit behind; he shouts at the animal and it clears the water in a single impossible leap. He had been told plainly, and more than once, that this horse—white blaze running down into the mouth, tear-channels beneath the eyes—brings death to the man who rides it. Setup ③ He kept it anyway. On the far bank he meets the recluse Sima Hui, who says two things. One sounds at the time like good news: there are two men in this district, Sleeping Dragon and Fledgling Phoenix, and either one of them could settle the empire for you. Setup ④ The other he says to the sky after Liu Bei has ridden off: the Sleeping Dragon has found his lord, but not his hour. Setup ⑤ The largest conclusion in the book is delivered a third of the way through by a man who then walks out of it.

Next comes Xu Shu, who wins Liu Bei several sharp little victories until Cao Cao forges a letter from his mother and draws him to the capital. Riding away, Xu Shu wheels his horse around and comes back to recommend a twenty-seven-year-old at Longzhong. Liu Bei goes three times. Twice he finds nobody home, and on the road every farmer, friend and father-in-law he meets sounds like a sage, stacking the expectation higher; the third time he is admitted and still has to stand outside while the young man finishes his nap. Then the Longzhong Plan: Zhuge Liang produces a map and takes the empire apart for him—Cao Cao holds the north, the numbers and the emperor, and cannot be met head-on; Sun Quan is three generations deep in the south behind good terrain and a loyal population, and can only be allied with; the one gap is Jing Province and Yi Province—take Jing as a base, take the west as a foundation, hold a third of the empire; then, when the situation shifts, strike north on two roads at once. Setup ⑥ After twenty years of drifting, Liu Bei has a piece of paper with steps on it.

The paper is barely warm before Cao Cao comes south. The governor dies, his younger son surrenders, and Liu Bei retreats with a hundred thousand civilians in tow, covering a few miles a day, until he is caught at Changban and his family is lost in the rout. Zhao Yun rides back alone into the wreck and finds Lady Mi with the infant heir; she has a spear wound in her leg, hands him the child and drops down a well. Liu Bei takes the baby and throws it on the ground: for the sake of this brat I nearly lost a great general. It is the most famous piece of political theatre in the book and the hardest to grade—the novel refuses to tell you what he is thinking, and shows you only the throw and Zhao Yun's tears.

Zhuge Liang crosses the river to Chaisang, where the southern court is arguing. The surrender party's case is frighteningly good: Cao Cao holds the emperor, so fighting him is treason, and he now has a fleet, so the river no longer belongs to us alone. Zhuge Liang out-argues the assembled scholars, then works on the commander Zhou Yu privately by quoting—with one word altered—a poem about the tower Cao Cao is building, so that it seems to promise him the two most beautiful women in the south, one of whom is Zhou Yu's wife. Zhou Yu comes out of his chair. What commits a state to war is not an argument about the national interest; it is one personal insult.

Red Cliffs is then the technical summit of the novel, a chain of interlocking frauds: an old schoolfriend rows over to talk Zhou Yu into surrendering, is deliberately made drunk, steals a planted letter, and goes home to make Cao Cao execute the only two officers he has who understand river warfare; the veteran Huang Gai is flogged raw in front of the assembled staff for insubordination and then defects in convincing fury; an adviser recommends chaining the northern ships together against seasickness—welding an entire fleet into a single target. One thing is missing: no southeast wind blows in winter. Zhuge Liang builds an altar, performs for three days and three nights, and the wind comes. The fire goes up; the chained ships cannot scatter. The largest victory in the book is won by no weapon at all, but by replacing, one at a time, everything the enemy is using to make judgments.

The retreat runs along the Huarong Road. Cao Cao laughs out loud three times, each time at how badly his enemies have used the ground—if there were an ambush here we would all be finished. After the first laugh, Zhao Yun comes out of the trees. After the second, Zhang Fei. After the third, Guan Yu is standing in the road with his blade. Cao Cao says only: general, I trust you have been well? And then begins to itemise—the banquets, the horse, the passes opened for him. Guan Yu pulls his horse's head aside and lets the man through. Payoff ② ↑ What makes this beautiful is that it was paid for in advance, dozens of chapters earlier, in instalments. What makes it cold is that Zhuge Liang knew he would do it and sent him anyway—having first made him sign for the assignment with his life. The Huarong Road is not Guan Yu's moral failure; it is Zhuge Liang's calculation performed on Guan Yu's morals, and the result the calculation needs is Cao Cao alive. Three kingdoms require three men. The most honourable scene in the book has the coldest piece of staff work underneath it.

Act Four · Three kingdoms: a province on loan, and a place called Fallen Phoenix

Victory over, the allies immediately start grabbing. While Zhou Yu grinds against Cao Ren for Nan Commandery, Zhuge Liang walks into the city ahead of both and takes the surrounding territory too, describing the arrangement as a loan. Envoys come to collect and go home with fresh IOUs. Sun Quan offers his sister in marriage as a way of getting a hostage; the wedding is meant to end in an ambush and instead ends with the south losing both the bride and a battle. Outmanoeuvred three times running, Zhou Yu bursts an old arrow wound and dies crying at the sky:

"Since Heaven made Yu, why did it make Liang as well?"—Romance of the Three Kingdoms, Zhou Yu's last words, my rendering

This is the novel's invention, not the historical man—more on that below. As fiction, though, it works: it turns intelligence into a competitive sport that can kill you, and from here on the thing Chinese readers find most addictive about this book is watching two clever men fence at a distance.

Then the western campaign. The weak governor of Yi Province, menaced by a neighbour, actually invites Liu Bei in to help defend it, and an official smuggles out the provincial maps. Liu Bei brings Pang Tong—the Fledgling Phoenix of the prophecy. On the march Pang Tong's horse panics and Liu Bei gives him his own mount: Dilu. They come to a narrow defile with dense trees on both sides, and Pang Tong, uneasy, asks what the place is called. Fallen Phoenix Slope. He says aloud that his own name is Fledgling Phoenix and this is no place for him—and the arrows come down off the ridge, all of them aimed at the conspicuous white horse and the man on it. Payoff ③ ↑Payoff ④ ↑ The horse that kills its rider finally kills one, and it is not its owner but the man who borrowed it. Two lines land in the same instant: the old warning about the animal, and the judgment that either one of these two men could settle the empire—which means that with both of them present, one has to go. Handing over that horse is the kindest thing Liu Bei does in the novel, and the most expensive.

With Pang Tong dead, Zhuge Liang has to go west himself, and Jing Province is left to Guan Yu alone. Liu Bei takes the western capital and then Hanzhong—the veteran Huang Zhong kills Cao Cao's commander at Dingjun Mountain, and Cao Cao comes in person, achieves nothing, mutters about chicken ribs and withdraws, executing on the way an officer clever enough to have guessed what he meant. In 219 Liu Bei declares himself King of Hanzhong. This is the summit of his career and the one moment when both legs of the Longzhong Plan are standing: Jing in hand, Yi in hand. It lasts a few months.

Guan Yu marches north from Jing against Fan Castle, floods an entire relieving army, captures one general and beheads another, and shakes the empire so badly that Cao Cao discusses moving the capital. A poisoned arrow has been through his arm; the surgeon opens it and scrapes the bone while he goes on playing chess. It is the last crown the book gives him. And in the very same passage, without emphasis, three fatal signals are set out: Sun Quan proposes a marriage alliance and Guan Yu insults his son as a dog's whelp; two colleagues responsible for supply are threatened in front of witnesses; and the rear is left with no one competent watching it.

Act Five · Collapse: Jing Province, Maicheng, the White Emperor City

The south changes commanders. Lü Meng reports himself ill and hands over to an unknown young scholar, Lu Xun, whose first act is a letter to Guan Yu so humble it is nearly obscene—and Guan Yu duly strips his garrisons for the northern front. Then the crossing in white: warships disguised as merchantmen, soldiers dressed as traders, the signal beacons along the river taken out in one night, and the provincial capital captured without a fight. Jing Province is not defeated, it is lifted from behind, by the ally. Payoff ⑥ ↑ One leg of the Longzhong Plan is now amputated: there will never again be a second road north, and every campaign Zhuge Liang fights for the rest of his life is pushed uphill on a plan that is missing half of itself. That is the real cost of losing Jing—not some counties, but a strategy that can no longer be true.

Guan Yu is caught between two armies and loses his own men first: the occupiers treat the soldiers' families conspicuously well, letters arrive at the front, and the army melts. He falls back to Maicheng, is ambushed breaking out, is taken with his adopted son, refuses to submit, and is executed. The Red Hare is awarded to the officer who captured him and starves itself to death.

The news reaches the west and Liu Bei collapses repeatedly. Zhang Fei, at his garrison, weeps day and night, drinks, flogs his soldiers, and orders white mourning gear for the entire army within three days on pain of execution. Two of his own officers cut off his head in the small hours and carry it to the enemy. Payoff ① ↑ The oath about dying on the same day now starts coming true, and the manner of it is the exact opposite of what the words meant: they do not die together, they are pulled down one by one by each other's deaths. What makes it exact is that nothing is left to coincidence—Guan Yu dies of pride, Zhang Fei of the haste of revenge, and Liu Bei is about to die of the obsession with it. Every link is the direct product of that oath.

In the north a dynasty ends too. Cao Cao's headaches worsen and he dies at Luoyang, spending his last instructions on dividing up the household incense and telling his women to learn shoemaking so they can support themselves—the man who spent a lifetime on "better that I wrong the world" closes on domestic admin. His son forces the last Han emperor to abdicate; four centuries of Han are over. Liu Bei promptly declares himself emperor in the west, keeping the dynastic name.

Then, against the flat opposition of Zhuge Liang and Zhao Yun, he throws everything at the south. Zhao Yun's objection is the blunt one: the usurper is Cao, not Sun; destroy the north and the south submits by itself. Liu Bei does not listen. Patience was the one skill he had all his life, and on this single occasion he chooses to be a brother rather than an emperor. The army goes downriver and camps for seventy miles through wooded hills to escape the summer heat. Lu Xun refuses battle for half a year, waits for the wind, and burns the entire line in a night. Liu Bei escapes to the White Emperor City, sickens, and entrusts his son to Zhuge Liang with a sentence that has been argued over for a thousand years:

"If my heir is worth supporting, support him. If he is not, take the throne yourself."—Romance of the Three Kingdoms, ch. 85, my rendering

Zhuge Liang breaks into a sweat, kneels, and knocks his forehead on the floor until it bleeds. Sincere trust, or one last test? The novel declines to say. It shows you the blood.

Act Six · Wuzhang Plains: one man against an era

Shu is one province, the weakest of the three. Zhuge Liang secures the south first, campaigning against the chieftain Meng Huo, capturing him seven times and releasing him seven times until the man talks himself out of rebelling. Then comes the memorial on taking the army north—the letter in which he sets down, item by item, the three visits to his hut and the deathbed charge, addressed to a boy emperor everyone knows is hopeless.

Six campaigns, and every one of them stops on the same thing. The first is the best positioned, and he gives a critical pass to Ma Su—brilliant in theory, and specifically named by the late emperor as a man not to be given real responsibility. Ma Su camps on a hill, is cut off from water, and is destroyed. Covering the retreat, Zhuge Liang stages the empty city: no garrison, Sima Yi's army approaching, the gates standing open, a few old soldiers sweeping the road, and himself on the wall burning incense and playing the zither. Sima Yi listens, becomes steadily more frightened, and withdraws. What makes the bluff work is not the music but a fifteen-year record of never once taking a risk—he stakes a lifetime of caution on a single hand. Back in camp he weeps and executes Ma Su, and demotes himself three ranks.

The later campaigns win tactically and lose to supply lines and to the court: grain arrives late, so he designs mechanical transport; the emperor listens to a slander and recalls him, and one scroll of paper cancels half a year of gains. The closest he comes is at Shangfang Valley, where he lures Sima Yi and his sons into a gourd-shaped ravine, seals both ends, and sets it alight—and then it rains, hard, and the fire goes out, and they ride away. Zhuge Liang sighs that a man may plan a thing but heaven decides it, and it cannot be forced. Payoff ⑤ ↑ Sima Hui's "not his hour," said dozens of chapters earlier, is only now paid in full: the problem was never that he wasn't clever enough. The book decided at the outset that cleverness would not be the last move.

The Wuzhang Plains. Gravely ill, he sets out seven lamps in his tent to petition for more years—if the central lamp burns for seven nights, the years are granted. On the sixth night Wei Yan bursts in with an urgent report, moving fast, and the draught puts the lamp out. Jiang Wei draws his sword; Zhuge Liang stops him and says it is fate, and no fault of his. He hands over his military writings, arranges every step of the withdrawal, and specifies how to fool the enemy after his death: carve a wooden figure of him and seat it in his carriage. He dies in an army camp in the autumn wind. The pursuing army sees the carriage wheeled out and bolts, giving the language a proverb about a dead Zhuge chasing off a living Sima. A joke placed directly after the heaviest page in the book—the storyteller's old trade, which knows the audience is crying and knows they cannot cry indefinitely.

The next thirty years are compressed hard. Jiang Wei campaigns north nine times and grows weaker with each; eunuchs take the court. In the north the Sima family spends three generations hollowing out the Cao emperors, doing to them precisely what Cao Cao did to the Han. Deng Ai brings an army over the trackless mountains at Yinping, his men rolling down the cliffs wrapped in felt, and appears without warning on the western plain. The last emperor of Shu surrenders. At a banquet in the enemy capital his hosts have western music played; his retainers put their heads down and weep, and he smiles and says:

"I am happy here. I never think of Shu."—Romance of the Three Kingdoms, near the close; Liu Shan at Luoyang, my rendering

Jiang Wei makes one last attempt at restoration, turning a general against his masters, and is killed when it fails. A dozen years later the Sima heir takes the northern throne outright and then finishes the south. The empire is one thing again, and the book closes on the line it opened with: the empire long united must divide, long divided must unite.

Taken whole, what drives these hundred and twenty chapters is not the fatalism of the famous opening line but the decision made in that garden: to put private loyalty above reasons of state. It is the only start-up capital Liu Bei's side ever has—what else persuades men worth ten thousand soldiers to follow a sandal-weaver through twenty years of homelessness? And it is the cause of death: Guan Yu uses it to release the enemy who should have died, then uses its other face (a man like me answers to nobody) to lose the province; Zhang Fei uses it to put himself under his own officers' knives; Liu Bei stakes the kingdom on it. Cao Cao carries no such freight—use whoever is needed, discard whoever is not—and his son ends up on the throne. The book spends a hundred and twenty chapters demonstrating how moving honour is, and the same hundred and twenty demonstrating that honour loses, and loses necessarily.

Three Slow-Motion Replays

1 · Plum wine: how one true statement becomes the most dangerous moment in the book

Not a blade is drawn in this scene; the tension is built entirely out of asymmetric information. The reader holds every card—we know Liu Bei is playing the harmless gardener, we know he has already joined a conspiracy against his host, we know one wrong syllable is fatal. Cao Cao holds half: he can see that Liu Bei is a great man, but not that Liu Bei has already moved. So the same sentence is two different sentences in two pairs of ears: Cao Cao thinks he is paying a compliment, and Liu Bei hears a verdict.

The props are handled by a professional. First the green plums, which prompt Cao Cao's own anecdote about telling a thirsty army there was a plum orchard ahead and watching their mouths water on a lie; placing that story here is itself a warning that this man's core competence is making people believe in things that do not exist. Then the dragon-shaped cloud, which lets him hold forth on how a dragon can be vast or tiny, can rise or hide—describing Liu Bei to Liu Bei's face. Then the thunder, the one moment in the novel when the weather saves somebody outright.

The finest touch is the gesture. He does not point at Liu Bei and say you; he points at Liu Bei, then at himself. The double gesture converts an unmasking into an invitation: I acknowledge you as my equal—do you accept? Liu Bei answers by dropping his chopsticks, a failure of the body, which is more convincing than any line could be. For the next ninety chapters the two of them never speak honestly face to face again.

2 · The Huarong Road: how to make a reader hope the hero disobeys orders

The construction here is the shrewdest in the book. The author does not simply put Guan Yu in the road; he runs two rehearsals first. Cao Cao laughs, says that an ambush here would finish him, and Zhao Yun comes out. Deeper into the rout he laughs again, and Zhang Fei comes out. By now the reader has been conditioned: when Cao Cao laughs, something happens. So before the third laugh everyone knows someone is waiting; the only question is who.

And the author gives that position to the one man who will not kill him. It is a piece of narrative arithmetic: if Zhang Fei blocks the road the story ends here, and only with Guan Yu can the moral weight be extracted in full. Better still, Guan Yu has signed for the assignment on pain of death. So letting Cao Cao go is not simply softness; it is paying another man's debt with his own life.

Then the pacing. Cao Cao does not beg. He itemises: the passes opened, the robe, the horse. The persuader's instrument is not an argument but an account book. When Guan Yu turns his horse's head aside, the novel records no interior monologue at all—only the movement. The heaviest moral decision in the book gets not one word of explanation, because honour that requires explaining is not honour.

3 · The seven lamps: a death staged as an astronomical event

Zhuge Liang's is the only death in the book in which the protagonist participates in his own procedure. He is not beaten—the enemy cannot get in. He is collected by his body and by the arithmetic of fate together. The author makes it visible as a ritual: seven lamps, seven nights, the central flame. Its function is to convert an abstraction—his allotted span has run out—into a countdown the reader can watch.

The way the lamp dies is chosen with great precision. Not a night raid, not a traitor: Wei Yan, one of his own, hurrying in with a report. No villain, no conspiracy, just a draught brought in by a conscientious subordinate. Jiang Wei moves to kill him and is stopped: it is not his fault. That refusal removes the last available object of blame, leaving only heaven.

And then the joke. One page after the death of the most beloved figure in the book, Sima Yi is made to gallop away from a wooden dummy. This is the inherited technology of the chaptered novel: the storyteller knows the room is crying and knows it cannot cry all night, so he supplies a laugh, pulls everyone back, and goes on. The joke also carries a second meaning—alive he could not outwit heaven, and dead he can still outwit men. It is both a pressure valve and an epitaph.

The Book's Core Device: Seven Parts Fact, Three Parts Fiction

The Qing scholar Zhang Xuecheng left a much-quoted verdict on this novel in his notebooks:

"Seven parts fact, three parts fiction—so that readers are constantly confused."—Zhang Xuecheng, Bingchen zhaji, my rendering

That is the device. This is not history and not pure invention; it is something more dangerous than either—invention built on a foundation of fact. Four components.

One: real skeleton, fictional joints. Dates, campaigns, offices and the order in which people die follow the official history closely enough that a reader can check them; but everything the histories cannot record—what a man thought before deciding, how two advisers talked, why a defeat happened—is supplied by the novel. And human memory of history preserves only the joints. What you remember is the fog on the river, not the year.

Two: politics and war broken into repeatable "stratagems." The planted letter, the flogging that sells a defection, the chained ships, the empty city—each with a stated premise, a sequence, and a condition under which it fails, each short enough to tell over dinner. This is the technical reason it travels further than any history: it rewrites unrepeatable events as repeatable procedures. The cost is that complex politics is reduced to a contest of wits.

Three: an enforced moral compass. Liu Bei's people are "us," Cao Cao is "them," the south wobbles in between. Luo Guanzhong did not invent this—the eleventh-century storytellers already had it—but the Mao recension tightened it to the limit with rewritten titles, marginal commentary, and the quiet softening of anything unflattering to Liu Bei. Readers who think they are reading history are reading history with an editorial attached.

Four: fate as final arbiter. Every intelligence in the book must bow at the last moment to something it cannot compute: the rain in the ravine, the draught that kills the lamp, the recluse's "not his hour" delivered eighty chapters early. This is what separates it from a manual of statecraft—it teaches you to calculate, and then tells you that calculation is not the last word.

What It Makes You Think About

Question 1
Guan Yu lets Cao Cao go on the Huarong Road. Is that the moral summit of the book, or the bill it should be made to pay?

Take the defence first, and make it stronger than his admirers do. A man who can convert a kindness he has received into a promotion can convert anything he is loyal to—sell out a benefactor for the state today, and tomorrow you can sell out the state for something larger. What holds Liu Bei's people together when they have nothing else is the promise that some things here are not for sale, and Guan Yu is the physical proof of that promise. Letting Cao Cao live announces to the whole empire that this side's credit is real—and credit is the one resource you can manufacture from nothing in a collapsing world.

Now the prosecution, at full strength. He had signed for the assignment. On that road he was not representing himself but an alliance that had just won Red Cliffs with other men's lives. He settled a private debt with public assets. Everyone killed in the next several decades of war can, in principle, be charged back to that stretch of mud. It is the purest specimen of private virtue purchased at public expense, and the only reason it does not look like a crime is that the author writes it so beautifully.

There is a third reading, and it may be the least comfortable: Zhuge Liang knew he would do it and posted him there anyway—so the person who actually decided was never Guan Yu. Afterwards the novel has Zhuge Liang charge the whole thing to fate, which absolves everybody. Once an act is described as destined, nobody has to answer for it; this is the book's most frequently used move, and the one most worth distrusting.

Bring it home. Have you ever spent your company's, your team's or your family's resources to settle a debt that was purely your own? It almost certainly felt like loyalty at the time. The test is not what you intended; it is whether you would still do it if the cost fell on a third party who never got a vote.

Question 2
Zhuge Liang marches north six times knowing he cannot win. Is that loyalty, or spending a country to repay one man's kindness?

The defence is hard to dent. First, standing still is dying: one province against nine, and the gap widening every year; passivity only loses more comprehensively. Second, the whole legitimacy of the state rests on irreconcilable war with the usurper—on what grounds does it still call itself Han? On the grounds that it is still fighting. Settle into the west and it becomes a regional warlord's holding and comes apart from inside. The northern campaigns are not a military choice, they are the country's mode of existence. Third, there is that deathbed offer of the throne on the table, and he takes the most exhausting and least rewarding road available: he does not usurp, and he does not coast; he burns himself as the wick.

The prosecution is equally strong. Continuous war means grain and conscripts extracted from one province's peasants year after year, and the attrition is real. He micromanaged to the point of personally reviewing minor punishments, converting institutional work into one man's work—so that when he died there was nobody to take over. The moment a person makes himself indispensable, he has already passed sentence on the organisation. And the deeper question: the origin of all of this is a private kindness—three visits to a hut. Is there a step missing between one man's gratitude and a national policy binding hundreds of thousands?

The everyday shape is common enough. To avoid failing someone—a parent, a mentor, the person who gave you your start—you sink your life into something you privately know you cannot win. From outside it reads as nobility; from inside, the line between a calling and a debt is rarely clear. One unkind but useful test: if that person told you tomorrow that you were released, would you feel grief, or relief?

Question 3
Casting the winner as the villain: is that literature's conscience, or self-deception at national scale?

Give the case for it properly. If history can only be graded on outcomes, then everything that was crushed was crushed for nothing. What this book has done for six hundred years is train a whole culture's moral instinct to care about the side that lost without changing. That is not cheap sentiment: the novel hands Cao Cao every real victory there is—territory, talent, the throne, an heir—and withholds exactly one thing, the reader's allegiance. Which happens to be the thing fiction can do and history cannot.

The other side cuts deeper. First, it makes readers impatient with competence and results: Cao Cao's agricultural colonies restored production across the north, and his edicts recruited on ability regardless of birth or private morals; the number of people who lived because of that may exceed the other two kingdoms combined, and the novel gives it almost no space. Second, the imbalance in the characterisation is universally conceded—the verdict of the modern writer Lu Xun (1881–1936; no relation to the southern commander whose name is romanised the same way) has never been answered:

"Wishing to display Liu Bei's generosity, he made him seem false; wishing to display Zhuge Liang's cleverness, he made him seem uncanny."—Lu Xun, A Brief History of Chinese Fiction, my rendering

One weeps and weeps, the other calculates everything, and the two heroes end up less human than the villain. Third, and most practical: this compass trains the ability to pick a side, not the ability to judge. The reader learns to ask first whether a man is one of us, and only then whether what he says is true.

Today: how much of your judgment of a company, a politician or a colleague is decided by the story they tell about what they are fighting for? We are the weaker side, we keep our word, we don't use dirty methods—that narrative is extremely effective, so effective that it is very often manufactured on purpose. The book teaches us to love Liu Bei. It does not teach us to tell a real one from someone telling his story.

Question 4
"Long united, must divide; long divided, must unite." Historical law, or an anaesthetic that stops you asking?

As wisdom: the line dismantles any illusion of a permanent order. It teaches readers to measure in centuries and therefore not to be hysterical about the immediate score; it is also honest, in that everyone in the book strains to the limit and the empire finally falls to a family that barely appears on stage. That is as cold a refutation of the great-man theory as anyone has written.

As anaesthetic: fatalism is convenient precisely because it explains everything and therefore explains nothing. The rain in the ravine and the wind on the plain are, functionally, a dignified formula the author prepares for defeat. Translate "we lost" into "the hour had not come," and the losing side never has to audit the plan; it only has to sigh. And union is quietly assumed to be the good outcome, with the price struck out: what follows the reunification in this period is a fresh round of civil wars and a longer fragmentation. The sentence sounds like a pendulum. Each swing is paid for in millions of lives.

Bring it home: the last time you attributed a failure to bad timing, was that clarity or evasion? Afterwards the two look almost identical, and there is exactly one way to tell them apart—clarity can also say what timing would have worked and what should have been done differently, while evasion stops at the sigh. Zhuge Liang, dying, hands over his writings and specifies every step of the retreat, which puts him in the first category. He does the remaining work after the sigh, and that is the most instructive thing about him, and the thing the sigh most easily hides.

Motifs: What This Book Keeps Returning To

The biographical record is nearly blank and the attributions are contested, so this section can only be honest about it: rather than "Luo Guanzhong's motifs," these are the obsessions of a whole family of historical romances. The other works bearing his name treat the same kind of moment—a dynasty rotted through, and men from nowhere binding themselves together by personal quality and changing the world. And Water Margin (variously attributed to Shi Nai'an, to Luo, or to both) tells the other half of the same story: here a band of sworn brothers goes after the empire; there a band of sworn brothers is absorbed by it.

Obsession one: the small group held together by private loyalty in a collapsing age. Three men in a garden, a hundred and eight outlaws on a marsh—loyalty first, enterprise second; and the pleasure these books return to is not winning battles but the moment a few people recognise each other in a room. Obsession two: such groups are always crushed by something larger. One is crushed by fate, the other by an amnesty—the more moving the writing, the colder the settlement. It is the same hand.

Criticism and Controversy

  • Being read as history. This is its largest side effect. Zhou Yu has been libelled for six centuries; Cao Cao is flattened into a stock schemer; a great many people believe the straw-boat arrows and the empty city actually happened. One novel has shaped public historical consciousness more than the entire body of scholarship on the period, which is simultaneously its achievement and its problem.
  • Characters as types. Mao Zonggang praised three of them as "the three superlatives"—supreme in honour, in cunning, in intelligence—and superlative means pushed to an edge. The advantage is that you never forget them; the cost is that they barely change. Guan Yu's character does not move an inch between his entrance and his death. Cao Cao, by contrast, is the most layered figure in the book, which is a quiet irony for a novel built to condemn him.
  • Women. There is scarcely a woman in it with a will of her own. Diaochan is an instrument of a plot and vanishes from the narrative once it works; Sun Quan's sister is a bargaining chip. The least defensible passage is an episode in which a hunter, unable to feed the fugitive Liu Bei, kills his own wife and serves her—presented by the novel as an act of honour, and later rewarded with gold by Cao Cao. A modern reader should stop here: it is the ugliest cross-section of the book's moral system, and it demonstrates that the honour in question is organised entirely around relations between men.
  • The text most people cite isn't quite the text. Today's standard 120-chapter version is the seventeenth-century Mao recension, which differs materially from the earliest surviving 1522 printing: rewritten chapter titles, wholesale replacement of poems, and a sharpened slant. "Luo Guanzhong writes" very often means "the Maos wrote."
  • The stratagems, extracted and abused. Later readers mine it as a manual of manipulation and a management-case anthology. The novel's own conclusion is the exact opposite: the best calculator in it loses the most completely. Learning the tricks and ignoring the ending is the most common misreading there is.
  • Honour, nationalised. Successive dynasties promoted Guan Yu by decree until he was a god with state sacrifices, and he was then adopted by guilds, merchant networks and secret societies. What happens to a fictional character on the way to becoming a deity stops being a literary question fairly early on.
  • Translation losses. The English reader has Moss Roberts's complete version (1991) and the earlier Brewitt-Taylor (1925). What survives least well is the antithetical chapter-title couplets, the density of interpolated verse, and above all the roll of a hundred-plus similar-sounding names, which a Chinese reader distinguishes by the shape of the characters and an English reader cannot.

Ten Sentences to Take Away

1. The fuse is not the famous line about union and division. It is the private oath in the peach garden—loyalty placed above reasons of state, which is both this side's start-up capital and its cause of death.

2. "Better that I wrong the world" is not an excuse but a principle stated aloud; Cao Cao is the only man here who will say his own operating logic in full.

3. The dangerous part of the plum-wine scene isn't Liu Bei's act. It is that Cao Cao says the quiet part out loud and lets him go anyway.

4. Cao Cao's face changes when Guan Yu bows for the horse, because the reason given is: now I can reach my brother faster.

5. The coldest thing on the Huarong Road is not that Guan Yu spares him. It is that Zhuge Liang knew and posted him there—three kingdoms need Cao Cao alive.

6. The horse that kills its rider kills the man who borrowed it; and if either one of the two prodigies could settle the empire, then with both alive one of them has to go.

7. Losing Jing Province does not cost some counties. It costs the plan—every campaign afterwards is pushed uphill on a strategy missing half of itself.

8. The brothers do not die on the same day; they are pulled down one by one by each other's deaths—pride, haste, obsession, each a direct product of the oath.

9. The empty city works not because of the zither but because of fifteen years of never gambling: a lifetime of caution staked on one hand.

10. The rain in the ravine and the wind on the plain say it all—the book teaches you to calculate, then tells you calculation isn't the last move; and its finest moment is that after the sigh he still finishes arranging the retreat.
