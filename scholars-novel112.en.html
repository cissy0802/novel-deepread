Novels, Deep-Read · Wu Jingzi · No.112

The Scholars

Rulin waishi · The Scholars · Wu Jingzi · completed c. 1750 · 56 chapters in the received text

中文 →

The provincial examination hall, on an ordinary day between sittings. A few merchants in town to buy stock tip the gatekeeper to let them have a look around.
Trailing behind them is a man in his sixties who keeps their accounts. He has never passed even the lowest examination. He steps into one of the cells, sees the two writing boards laid out in a neat row, and something in him gives way. He beats his head against the board and drops to the floor.
They bring him round. He says nothing and does it again, then goes wailing from cell to cell, rolling on the ground, until he coughs up blood.
A few months later he passes. A few years after that he is the examiner of a whole province, the one sitting up front while other men bend over the boards.

Full spoilers ahead. This covers how Fan Jin loses his mind on the day he passes and who slaps him back to sanity, what Yan the Licentiate's two raised fingers mean on his deathbed, what became of the devoted boy who studied by night beside his paralysed father, why a father laughs out loud when his daughter starves herself to death, and who, in the last chapter, the author hands the scholars' legacy to. The book has no hero. Each character tells his story and passes the baton, and you can't see what the race was about until the last leg.

In one sentence

From the middle years of the Ming down to the 1590s, wave after wave of educated men chase "rank, fame and fortune." They sit examinations until they crack, swindle their way to money and pose their way to fame. A few try to start over, building a shrine in Nanjing and staging one perfect ancient rite, and then they scatter. In the final chapter, whatever was worth keeping in the scholar's ideal turns up in four tradesmen who never sat an exam.

Where it stands

Wu Jingzi (1701–1754) came from Quanjiao in Anhui. His great-grandfather's and grandfather's generations had been crowded with examination degrees, and by his own time the family was in decline. He spent his inheritance freely and in 1733 moved to Nanjing. A couple of years later he was nominated for the boxue hongci, a special examination the throne held to recruit eminent scholars outside the usual track. In the end he didn't go to Beijing, citing illness. Du Shaoqing, the character in the novel who fakes an illness to dodge an imperial summons, is Wu himself. The book was finished around 1750 and never printed in his lifetime. The earliest surviving edition is the Woxian Caotang text of 1803.

Its place in literary history is clear-cut. In his Brief History of Chinese Fiction, Lu Xun says that only with this book did China at last have a novel that deserved to be called satire. It was the first long Chinese novel to take an entire class, the educated elite, as its subject, and it does so with almost no plot. Its material is people. The standard English translation, The Scholars, is by Yang Xianyi and Gladys Yang (1957).

The Story

First, the ladder. Without it, nobody's madness in what follows makes sense. A man who had not passed was a candidate (tongsheng), however old he was. Passing the county, prefectural and qualifying examinations made him a licentiate (xiucai), the first rung of real status. Passing the provincial examination, held every three years, made him a provincial graduate (juren), eligible for office. Passing in the capital made him a metropolitan graduate (jinshi). The required form was the eight-legged essay. Topics came from the Four Books, the candidate had to speak in the voice of the sages, and the body was built from four pairs of strictly parallel passages, which are the eight "legs." There was also a side door. A family could buy the title of Imperial College student (jiansheng), and that got a man into the provincial hall without the lower exams.

Act I · Prologue: a man who won't sit the exams watches stars fall

The book opens at the end of the Yuan dynasty. Wang Mian is a village boy in Zhuji, Zhejiang. His father dies when he is seven, and at ten he is sent to herd a neighbour's buffalo. One summer evening a storm passes over the lake. The low sun catches the lotuses, beads of water roll across the petals, and he stands there wishing he could paint it. He saves for pigments and teaches himself. Before long townspeople are paying for his lotuses.

The county magistrate wants the paintings as a gift for a superior and summons him, so Wang Mian slips away to Shandong. Later the rebel leader who will become the first Ming emperor comes to his door in person to ask about governing, and Wang tells him, in substance, to win people over through humanity and justice. When the dynasty is founded, Wang reads in the official gazette how the new government will choose its officials: an exam every three years on the Four Books and Five Classics, written as eight-legged essays. He shakes his head. Now that scholars have this one road to glory, he says, they will think lightly of learning, conduct, and the judgement of when to serve and when to withdraw. (Chapter 1)

That night he watches a hundred or so small stars streak down toward the south-east. Heaven, he says, is sending a host of star-spirits down to sustain the fortunes of letters, though he won't live to see it. Seed ① Soon afterwards the court tries to give him a post. He flees into the Kuaiji mountains and dies there. On a first reading it's just a graceful close to a hermit's tale, and the stars look like a benediction.

Act II · Two old candidates: a writing board and a hen

The story jumps more than a century, to a village in Wenshang county, Shandong. The village school has hired Zhou Jin, past sixty, still a candidate, wearing a shabby felt hat. A young new licentiate named Mei Jiu makes up a jingle at his expense in front of the whole table. One rainy evening a newly minted provincial graduate, Wang Hui, shelters in the temple and borrows the schoolroom for supper. His table holds chicken, fish, duck and pork. Zhou gets a dish of old greens and a pot of hot water. The next morning Zhou spends hours sweeping up chicken bones and fish spines.

Flipping through the pupils' copybooks, Wang Hui sees the name of a seven-year-old, Xun Mei, and goes pale. A couple of years back, he says, he dreamed he saw the metropolitan pass list, and next to his own name was one Xun Mei of Wenshang. And it turns out to be a little boy, which only shows dreams can't be trusted. Seed ② Everyone laughs.

Zhou loses even this job. He goes to the provincial capital to keep books for his merchant brother-in-law, and that leads to the head against the board. Moved by his weeping, the merchants pool two hundred taels to buy him the Imperial College title. He gets into the examination hall and passes, then passes in the capital, and is eventually posted to Guangdong as education commissioner, the official who examines every would-be licentiate in the province.

In the hall in Guangzhou he notices one candidate who is sallow and grey-bearded, shivering in a threadbare hemp gown in the twelfth month. The register says he is thirty. Asked, he admits he is fifty-four and has sat the exam more than twenty times. Something catches in Zhou. He reads the man's paper and finds it nonsense. He reads it again and finds some sense in it. On the third reading he declares it the finest prose under heaven, every word a pearl. The man is Fan Jin, and Zhou passes him first on the list. Was the essay really good, or did Zhou see himself in that hemp gown? The book doesn't say.

Fan's father-in-law, Butcher Hu, turns up with a length of pig's intestine and a bottle of wine to celebrate, and lectures his son-in-law while he's at it. When Fan asks to borrow money to travel to the provincial exam, Hu spits in his face and tells him a toad shouldn't dream of eating swan. Fan goes anyway, behind Hu's back. When he gets home the family hasn't eaten in two or three days. On the morning the results are posted, his mother sends him to market to sell their one laying hen for rice. He is standing in the market clutching the hen, waiting for an offer, when a neighbour runs up shouting that he's passed.

At home he finds the official notice pasted on the wall: seventh place. He claps his hands, cries out with a laugh that he's done it, he's passed (Chapter 3), and falls over backwards, jaws clenched, out cold. Revived, he bolts out of the house clapping, tumbles into a pond, and runs on to the market dripping mud and laughing. Someone suggests that a slap from the man he fears most will scare him back to his senses, and everyone thinks of Butcher Hu. This time Hu balks. A provincial graduate, he says, is a star from heaven, and striking one brings retribution. It takes two bowls of wine before he works up the nerve. One slap and Fan comes to. But Hu's hand starts to throb and won't bend again. Convinced heaven is punishing him, he asks for a medicinal plaster.

That afternoon the local squire, Zhang, arrives in a sedan chair with fifty taels and the deed to a house. Others bring land and shops, and down-at-heel families come offering themselves as servants for protection. Fan's mother looks around the room full of things, says over and over that all of it is hers now, gives a great laugh, falls back and chokes to death.

Act III · After success: a shrimp ball, two lamp wicks, a pillow-chest

While Fan is in mourning, Squire Zhang takes him to call on Magistrate Tang of Gaoyao county. The aim is "autumn wind," cadging a gift on the strength of a connection. At dinner the cups and chopsticks are inlaid with silver, and Fan shrinks back, saying a mourner cannot use such things. Porcelain cups and ivory chopsticks are brought, and he still won't. Only plain white bamboo will do. The magistrate starts to worry that a man this strict about mourning won't eat meat, then sees Fan pick a fat shrimp ball out of the bird's-nest soup and pop it into his mouth. Lu Xun quotes the whole passage in his history and remarks, in essence, that there isn't a single word of condemnation and yet the hypocrisy is laid completely bare. The author never scolds. The man simply stands there undressed.

Next come the Yan brothers. The elder, Senior Licentiate Yan (a gongsheng, a licentiate selected for the Imperial College), is the local bully. When a neighbour's piglet wanders into his yard he keeps it, and on a boat trip he claims a few slices of cloud-cake are precious medicine so he can extort the boatmen. The younger, Imperial College Student Yan, is worth well over a hundred thousand taels and can't bring himself to buy a pound of pork. When his wife is dying, her two brothers, both licentiates, accept a hundred taels each and loudly endorse raising his most attentive concubine, Zhao, to the rank of wife. Then the miser falls ill himself, loses his voice, and lies there with two fingers raised, unable to die. That scene gets its own slow-motion replay below.

As for Wang Hui and his dream: he and that seven-year-old do pass the metropolitan exam in the same year, on the same list Payoff ② ↑, which makes them "classmates" in the examination sense, a bond men kept for life. The author makes a mocked dream come true to the letter, on a list full of mediocrities. The dream was accurate, and the accuracy means nothing. It was planted at a rainy-night supper in Chapter 2, where it looked like a graduate showing off to a village tutor. When it pays off in Chapter 7, you realise that the only thing standing between that table of chicken bones and the pass list was luck.

Wang Hui is later posted as prefect of Nanchang, taking over from Prefect Qu. At the handover he asks what "accommodations" the post offers. Qu's son, Qu Jingyu, answers with a smile. Under his father, he says, the yamen rang with three sounds: poetry being chanted, chess pieces being placed, and songs being sung. From now on he expects it will be the clink of silver scales, the clack of the abacus and the thwack of the bamboo rod. (Chapter 8) Soon after, the Prince of Ning rebels, and Wang Hui goes over to him. When the rebellion collapses he flees by night. On a boat in Zhejiang he runs into Qu's grandson, the young Master Qu, accepts some travelling money, and leaves him a pillow-chest. Inside is a manuscript of poems by the early-Ming poet Gao Qi that no one else has seen. Seed ③ Then Wang Hui changes his name and vanishes.

Young Qu has the poems printed with his own name on the cover as "editor," and overnight he becomes a celebrated young man of letters. The two Lou brothers of Huzhou, sons of a former grand secretary, find the examinations vulgar and want to discover true worthies. What they find is an old pedant, a fake recluse and a self-styled knight-errant named Zhang Tiebi. Late one night Zhang turns up with a blood-soaked leather bag and says it holds the head of his enemy. He borrows five hundred taels to repay an old debt of honour and never comes back. When the brothers finally steel themselves to open the bag, they find a pig's head. The men who tried to go around the examinations in search of real talent found a cheaper class of fake.

Young Qu marries Miss Lu, whose father raised her on eight-legged essays as if she were a son. When she discovers her husband can only write poetry, she weeps in private, and later she shuts her four-year-old in a room to drill him in the form.

Act IV · Mr Ma, and the boy he saved

One of Young Qu's servants elopes with a maid and is caught. Rummaging through her things, he finds the pillow-chest and realises it belonged to Wang Hui, a wanted traitor. Harbouring a rebel's property could cost his master his head, so he uses it for blackmail. The man who steps in to settle the matter is Young Qu's new friend, Mr Ma. Payoff ③ ↑ When the chest changed hands in Chapter 8 it looked like nothing more than a box for some poems. Six chapters later you see it was a bomb. Young Qu won his hollow fame from that chest and nearly lost his life to it, and the man who defused it is the least fame-hungry person in the book.

Ma Chunshang, known as Mr Ma the Second, makes his living as an anthologist. He selects and annotates model examination essays and sells the collections to candidates. He has been failing the exams for more than twenty years and still believes in them completely. Even Confucius, he tells Young Qu, would be writing examination essays if he were alive today, since otherwise who would give him a post. He means it. To get a new friend out of trouble he hands over every tael of his savings, a little over ninety.

Then he takes a walk around West Lake by himself. What he notices along the way is the meat hanging in the taverns and the snacks in the bowls. When groups of women pass, he keeps his eyes down. The hills and water make no impression on him at all. He just walks and eats. At one spot he finds calligraphy by a former emperor enshrined upstairs, hurriedly straightens his clothes, and bows five times. It's funny and hard to watch at the same time. Here is a good man so full of one system that there is no room left in him for West Lake.

On a hill above the city he comes across a young fortune-teller reading examination essays at his stall. The young man is Kuang Chaoren from Yueqing, stranded far from home, with a paralysed father back in the village. Ma gives him ten taels, some clothes and a set of books, and tells him to go home, look after his father and work on his essays.

Kuang goes home. He keeps the family going with petty trade, carries his paralysed father to the privy at night, and then studies until the small hours. The new county magistrate, Li Benying, passes by late one night, hears him reading aloud, is deeply moved, and sponsors him through to licentiate status. On his deathbed Kuang's father calls him close. His devotion to family is rare, the old man says, but he mustn't let a little prosperity grow a snob's outlook in him and change the heart he had as a boy. And when he marries he should choose a family of his own standing and not chase wealth or climb above himself. That is the gist. Seed ④

Then Magistrate Li is impeached. Afraid of being dragged down with him, Kuang leaves for Hangzhou. From a coterie of self-styled poets by the lake he learns that verse is easy and that a few famous names make a poetry society. From a yamen fixer named Pan the Third he learns something more lucrative, forging documents and sitting exams in other men's names, two hundred taels a time. Pan also arranges a match for him with the daughter of a poor man named Zheng.

Later Li is restored to office and promoted to a censorate post in the capital, and he recommends Kuang for a teaching position. Li wants to marry his niece to him and asks whether he already has a wife. Kuang says no. So he makes a respectable second marriage in Beijing, while his first wife is sent back to his village, can't bear the hardship, and dies. Payoff ④ ↑ In Chapter 17 the father's last words read like the ordinary worries of an old man. Looking back, they work almost point by point as a list of everything his son goes on to do. The author never steps in to judge Kuang. He has a dying man write the verdict in advance, then lets the son break it clause by clause.

Back in Hangzhou on business, Kuang hears that Pan the Third is in prison and wants to see him. Kuang says that, as a servant of the court, visiting a convict would damage his official reputation, and he doesn't go. On the boat back to the capital he boasts that in five provinces scholars keep a tablet on their desks honouring "the late Confucian Master Kuang." A fellow passenger, Niu Buyi, points out with a laugh that "late" is for the dead, and Kuang is very much alive. Kuang goes red and argues. (Chapter 20) Along the way he dismisses Mr Ma as all rules and no talent. That is the last we see of the boy who studied by night beside his father's bed.

Smaller frauds follow. A youth named Niu Pu gets hold of the poems of the dead poet Niu Buyi and simply takes over his name, and when Niu Buyi's widow travels hundreds of miles to find her husband, she finds a stranger. In this world a name is worth more than a person. You can steal it, borrow it, or print it on someone else's poems.

Act V · Nanjing: a shrine

When the baton reaches Nanjing, the air changes. The first figure there is an actor, Bao Wenqing. He pleads for a magistrate in trouble and afterwards turns down every tael of a five-hundred-tael thank-you. When a fellow actor swans about in a scholar's gown, Bao tells him off for forgetting his place. The man in the book with the deepest respect for scholarly dignity is an actor, a man scholars look down on.

Then come two cousins of the Du family. Du Shenqing is an aesthete to the point of fastidiousness who longs for a beautiful young man as a soulmate. A friend sets him up, and the beauty turns out to be a fat, heavily bearded Taoist priest. He stages a contest ranking the city's opera actors by looks, and when he has had his fill of elegance he goes off to the capital to look for an official post.

His cousin Du Shaoqing is another sort of man. He pours out his family's money like water. Anyone who asks gets something, until there is nothing left. The old family steward, dying, urges him to go to Nanjing, where he might find kindred spirits, so he sells the house and moves. One spring day, a gold wine cup in one hand and his wife's hand in the other, he strolls laughing along the ridge of Qingliang Hill for the better part of a mile while onlookers gape. A husband holding his wife's hand in public was scandalous then. When the provincial governor recommends him for an imperial summons, he fakes an illness to get out of it and then asks his wife whether staying in Nanjing to look at flowers and drink wine with her isn't better than going to Beijing.

A friend of theirs, Chi Hengshan, proposes a project: to build a shrine to Taibo south of the city and honour him once with the ancient rites and music. Seed ⑤ Taibo was the eldest son of the Zhou patriarch. Knowing that his father wished to pass the succession to the youngest brother, he went off to the far south and gave up the throne. The Analects praise him for yielding the realm three times. In a book where everyone is grabbing, they choose to honour a man who gave way. Chi's idea is that rehearsing the rites together will form people of character and do the world some small good.

Meanwhile Zhuang Shaoguang is summoned to the capital for an audience with the emperor. He feels a stabbing pain in his scalp, and back at his lodgings he takes off his cap and a scorpion crawls out. He takes it as a sign that his way will not prevail and asks leave to go home. There is also Yu Yude, a poor scholar from Changshu who passes the highest exam and then chooses a quiet post as a lecturer at the Nanjing Imperial College. Proctoring an exam, he quietly pockets a crib sheet a candidate has smuggled in and returns it to him in private. The book's worthies are all gathered here, and what they share is that they don't grab.

In Chapter 37 the shrine is finished. Dr Yu leads the sacrifice, and more than seventy scholars perform the ancient music, incense and libations from dawn to dusk while crowds pack the street to watch. It is the high point of the novel, and the one place where the author lets himself write about goodness without irony.

The mood lasts only a few chapters. Once the story leaves Nanjing we get Xiao Yunxian, a young officer who recaptures a north-western frontier town and brings it back to life. He rebuilds the walls, digs irrigation channels, teaches the settlers to farm and opens schools. When he submits his accounts, the Board of Works audits them, finds he has "overspent" by more than seven thousand taels, and orders him to repay it himself. There is also Filial Son Guo, who walks across several provinces looking for his father, who has become a monk. He finds him in a Sichuan hermitage, and the monk flatly refuses to acknowledge him. The monk is Wang Hui, who went over to the rebel prince and fled under a new name. Guo settles outside the hermitage and takes odd jobs to support the father who won't admit he exists. The man who did real work is broken by an audit, and the man who once kept up appearances can't even acknowledge his own son.

Before long Dr Yu is transferred to Zhejiang. His friends see him off outside Nanjing's Sanshan Gate, and one by one they drift apart.

Act VI · Dispersal: a laugh, a ruined shrine, four tradesmen

Wang Yuhui is an old licentiate in Huizhou who never got further. He spends his days compiling books on ritual and village covenants to improve the common people. When his third daughter's husband dies, she tells her father she means to follow her husband and will stop eating. Her in-laws and her mother beg her not to. Wang tells her, in effect, that this is the sort of deed that wins a name in history, so how could he stand in her way, and that she should go ahead. She starves for eight days and dies. Her mother faints from weeping. Wang throws back his head and laughs, crying out "She died well! She died well!" (Chapter 48), and walks out of the room laughing.

The authorities put up a memorial arch for her and enshrine her tablet, there is a great banquet, and everyone toasts him. At home his wife weeps day after day, and he can't stand to be there, so he travels to Suzhou to clear his head. On the boat he sees a young woman dressed in white, the colour of mourning, and something in him turns over. Hot tears pour down his face.

Chapter 55 opens with the author remarking that by the twenty-third year of Wanli, the celebrated scholars of Nanjing had gradually worn away to nothing. Payoff ① ↑ Then he gives us four people. Ji Xianian lodges in temples and writes a superb hand, and when a rich man he doesn't respect asks for calligraphy, he sends him off with a tongue-lashing. Wang Tai sells paper spills for lighting pipes. Watching a game of weiqi, he can't resist sitting down, beats a master, and walks off laughing that beating a bad player is the best fun in the world. Gai Kuan once ran a pawnshop, gave his fortune away, and now sells tea outside the city wall. Jing Yuan is a tailor who plays the zither and practises calligraphy when his work is done. Earning a few coppers a day and eating his fill, he says, he envies no one's wealth and watches no one's face. Heaven doesn't own him and earth doesn't govern him. Who says only scholars get to be happy?

Gai Kuan takes an elderly neighbour to see the Taibo shrine. Half the main hall has fallen in, the doors and windows are gone, and a few country women are digging wild greens in the courtyard. As for the ritual vessels and instruments, nobody can say where they went. Payoff ⑤ ↑ The more solemnly the great sacrifice was written in Chapter 37, the colder this passage feels. The author built the book's one luminous moment with his own hands, and with his own hands he sets women foraging in its ruins. He doesn't show how it fell. He only shows that it has.

Finally Jing Yuan goes up Qingliang Hill to visit old Yu, who keeps a flower garden, and plays him a piece on the zither. The music is clear and mournful, and as he listens the old man begins to weep. That is where the book ends.

Think back to the stars Wang Mian watched fall in Chapter 1. Over fifty-odd chapters they drop into examination halls, yamens, poetry clubs and shrines and go out one after another. The ones still shining at the end belong to a calligrapher, a chess player, a tea seller and a zither player. Zither, chess, calligraphy and painting, the four accomplishments scholars were proudest of, end up in the hands of men who never sat an exam. The buffalo boy of the prologue and the four tradesmen of the last chapter close the book like a clasp.

If you trace all fifty-odd chapters back to a single fuse, it isn't any one person's act. It is the regulation in Chapter 1: the court announces that from now on a scholar needs to master just one kind of essay to rise. Zhou Jin's head against the board, Fan Jin's madness, Kuang Chaoren's turn and Wang Yuhui's laugh are all aftershocks of that one decree. Wang Mian saw through it the day it was issued. The rest of the book just confirms it, one life at a time.

Three slow-motion scenes

1 · Fan Jin's slap

The force of this scene lies in where the information sits. The heralds have already reached his house, so we know he has passed. He is the last to find out, still standing in the market waiting for someone to bid on a hen. He is holding the last thing his family owns while the biggest thing in his life falls on his head, and the drop between the two is his madness. Then Wu doesn't linger on the madness. He turns the camera to Butcher Hu, who spat in Fan's face the day before, now needs two bowls of wine to find the nerve for a single slap, and ends up with a hand that won't bend and a plaster on it. It isn't only Fan who loses his mind. It's that hand, a whole world's hand, which reversed direction overnight. Wu never once uses the word "snobbery." He lets the butcher's knuckles say it.

2 · Two fingers

The miser lies in bed with phlegm rattling in his throat, breath after breath, unable to let go. He works one hand out from under the quilt and holds up two fingers. His elder nephew leans in and asks whether there are two relatives he hasn't seen. He shakes his head. The younger nephew asks whether there are two sums of silver he hasn't accounted for. His eyes go wide and he shakes his head harder. The wet nurse suggests it's his two brothers-in-law, who aren't there. He shuts his eyes and shakes his head. Then Zhao, the concubine-made-wife, comes over and says she knows. There are two wicks burning in the oil lamp, and he's worried about the waste. She'll take one out. She pinches one out. Everyone looks at him. He nods once and dies. (end of Chapter 5, start of Chapter 6)

It is built as a suspense structure. Four people take turns guessing, and every guess exposes the guesser. The nephews are thinking about relatives and money, which is to say the estate. The nurse is thinking about the in-laws, the web of obligation. Only Zhao, who schemed her way into the wife's place, really knows him. The answer is absurd enough to laugh at, and then the laugh goes cold. A man worth a hundred thousand taels spends his last thought on the price of lamp oil. The guessing is a family portrait. The answer is a death mask.

3 · The laugh, and the tears on the boat

In the Wang Yuhui chapter Wu splits the father's reaction in two and keeps the halves apart. When his daughter dies, he laughs. At the arch and the banquet, he accepts the toasts. Only on a boat near Suzhou, at the sight of a stranger in white, do the tears come. Grief is deferred, and it is deferred to a place that has nothing to do with his daughter, set off by the colour of a stranger's clothes. That is far more devastating than showing him sob at the bedside. The doctrine lies so thick on him that his own grief has to get around the whole ceremony and travel a hundred miles before it can leak through a crack. Wu doesn't write that he regrets anything, or that he understands anything. Those tears are the entire verdict, and his own body writes it for him.

The core device: a relay with no hero

This isn't a speculative novel with a premise to work through. Its signature device has three parts.

First, the relay structure. No protagonist runs through the book. Zhou Jin leads to Fan Jin, Fan Jin to Squire Zhang, Zhang to the Yan brothers, and so on. Each character brings on the next and exits, sometimes flickering back dozens of chapters later as a name in someone's gossip. Lu Xun observed that although it is called a long novel, it reads like a string of short pieces. The structure is itself the argument. If everyone in a society is on the same ladder, it doesn't matter who plays the lead, because the lead is the ladder. The commentator in the 1803 edition said that the words "rank, fame and fortune" are the book's focus. The faces change like a lantern show, and those words never leave the stage.

Second, satire without a verdict. Earlier Chinese fiction was full of the storyteller's voice, forever turning to the audience to comment. Wu almost never does. Fan's shrimp ball, the miser's wick and Kuang's "late Master" are all shown as gesture and dialogue, and the judgement is left to you. The effect is that the moment you laugh, you've already passed sentence, and you can't pin it on the author.

Third, a symmetrical frame. Wang Mian in the first chapter and the four tradesmen in the last stand outside the examination system. Between them sit fifty-odd chapters inside it. The figures get closer to true worth as you approach the middle, peak at the Taibo shrine, and then slide back down. That gives the book a direction: the way out isn't at the top of the ladder but off it. In honesty, though, this way out is a literary gesture, not a social programme. The four tradesmen are idealised figures, not a sociological sample of late-Ming street life, and they get to be that free because the author gives them only one chapter.

What it makes you think about

One · The exams wrecked half of Zhou Jin's life and half of Fan Jin's. Without the exams, though, they would never have got near the board.

Imagine you're a poor scholar of that time and someone proposes abolishing the exams in favour of having notables and senior officials "recommend men of talent." Would you vote yes?

The strongest case for keeping the exams, however bad they are, is in the book itself. Every attempt to find talent outside them turns out worse. The Lou brothers go hunting for worthies and come home with a pig's head, and a summons to court depends on one governor's word. Zhou Jin had no family name and no connections. In Butcher Hu's world, the only thing that could raise a man selling a hen overnight was a paper that anyone could sit and that the examiner read without knowing whose it was. What it tested may have been hollow, but it was a rule strangers could agree on. Take it away and what remains is patronage and pedigree, and patronage and pedigree would never have picked Fan Jin.

The strongest case for changing them is that the problem isn't examining, it's examining only this one thing. Once a single kind of essay is the only exit, everyone spends their best twenty years on a skill unrelated to actually doing anything. A selection system that rewards something further and further from what it wants isn't selecting talent. It is mass-producing people who live for the test. And the men it does select, like Wang Hui, take office and immediately bring out the scales and the rod. A fair procedure that keeps producing unworthy officials will eventually drag down trust in the procedure itself.

Where does Wu stand? Through Wang Mian he attacks the "one road to glory," not learning itself. He turned down the special exam himself, yet his book offers no better alternative. Bring it home: university entrance exams, civil-service tests, every screen that looks only at the score. You can hate the board, but first be clear about what you'd replace it with, and whether the new board would tilt even further toward people who already have a way in.

Two · Your daughter says she'll starve herself to follow her dead husband. You're her father. Do you respect her or stop her?

Wang Yuhui didn't push his daughter. The idea was hers. All he did was not stand in her way, and then encourage her. Modern readers condemn him almost unanimously. Take the other side seriously for a moment.

The strongest case that he respected her goes like this. She was a grown woman. In her world, a widow's future was a lifetime as a dependant in her parents' house or a remarriage arranged for her, and she found both humiliating. She chose the most dignified ending her values allowed and received the highest recognition her world could give. What makes us so sure that overriding her choice with our values honours her more than her own judgement does? In cases like assisted dying, or a believer refusing a blood transfusion, we cite respect for the person's own wishes as our reason for not stopping them.

The strongest case that he should have stopped her asks who put that choice into her head in the first place. Wang spent his life writing books of ritual and moral instruction, and his daughter grew up inside them. When someone's "free will" happens to be the script you taught them, your not stopping them isn't respect. It's a director watching the actor reach the final scene. And what he gave her wasn't silence but encouragement and a laugh. At the moment he laughed, he was thinking about virtue, not about his daughter.

Where does Wu stand? The tears on the boat show where he leans: a doctrine can make a man laugh as his child dies, but the body refuses. Yet Wu doesn't make him repent, and no one in the book denounces him. Bring it home: workplaces that honour people for "soldiering on while ill," or you wearing yourself out for a job you insist you chose. The question is never only what she chose. It's also who drew the boundaries of what she could imagine choosing.

Three · Du Shaoqing fakes illness to avoid office. Xiao Yunxian serves, does real good, and is fined seven thousand taels. When a system is rotten, do you go in or walk away?

Nearly everyone the author loves chooses to stay out. Wang Mian flees to the mountains, Du Shaoqing fakes an illness, and Zhuang Shaoguang uses a scorpion as his excuse to go home. The one man who goes in and actually gets things done, Xiao Yunxian, is flattened by an audit.

The strongest case for walking away is that Xiao's fate proves the more real your work, the more surely the ledger comes for you. If you stay, the best outcome is to become Dr Yu, a gentle sinecurist who quietly shields people and stays clean himself, and that is already the limit. Better to stand outside than let your name endorse bad rules, and at least preserve one person who doesn't lie. Du Shaoqing walking hand in hand with his wife is a demonstration that a person can live without the system.

The strongest case for going in is that Xiao may have been fined, but the channels really were dug, the fields really were farmed, and the children really did learn to read. On the day Du Shaoqing pretended to be ill, the post didn't stay empty. It went to the next Wang Hui. Keeping your hands clean is a virtue only people with somewhere else to go can afford. Du had an inheritance to spend and Nanjing to retreat to. The settlers of that frontier town had neither. When a good man walks away, the system feels no shame. It just loses one person who was holding it up.

Where does Wu stand? His life stands with Du Shaoqing, who is Wu himself. But he wrote the Xiao Yunxian chapters, and they read the most like a serious drama and hurt the most. Bring it home: a workplace with a rotten culture. Do you quit, or stay and do what good you can while accepting that you'll get dirty? Each side will call the other cowardly, and the book's honesty lies in making both sides pay.

Motifs: what a one-novel author keeps returning to

Apart from this novel, what survives of Wu Jingzi is a collection of poems and prose and a commentary on the Book of Songs that was rediscovered only at the end of the twentieth century. So to find his obsessions you look at his life. The novel is what he produced by taking himself apart and dealing the pieces out to dozens of characters.

The first obsession is decline. He was born into an examination dynasty and squandered his share, and in his hometown he became the cautionary example parents held up to their sons, something he says himself in one of his lyrics. That is why the spendthrift Du Shaoqing is written with such relish and the snobbish relatives with such venom. The second is the question of when to serve. When should an educated man take office, and when should he withdraw? Hence a whole row of men who won't serve, Wang Mian, Du Shaoqing and Zhuang Shaoguang, with Xiao Yunxian as their counterweight. The third is ritual and music. According to Qing-era accounts, while in Nanjing he helped friends raise money for a shrine to past worthies, and when funds ran short he sold his old family house in Quanjiao to cover the gap. The Taibo shrine is close to a record of that, and its collapse reads like his own cold look back at all that enthusiasm.

This is a man who quit the race and then turned round to write about it. That's why the satire always carries a trace of tenderness. He understood that head against the board far too well.

Controversy and criticism

A loose structure. This is the most common complaint. Lu Xun's "like a string of short pieces" is neutral description. Hu Shi was blunter, saying in effect that the book has no overall design and is just short sketches strung together. Defenders reply that the relay form is what the theme requires. Still, the middle and later stretches, especially the Wuhe county chapters and the Tang brothers, do drag, and characters change so fast that it's hard to keep track of who is who.

The good characters are less alive than the bad. Fan Jin and the miser leap off the page, while Dr Yu and Zhuang Shaoguang feel like moral specimens, and the great sacrifice is solemn but dull. Wu's eyes are brightest when he is laughing at people, and his pen stiffens when he admires them.

The text and the chapter count. Wu's friend Cheng Jinfang recorded the book as fifty juan, but the surviving editions have fifty-six chapters. Chapter 56, the "posthumous honours list," in which the emperor awards degrees to the book's dead scholars, is widely regarded as a later addition, and a few other chapters have been suspected too. That is why this essay stops at Chapter 55.

Portraits of real people, and "public-mindedness." According to a late-Qing afterword by Jin He, many characters had real-life models. Du Shaoqing is the author, Mr Ma is based on his friend Feng Cuizhong, and Zhuang Shaoguang on the scholar Cheng Tingzuo. Lu Xun praised the book for satirising out of public-mindedness rather than spite, while some critics detect private grudges in certain portraits. The story is nominally set in the Ming but plainly depicts Qing society, so its "historical" detail shouldn't be taken at face value.

Women and orthodox morality. The book includes Shen Qiongzhi, who escapes being made a salt merchant's concubine and supports herself in Nanjing by selling her poems and embroidery, and she is often cited as a progressive touch. Yet Miss Lu is played for laughs as an essay fanatic, and the Wang Yuhui episode remains contested. Some read it as an indictment of a moral code that devours people. Others argue Wu doesn't reject widow martyrdom as such and only pities a father's pain. The text supports both readings.

Crossovers

  • Economics · signalling theory: In 1973 Michael Spence argued that in the job market, education can work purely as a signal. It pays not because of what you learned but because few people can bear its cost. The eight-legged essay looks like a pure signal, and its very emptiness made it "fair." Where the analogy breaks: Spence's signallers are rational choosers, and Wu's candidates are gambling their whole lives.
  • Sociology · the credential society: In The Credential Society (1979), Randall Collins argued that degrees often serve to mark off status groups and shut others out, not just to certify skill. On the day Fan Jin passes, strangers bring him a house and offer themselves as servants. Where it breaks: modern diplomas rarely rewrite every relationship overnight.
  • Economics · Goodhart's law: The economist Charles Goodhart's observation is usually paraphrased as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." That is Wang Mian's warning: the exam meant to gauge learning and conduct became the goal, so people trained only for the exam. Where it breaks: Goodhart was talking about monetary policy.
  • Social psychology · effort justification: In a 1959 experiment, Aronson and Mills found that the harder people work to join a group, the more they overvalue it. After twenty-odd years of failure, Mr Ma still insists even Confucius would be writing exam essays. Where it breaks: under Ma's devotion lies real kindness.

Ten lines to take away

① A man in his sixties beats his head against a writing board, and the book begins with that blow.

② On the day he passes, he is in the market holding the family's one hen, waiting for a bid.

③ The butcher's hand that won't bend again is what it looks like when a world reverses overnight.

④ A man with a hundred thousand taels spends his last breath on the cost of a lamp wick.

⑤ A mocked dream comes true to the letter, on a pass list full of mediocrities.

⑥ His father wrote out the verdict on his deathbed, and the son spent half his life breaking it clause by clause.

⑦ A good man is so full of one system that there's no room left in him for West Lake.

⑧ He laughed as his daughter died, and his tears took a hundred miles to find a stranger's white dress.

⑨ They honoured a man who gave way. Later the shrine fell, and women dug wild greens in its courtyard.

⑩ Zither, chess, calligraphy and painting end up in the hands of men who never sat an exam. That's where Wang Mian's stars came down.
