Novels, Deep-Read · Marcel Proust · No.7

In Search of Lost Time

À la recherche du temps perdu · Marcel Proust · published 1913–1927

中文 →

On the stairs of a country house, a child stands holding a candle and refuses to go up. There are still guests in the dining room; his mother will not be coming to kiss him goodnight.
So he does something he knows is outrageous: he writes a note and begs the maid to carry it in to the table. No answer comes back. He waits in the dark corridor until the guests have gone, and then throws himself at his mother in front of everyone.
On any other evening his father would have punished him for it. Tonight his father only waves a hand — don't make a scene, go and sit with him.
So she stays, and spends the night beside his little bed. He has won.
And listening to her turn the pages, he cries — because he already dimly understands that what he has just extracted this way is the only way he will ever know how to ask for it.

This page contains complete spoilers — how Albertine dies, how long it takes the narrator to start grieving for his grandmother, what becomes of the Baron de Charlus, and what the raised paving stone tells the narrator at that final afternoon party. Everything worth discussing in three thousand pages sits in the last thirty; without the ending, the book shrinks to the one-line summary about a cake and a memory. If you want to keep the suspense, read the novel first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

A sickly, hypersensitive man who wants to be a writer and cannot write a word spends three thousand pages losing everything he had: his grandmother dies, both of his loves rot away in suspicion, the aristocratic world he worshipped turns out to be full of boring people, and he himself grows old, ill and certain he will never produce anything. Then, at an afternoon party where almost everyone has aged past recognition, his foot catches on two uneven paving stones and his body lurches before his mind can catch up — and he understands that the time he lost is not gone, it simply cannot be retrieved by the act of remembering; there is only one way to get it out, and that is to write it. The three thousand pages you have just read are the book he goes home to write at that moment. Along the way he also sets down the working mechanics of jealousy, the sociology of snobbery, and the situation of homosexual men in Paris around 1900, none of which has dated.

Where it sits

Marcel Proust (1871–1922), Parisian, son of a distinguished public-health physician and of a mother from a wealthy Jewish family. Asthmatic from the age of nine and ill for the rest of his life; as a young man a fixture in the salons, known to everyone worth knowing and generally taken for a rich, well-mannered literary dilettante. From his thirties he withdrew into the cork-lined bedroom, writing at night, sleeping by day, kept going by inhalations and coffee.

Everything in the first half of his career was preparation: an exquisite and inconsequential book of sketches (Les Plaisirs et les Jours, 1896); an abandoned novel, Jean Santeuil, made of almost the same material as the Recherche but written in the third person, which killed it; two translations of Ruskin, which taught him that looking is a discipline you practise; and a critical essay, Contre Sainte-Beuve, aimed at the fashionable method of explaining a book by its author's life. Halfway through, that essay stops being criticism and turns into a conversation with his mother — and the three-thousand-page novel grows out of exactly that digression.

In 1913 the first volume was turned down by several publishers (including the house where Gide was a reader; Gide wrote to apologise the following year) and Proust paid for it himself. The second volume took the Prix Goncourt in 1919. He died of a pulmonary infection in November 1922 with the last three volumes still in notebooks, assembled for print by his brother and his editors; the set was complete in 1927. The book straddles the seam between the nineteenth century and the twentieth: he throws out none of the furniture of Balzac and Flaubert — characters, salons, social climbing — he moves all of it indoors and then dismantles it from inside. Same earthquake as Joyce and Woolf, different method: he does not break the sentence, he lengthens it until it can hold a mistake and its correction at once.

The story

Act one · Combray: a child and two directions

The narrator is never named (twice in the later volumes someone calls him Marcel, and that is all). The first sentence of the book is "For a long time I went to bed early" (opening of vol. 1) — a sleepless man who cannot tell which of his bedrooms he is lying in, so that every room he has ever slept in floats up one after another.

The one that floats up most clearly is Combray, the provincial town of his childhood summers. A magic lantern throws a medieval story onto his bedroom wall, and the villain slides along it wearing the wallpaper as a costume. Downstairs, Aunt Léonie has not left her bed for decades and conducts surveillance on the whole street from her window. In the kitchen is Françoise, whose loyalty upward is a religion and whose cruelty downward is a trade.

Then the cake. Years later, on a winter afternoon, his mother gives him tea and a madeleine. He is empty, planning to write the day off, and the mouthful of soaked crumbs touches his palate — a shudder goes through him and an enormous unaccountable happiness cuts him off from his own troubles. He puts the cup down and tries to work out what this is; the second and third sips are weaker. He cannot find the cause and can only wait — until all of Combray rises out of a small cake, with its church, its streets, his aunt's room and the cup of tea attached. At the time he thinks he has recovered an afternoon. Seed ①

Outside the town there are two directions for a walk, and the family never joins them up. One is "Swann's way", past a hedge of flowering hawthorn where Swann's daughter Gilberte is standing; the first time she sees the narrator she makes a coarse gesture and runs off. The other is "the Guermantes way", along a small river, and at the end of it is the name of an ancient family — for a child, "Guermantes" is not a set of people, it is a violet lantern-slide light. In his mind the two ways are incompatible worlds that will never touch as long as he lives. Seed ②

And one thing he sees without understanding. Passing a house at Montjouvain one evening, he hides at the window and watches Mademoiselle Vinteuil, whose father has just died, with her woman friend: the photograph of that meek music teacher, the local joke, has been set out on the table, and the two women perform every kind of obscenity in front of it and spit on it. The child sees a piece of cruelty he cannot interpret, and goes home to bed. Seed ③

He does manage one real piece of work in this act: riding in the doctor's carriage, he watches the three steeples of Martinville slide apart, overlap and finally line up as the road turns, asks for paper, and writes the first decent prose of his life — then sings out loud like a hen that has laid an egg. Years later, revised, that page really does appear in a newspaper. In three thousand pages it is almost the only thing he finishes.

Act two · Swann in Love: an instruction manual, issued in advance

A novella drops into the middle of the first volume, about events from before the narrator was born: how Swann fell in love with Odette. It is the shrewdest structural decision in the book — you are shown the complete case history first, and then you watch the protagonist repeat it without missing a step.

Charles Swann is Jewish, a collector, a man of exquisite taste, received in the highest houses in Paris. Odette de Crécy is a courtesan who lives off men and has a commonplace mind; at first Swann does not even find her his type. How does he fall in? At the Verdurins' pretentious little circle he hears a sonata for piano and violin, and one small phrase in it opens a hole in him — and he makes that little phrase the national anthem of their affair. Seed ④ Soon he has stopped asking her how she is and asks only who she was with last night.

One evening she is not at the Verdurins', and he takes a cab along the boulevards from restaurant to restaurant, reading every table through the glass. He finds her; nothing happens. But from that night on what he wants is not her, it is intelligence about her: reading a letter she wrote to someone else through the thin envelope, paying for information, interrogating, lying awake over one vague answer. Proust is not writing "love produced jealousy" but the reverse: jealousy is the only fuel this kind of love runs on, and a man loves in proportion to what he cannot verify.

The novella ends with Swann coming out of the illness and thinking, with astonishment, that he ruined years of his life for a woman he did not even like. Seed ⑤ — and then, some volumes later, you learn that he married her, and lives with her and their daughter in a socially demoted style. The manual is for the reader. The patient never reads it.

Act three · Names turning into people: Gilberte, and the girls at the sea

The narrator's first love is Gilberte, met on the Champs-Élysées — the girl from behind the hawthorn. The main business of loving her is being admitted to the Swann house, which is a shrine in his mind. Once he is having tea there every afternoon, it stops being a shrine. The most reliable law in the book is that the object of desire loses its colour the moment you have it, because the colour was never on the object, it was on the distance. He and Gilberte do not quarrel; he designs a strategy of feigned indifference, keeps it up for months, and it comes true.

Then the first summer at Balbec on the Normandy coast, with his grandmother. It is the warmest stretch of the book: the old woman in the next room, three knocks on the wall as an agreed signal for "I am here", terrified he will catch a chill and equally terrified he will be bored, fending off every vulgar acquaintance on his behalf. In an enormously complicated novel she is almost the only uncomplicated person — she loves him, wants nothing back, and attaches no conditions.

On the esplanade there is a band of girls on bicycles, coming along in sportswear like a frieze that has started moving, and it is impossible to tell which is which. Albertine's first appearance is not a person but a blur — which matters, because she never does come into focus. The same summer he meets Saint-Loup (young, aristocratic, handsome, generous, in love with an actress who despises him) and Saint-Loup's uncle, the Baron de Charlus, whose talk turns vicious and tender by turns and whose way of looking at people makes the narrator obscurely uneasy. And there is the painter Elstir's studio, where sea and land invade each other and the harbour houses seem to grow out of the water; the old painter's lesson becomes the method of the whole book — to paint what you see, you first have to forget what you know.

Act four · The Guermantes way: a name taken apart, a person buried

The family moves into a wing of the Guermantes house, and the violet name becomes an actual woman: Oriane, Duchesse de Guermantes, large-nosed, with a drawling rustic wit, manufacturing the cruel bons mots that all Paris will repeat tomorrow. The narrator finally gets invited into the salon, and then spends several hundred pages confirming one thing: these people are quick, cultivated, narrow and utterly unoriginal, and their single genius is the defence of their own precedence. The Dreyfus affair (the wrongful conviction that split France from 1894) is meanwhile reshuffling the salons: whether you are a Dreyfusard suddenly matters more than how old your title is.

The volume ends with a textbook scene. The Duke and Duchess are late for a dinner; Swann arrives looking terrible. She asks casually whether he will travel with them; he says he will not be alive by then, the doctors give him a few months. She hesitates — either she takes it seriously and misses her dinner, or she does not hear it. She does not hear it, produces a weightless reassurance, and heads for the carriage. At which point the Duke notices that her black shoes do not go with her red dress and drags her back to change them. A man announces his own death and it is filed in the gap left by a change of shoes; there is not one villain in the room, and that is worse than a villain.

Then the grandmother dies. The stroke comes on by a public lavatory on the Champs-Élysées, and there follow dozens of pages of slow, specific, merciless dying: doctors contradicting each other, relatives each with a performance to give, the old woman's face retreating day by day towards how she looked as a girl. She dies and the narrator is not very sad. The real grief arrives a year later, in a hotel at Balbec, when he bends to take off his boots, thinks of her, and is knocked flat. Proust calls this "the intermittences of the heart": nobody loves or mourns continuously; a person is a series of selves taking shifts, and they do not know each other.

It is also in these volumes that he looks down from a stairwell window into the courtyard and sees the Baron de Charlus and Jupien the waistcoat-maker circling each other like a bee and an orchid before going indoors. The passage is written as a botany lesson, and it puts a theme that runs through the whole novel on the table: how people who must spend their lives in disguise recognise one another. This is the first large-scale, direct, minutely observed treatment of homosexual life in the European novel — written in a vocabulary that is very hard to accept today, which gets its own entry below.

Then the detonation. At Balbec the narrator is entangled with Albertine and about to shake her off, when she mentions in passing, before the train, that Mademoiselle Vinteuil's friend has always looked after her, like an older sister, almost a mother. Payoff ③ ↑ The window at Montjouvain goes off in his head: the performance a child could not interpret becomes, twenty years later, a shell that lands on him. He no longer wants to be rid of her; he wants her watched. What makes this beautiful is that an image which was pure noise at the time has been kept in storage for a volume and a half, waiting for an idle remark to load it — and the author does not join the wires up for you, he simply lets the narrator start shaking in the railway carriage.

Act five · The Prisoner and The Fugitive: keeping a person indoors

Albertine moves into his flat in Paris. He buys her clothes, gives her money, schedules her movements to the minute; she goes out accompanied and comes back to be debriefed. In the mornings he lies in bed listening to the street cries — the snail seller, the glazier, the old-clothes man, some of the finest music in the book — while working out how to ask her about yesterday.

One important death falls inside the captivity: the writer Bergotte, already ill, goes to an exhibition to check one small patch of yellow wall in a Dutch painting, and collapses on a bench in the gallery. It gives the narrator his first glimpse of a serious question — why would anyone pay with his life for a patch of colour, unless there is another set of books being kept somewhere outside this world.

Then the evening at the Verdurins', where a work nobody has heard is performed: Vinteuil's Septet. The mild provincial music master whose portrait was spat on left behind a heap of very nearly illegible drafts, and the person who sorted, copied out and delivered them to the concert platform, page by page, is that same woman friend. Payoff ④ ↑ In the music the narrator hears a relative of the little phrase, and also the thing he has been trying to prove: every real artist brings with him a world that is his alone, and that world does not live inside his circumstances, so destroying a man is not the same as destroying his country. The beauty is double: a desecrated father is resurrected by the woman who desecrated him, and the first hard evidence in the book that art can recover time comes out of that filthy relationship. The same evening, Madame Verdurin, wanting the violinist Morel to herself, needs a few sentences to make him humiliate Charlus in front of everyone — and the Baron, frozen in the middle of the drawing room, is written for the first time as a pitiable man.

Albertine leaves. Françoise comes in with the news: "Mademoiselle Albertine has gone." (opening of vol. 6) A flat sentence with no adjectives in it, followed by three hundred pages of catastrophe. He sends someone after her, writes to say he doesn't care, writes again to beg her to come back — and then a telegram: she was thrown from a horse against a tree and is dead.

The hardest and best part of the book starts there: she is dead, and now the jealousy really goes to work. He pays people to go where she had been and ask about her and other women; the answers contradict one another and every one of them can hurt him again — the witness he needs to cross-examine can no longer speak, so the case can never be closed. Then forgetting arrives, not by decision but like a water level dropping an inch at a time: one day he notices that thinking of her does not hurt, which is to say the self that loved her has died, and he is not sad about that either — the coldest stroke in the novel. Payoff ⑤ ↑ He has finally become Swann, with one difference: Swann could still say that he ruined years over a woman he did not like, and the narrator cannot say it, because he knows perfectly well that what he loved was never the girl but the hole in himself. Afterwards he goes to Venice with his mother; later still, he learns that Gilberte has married Saint-Loup.

Act six · Time Regained: a party where nobody is recognisable

The story jumps to the war. On nights when the zeppelins come people go out to dinner as usual; Madame Verdurin rides wartime fashions to the top of society at last; yesterday's Dreyfusards are today's patriots. Charlus drifts through the streets like a ghost, pro-German and abused for it; during one air raid the narrator wanders into a male brothel run by Jupien and watches through a partition as the Baron pays to be beaten with chains. The proudest man in the book is buying the experience of being treated as refuse. Saint-Loup is killed at the front, covering his men's retreat.

The narrator then spends years in a sanatorium and comes out an old man who no longer expects anything of himself. He goes to an afternoon party at the Prince de Guermantes's. Stepping aside for a car in the courtyard, he puts one foot on a paving stone slightly higher than its neighbour — his body lurches, and the whole long-lost, unaccountable happiness comes back: Venice, the two uneven stones in the baptistery of St Mark's. Payoff ① ↑ Then, in the little waiting room, a spoon against a plate brings back a line of trees beside a railway, a starched napkin brings back the sea at the window in Balbec, and a copy of François le Champi on the shelf — the very book his mother read at his bedside that night in childhood — brings back the whole of that night.

This time he does not stop at astonishment, as he did with the madeleine. He shuts himself in a room and thinks for dozens of pages until the law comes out: what is precious in memory is not the event but the instant in which one sensation coincides across two times; in that instant the person is outside time, so he need not fear death. And the coincidence cannot be requested, only waited for — and then fixed by writing. He was never short of material; what he lacked was this realisation — that what he has to write is not the things he saw but the optics he saw them with.

Then he opens the door into the drawing room and the most frightening page in the book begins. He assumes the host has thrown a costume party: the guests have powdered their hair, glued on white beards, and walk as though on stilts. It takes him a moment to understand that nobody is in costume, that many years have passed, and that he is wearing the makeup too. Grand duchesses have turned into peasant grandmothers; the arrivistes of thirty years ago are now authorities; the old names sit in corners with nobody talking to them.

Among the guests a girl of sixteen is brought to be introduced: Mademoiselle de Saint-Loup, daughter of Robert and Gilberte — Swann's granddaughter and a Guermantes at once. Payoff ② ↑ The two walks that could never intersect have been joined in this girl. The beauty is not in the coincidence — marriages between old titles and new money were the most ordinary thing of that era — it is that the invisible thing, time, has been turned into a person you can shake hands with: the road was not walked through by anybody, it was walked through by time itself. Looking at her, his first thought is how old he is; his second is that he must start writing immediately, because he does not know how much is left. The last word of the novel is "Time".

Winding the causes down to a single fuse: what lit these three thousand pages is not the madeleine, it is the night at Combray when his mother came upstairs. That night a child discovered that love can be extracted by making someone else anxious, and that adult rules give way in the face of tears. Every love he has afterwards is that night enlarged: with Gilberte it is feigned indifference, with Albertine it escalates into surveillance and interrogation; what he keeps asking for is never a particular person but the confirmation that she will stay tonight. The madeleine is only a receipt delivered decades later, telling him that time wasted this way can still be exchanged for something else — but only for a book, and writing it will cost him whatever life he has left.

Two scenes in slow motion

One · The madeleine: the most quoted passage of the century, and usually misread

The received version is "a cake reminded him of his childhood, how lovely." The text does close to the opposite. Look at the initial conditions: it is cold, he is out of sorts, the day has been mediocre, the tea is a formality — the happiness has to land on someone completely unprepared, or it would be nostalgia. Then the body moves first: the shudder arrives before any explanation, and he does not know why he is happy. Then the least intuitive design decision in the passage: he tries again, and again, and the effect weakens every time. Proust builds a failure mechanism into his own miracle, because he needs to prove the thing is not subject to the will — whatever can be repeated on demand is not it.

Then the sentences. The long ones are not decoration, they are the shape of an action: clause inside clause is the process of groping in the dark and losing the thing each time he thinks he has it, and the answer, when it comes, is not thought out but surfaces on its own. The register throughout is analytic, nearly cold; there is not one word to the effect of "how beautiful". So what the passage describes is not a memory but a discovery that goes unrecognised: he is already holding the key to the whole book, and it will take him two thousand more pages to see that it is a key.

Two · The costume party: giving time a body you can look at

Time has no shape, and writing about it collapses into lyricism at the first opportunity. Proust's solution is to route it through a mistake. He does not write "twenty years had passed and everyone had aged"; he hands you the narrator's perception intact, error included: these people are in costume. So the reader understands what has happened before the narrator does, and that lag is the subject of the entire novel — we never see change happening, we recognise, a moment late, that it happened long ago.

The harder stroke is at the end, where he compares the old to men walking on stilts taller than church spires, terrified of falling at every step: the height of the stilts is the length of the years each one is carrying. One visible image saves him a hundred pages of meditation on death. This scene and the madeleine are a pair — one short-circuits time, the other lays it out flat — and they happen at the same party, which is why those thirty pages are the engine room of the book.

The core device: a machine for making time visible

The famous component is involuntary memory (mémoire involontaire), but on its own it does not run. The machine actually turning over has four parts.

Part one: two narrators. The "I" who tells and the "I" who lived are decades apart, and every sentence has both men in it: the young one making the mistake, the old one who already knows the outcome. Every judgement, every general law, every "at the time I thought" is added by the old one. This is precisely why Jean Santeuil failed — same material, third person, no time lag, and nothing left but a heap of pretty fragments.

Part two: the rules of involuntary memory. The operating conditions are explicit: it can only be triggered by a sensation (a taste, an unevenness underfoot, metal on china), never by an idea; the trigger has to coincide in the body, not merely resemble in content; what it delivers is not the fact but the sense of having been there; and it must be accidental — reach for it and it is gone. The part that holds is accurate observation: cue-driven, embodied recall really is a different substance from what you produce by trying to remember. The part that is literary necessity rather than a scientific result is the step he takes next — that this coincidence proves there is something in us outside time, and therefore that art can defeat death. That step is a belief, not psychology: this is the novel's metaphysics, not a finding about memory. Present-day memory research leans the other way, towards recall as reconstruction rather than retrieval — and his own book is full of memories being rewritten, as when Gilberte tells him, decades on, that the coarse gesture behind the hedge was a pass at him, and the first meeting he had been carrying for a lifetime was not what he remembered at all.

Part three: the law-making voice. He is forever deriving a universal rule from a particular scene — "in love one always…", "the snob invariably…". It is why the book reads half as novel and half as moral treatise, and it is where it is most exposed: one man's case history written up as a law of the species (see the first question below).

Part four: optics. People here always arrive as the wrong image first — a name, a silhouette at a distance, a rumour — and are then corrected repeatedly, with no final version issued. Charlus is an eccentric aristocrat, then an astonishing secret, then an old man being beaten with a chain; Albertine has dozens of faces, because what the narrator sees each time is his own suspicion projected onto her. Writing a person as a stack of incompatible images and writing time as two coinciding instants are the same operation.

What it forces you to think about

Question one

Do you love that person, or the shape of the hole in yourself?

Proust's answer is unambiguous: love is a product of imagination and uncertainty, and the object is nearly interchangeable. The evidence is laid out neatly — while Albertine lives in his flat and can be questioned at any hour, he is bored; the moment she leaves she becomes infinitely desirable; once she is dead she becomes the entire content of his life. If someone's hold on you rises and falls with your ability to verify where they are, then it is plainly not them you love.

Now the other side at full strength: the theory is one anxious man universalising his own pathology, and his own book is the refutation. There is a love in these pages that works nothing like that — the grandmother's: nothing demanded back, no uncertainty required, no jealousy in the fuel line, and the most moving passage in three thousand pages is not any love affair but an old woman knocking three times on a wall. Add Françoise's loyalty and a mother's lifetime of care, and none of it obeys his law. Real love is not "confirming where the other person is", it is staying curious about another person — and this narrator is never once curious about Albertine; he interrogates her and never asks what she thinks. So perhaps the question is not whether love is an illusion, but whether his kind of love was love.

Bring it home: the last time you stared at a read receipt for three minutes, did you want to know whether she was all right, or who she was with? Which way you lean is which side of this argument you are on.

Question two

Does writing your own cruelty down honestly settle the account?

The five hundred pages of The Prisoner are among the least flattering confessions in literature: a man half-imprisons a young woman in his flat, controlling her movements, her money and her friendships, on the grounds that he cannot sleep. The case for the prosecution is hard to answer: Albertine never gets a single page of interiority of her own; what she wants, thinks and fears exists only in the form of the narrator's conjectures. She is not only shut in the flat by the narrator, she is shut inside the book by the author — a double imprisonment, and the second one is Proust's own work.

The defence can also be put at full strength. The book never takes the narrator's side. It disassembles every component of jealousy — the interrogation scripts, the after-the-fact investigations, the control that files itself under concern — so thoroughly that it becomes very hard for any reader to keep describing possessiveness as devotion. It offers no conclusion; it uses up your excuses in advance, which is one of the most practical things literature can do. And if only morally presentable experience may be written, then on the subject of control we will forever have external reports and no testimony from inside, and it is the inside account that changes readers.

The fair summary is probably this: an honest confession has real cognitive value, but it does not produce absolution. It makes the reader clearer; it does not make the author clean. The everyday version of the problem is shared location and the mutual right to read each other's phone — if both parties agree, is that security or a cage? Note the loophole: consent inside a dependent relationship is never clean, because the cost of refusing is usually paid by whoever has less.

Question three

Is a life for a book a good trade?

Proust's answer is the conclusion of the novel: the wasted years were not wasted, they were raw material, and only written experience fully takes place. That answer comes with evidence that is nearly impossible to argue with — he did it, and we are still reading; ill, half a lifetime late, working through the night in a cork-lined room, dead before the set was in print, he nevertheless refined thirty years of social scrap into an object that will not spoil for centuries. He even put the insomnia and the asthma to work: only a man forced to be alive at night would study time as an enemy.

Turn it over, though, and the conclusion is dangerous. It is unfalsifiable — it holds only if you succeed. Applied to the other nine hundred and ninety-nine people, it is a beautiful sentence for postponing a life: I need not live well now, because I am going to write later. Worse is the implication that a life not written down did not really happen. That is consoling to writers and arrogant towards everyone else; the people in this book who do not write — the grandmother, Françoise, his mother — are precisely the ones living most concretely. And remember who paid: he could write all night because he had money, servants and no job, while the people boiling his water and carrying his letters never got a chance to reclaim any time of their own.

Bring it home: that thing you keep not starting — if it is never finished, do the dinners, trips and friends you gave up for it still count as well spent? Proust won the bet, which is why he is quoted as an argument; but winners are never a good statistical sample.

The subject Proust keeps returning to

First, the gap between a name and a thing. "Guermantes" is a violet light to a child and a woman with a talent for wisecracks in the drawing room; the name Balbec holds a Persian-looking church and a raging sea, and turns out to be a vulgar resort hotel. He spent his life on this subtraction: what we love is mostly the name, and the work of living is exchanging names for things at a permanently unfavourable rate.

Second, jealousy as a way of knowing. Jealousy in his hands is not an emotion but bad science: hypotheses, evidence, cross-examination, faked controls — and it must fail, because another person's interior is a sample you cannot obtain. That is also his final verdict on knowing anyone: it cannot be done.

Third, the self is not one thing. "The intermittences of the heart" covers all his characters: a person is a series of selves working shifts, and the one that was in love leaves without notice while the one that grieves clocks off on time. The intuition is already in the failed Jean Santeuil; what was missing was a form to carry it.

Fourth, art is the only place experience fully happens. This line starts in the Ruskin years. By Contre Sainte-Beuve he had found his enemy — criticism that explains a book by the anecdotes of its author's life — and with it his thesis: the self that writes is not the self that goes to parties, and only the deeper one counts. Put those four things into a single machine and you have the three-thousand-page novel; everything he failed to write in the first half of his life was a search for the blueprint.

Criticism and controversy

  • It is too long, and in places it genuinely idles. Salon conversation runs to dozens of pages; the precedence of minor characters can occupy a chapter. The most widely repeated rejection note of the time (attributed to a publisher's reader) ran roughly: I fail to see why a man needs thirty pages to describe turning over in bed before he falls asleep. It survives as a joke, but the problem it names is real: the density is wildly uneven, and the best thirty pages may be one page away from the dullest.
  • The Goncourt of 1919. When the second volume took the prize there was uproar: a rich man of forty-eight writing about girls in salons had beaten Roland Dorgelès's trench novel Les Croix de bois, one year after the war ended. Some of the attack was prejudice about his class and his social manoeuvring; some of it was a question that is not easy to answer — whether that particular year's prize should have gone elsewhere.
  • Class. Almost nobody in this book works, and where the narrator's money comes from is never explained; Françoise is superbly written, but the vantage point stays upstairs. A book that argues over three thousand pages that time is draining out of everyone describes in detail only the time of the leisured.
  • Albertine and the "transposition of sexes". On the evidence of manuscripts, letters and biography — including Proust's relationship with Alfred Agostinelli, his secretary and chauffeur, who died in a plane crash in 1914 — many scholars hold that the model for Albertine was male and was converted into a woman during revision. Proust never said so. Treating it as settled is not rigorous, but it does account for something odd in the text: her behaviour and her situation do not always sit convincingly on a young woman of that period.
  • How homosexuality is written. This is the first time the European novel treated the signals, the cost of disguise and the self-hatred of homosexual men at this length and this level of detail, by an author who was himself homosexual. But the frame he used was the period's pathological one — "inversion", a cursed race, an inherited misfortune. It is simultaneously the earliest sustained depiction and one of the most self-abasing texts on the subject; both things are true at once.
  • How Jews are written. Swann is among the most cultivated people in the book, while the other major Jewish character, Bloch, is a collection of ludicrous mannerisms; and the novel transcribes a great deal of salon antisemitism. Proust's mother came from a Jewish family, and he took the Dreyfusard side, helping to gather signatures for the intellectuals' petition. The book both dissects antisemitism and reproduces the stereotypes of its moment, and there is still no consensus about where the line falls.
  • The last three volumes are posthumous. The Prisoner, The Fugitive and Time Regained were all still being expanded when he died and were prepared for print by family and editors, leaving contradictions that cannot be cleaned up (characters die and reappear). A corrected typescript of The Fugitive, revised by Proust, came to light in 1986 and differs substantially from the received text; scholarly editions since have been re-established on it. In English, the earliest translation replaced the title with a phrase from Shakespeare, Remembrance of Things Past, and only later revisions restored something close to the French. Strictly speaking, the Recherche you are holding may not be the one Proust would have signed off.
  • Titles and translations. The first complete Chinese version, published between 1989 and 1991, was the work of more than a dozen translators, with the unevenness that implies; two later translators each set out to redo the whole thing alone and neither finished. The standard Chinese title means something like "recalling years that flowed away like water" — graceful, but perdu means lost, and wasted, and the final volume is called Time Regained: that loss-and-recovery symmetry is exactly what the familiar title drops.

Ten lines that stay

① The book is not about memory. It is about a man working out what he is for — and it takes him three thousand pages to find that the answer is writing those three thousand pages.

② What the madeleine actually says: the most important things cannot be fetched by trying, only waited for; so the only preparation available is not to be busy when one arrives.

③ The fuse is not the cake. It is the night his mother came upstairs: a child learns to trade anxiety for love, and every love afterwards is that night enlarged.

④ Swann in Love is placed at the front because it is the manual, issued in advance. The patient never reads the manual.

⑤ Jealousy is not loving too much, it is needing to verify and being unable to. He is still paying informants after she is dead, because that case can never be closed.

⑥ A man announces his own death and it is filed in the gap left by changing a pair of shoes — with no villain in the room, which is colder than a villain.

⑦ Three knocks on a wall is the one love in the book that runs without uncertainty as fuel; he wrote about desire all his life and saved his best writing for the kind that asks nothing back.

⑧ The intermittences of the heart: you do not love anyone continuously, you are a series of selves on shifts, and the one that loved will leave without notice.

⑨ The costume-party page gives time a body: old men on invisible stilts, the height of the stilts being the years each of them is carrying.

⑩ The two walks that could never meet are joined at the end by a girl of sixteen — the road was not walked through by anybody, it was walked through by time itself.
