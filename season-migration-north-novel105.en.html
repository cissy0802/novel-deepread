Novels, Deep-Read · Tayeb Salih · No.105

Season of Migration to the North

Tayeb Salih · 1966 · novel · written in Arabic (Mawsim al-Hijra ila al-Shamal)

中文 →

A village at a bend of the Nile, a summer night. A handful of men sit drinking spirit distilled from dates and talking about the harvest, the water pump, whose daughter is marrying whom. The stranger among them hardly says a word. He has lived here five years: he farms, he married a local girl, he sits on the agricultural scheme's committee, and everyone agrees he is a decent, quiet man.
Late in the night, drunk, he tips his head back and begins to recite a poem — in English, in an accent fit for a London lecture hall.
The others laugh it off as drunken babble. Only one young man, just home from a doctorate in England, sits with his glass in his hand and feels his spine go cold.

This piece contains complete spoilers: what the stranger did in London, whom he killed, how he vanished from the village; how the widow he left behind was forced to remarry and what she did on her wedding night; and what was in the room he kept locked for years. The book's force lives in the look back from the last page.

In one sentence

A young Sudanese man returns from his studies in England to his village on the Nile and discovers that the silent outsider in their midst made the same journey a generation earlier — an economist who "conquered" Englishwomen in London with lies and his body, killed his wife and served seven years; the young man thinks he is listening to someone else's story, until he unlocks the forbidden room and finds a mirror.

Coordinates

Tayeb Salih (1929–2009) grew up in a farming village on the Nile in northern Sudan, studied in Khartoum, then moved to Britain, where he spent years at the BBC's Arabic Service before working for UNESCO and other bodies; he lived most of his life abroad. He wrote very little, and almost all of it is set in one invented village, Wad Hamid. This novel first appeared in a Beirut journal in 1966; Denys Johnson-Davies's English translation came out in 1969.

It occupies a foundational slot in postcolonial fiction — novels about how the colonized deal with what the colonizer leaves behind. In 2001 the Arab Literary Academy in Damascus named it the most important Arabic novel of the twentieth century. It is routinely read alongside Heart of Darkness, which this site has covered: Conrad sends a white man south into the heart of Africa; Salih sends an African north into the heart of Europe.

The story

The novel is narrated by an "I" who is never named. The stranger's life does not arrive in one piece: some of it he confesses himself one night, some the narrator later assembles from other people, from recollections of a trial, and finally from the locked room. Below it is rearranged by cause and effect, with notes on when each piece surfaces.

Act one · Homecoming: seven years and a palm tree

The narrator has been away seven years, writing a doctorate in England on an obscure English poet. On his first morning back in Wad Hamid he lies in the room he slept in as a boy, listening to the doves, looking at the palm tree in the courtyard. foreshadowing ① He tells himself that he is not a feather blown about by the wind but something like that tree — rooted, with an origin and a purpose. Europe was an interlude; now the story is back on its proper track.

Everything in the village reassures him. His grandfather is still alive, very old, thin as a dry stick, praying, keeping accounts, telling stories about landlords and famines. The old man's friends come by every day: Wad Rayyes, past seventy, who has married a string of wives and is still scheming about the next; Bakri, who argues with everyone; and Bint Majzoub, an old woman married several times over, who smokes, drinks, and tells filthier stories louder than any of the men. His childhood friend Mahjoub stayed on the land and has become a leading man on the scheme's committee; he could not care less about English poetry and cares a great deal about pumps and schools.

One face is missing from the welcome. A middle-aged man stands at the edge of the crowd and does not come forward. The narrator asks and is told: that is Mustafa Sa'eed, who came from somewhere near Khartoum five years ago, bought land, married Mahmoud's daughter Hosna, had two sons. Says little, steady with his hands, serves on the committee, well liked. Where he came from and what he did before, nobody can say.

The narrator senses that the man is not of the village without being able to say why. Then comes the summer night and the English poem — the opening scene. The next day the narrator corners him: who are you? Mustafa plays dumb, then gives way and tells him to come to the house that evening.

Act two · A résumé of lies: Khartoum to London

That night Mustafa tells the first half of his life, after making the narrator swear to repeat none of it.

He was born in Khartoum in 1898 — the very year British guns at Omdurman destroyed the Mahdist state and folded Sudan into Anglo-Egyptian rule. His father died before he was born. His mother, who had no other family, treated him with a cool politeness, as if from behind a mask. In those days Sudanese families hid their sons so the colonial government could not put them in its new schools; the boy saw a man in uniform in the street, followed him of his own accord, and enrolled. Nobody delivered him to the colonizer's education; a child nobody held back walked into it.

His mind was frighteningly fast, like a cold blade. His teachers marveled and sent him to secondary school in Cairo. When he left, his mother did not cry; she gave him some money and, in effect, told him his life was his own affair. In Cairo he lodged with an English couple, the Robinsons; Mrs. Robinson mothered him, and he felt, for the first time, desire for a woman. At fifteen a scholarship took him to London — reportedly the first Sudanese sent abroad this way. He read economics, was lecturing at a London university while still in his twenties, wrote books on the economics of colonialism, moved through progressive salons and was shown off as the hope of Africa.

What he really worked at was something else. He fitted out a bedroom as an Englishwoman's dream of the Orient: sandalwood, incense, Persian rugs, walls of mirrors. He told the women about elephants and lions walking the streets of Khartoum, about desert tribes and sorcery — the stranger the better; whatever Africa they wanted, he became that African. He called the bedroom a den of lies and called himself an invader: he had come to Europe, as he saw it, as a conqueror, collecting with women's bodies a debt the empire owed. Ann Hammond, Sheila Greenwood — several of the women he was with later killed themselves.

There he stops, makes the narrator swear secrecy once more, and sees him out. A man who went north has told his story to the one listener who has just come back from the north himself.

Act three · Jean Morris: the hunter becomes the hunted

The part Mustafa does not tell that night the narrator pieces together only later.

There was one woman his act did not work on. Jean Morris looked at him without curiosity, only contempt. He pursued her for three years; she mocked him, refused him, now and then left him a scrap of hope. When he finally married her, the marriage was a slow war. In front of him she smashed the precious things he collected, tore up a rare Arabic manuscript, ruined a prayer rug; she saw other men and made sure he knew. The game he had used to hunt others was now being played on him.

One night, in their bed, foreshadowing ② he killed her with a knife. As he later remembered it, she did not resist; she seemed almost to welcome it, to be asking him to come with her. It is the first time in the book that someone dies on a marriage bed by a knife — and at this point it reads like a crime of passion of a kind Western readers know well, with a disturbing erotic charge.

The trial is at the Old Bailey. His defense is led by his old teacher, an eminent professor, who tells the jury that the man in the dock is a genius warped by colonialism and that the women's deaths cannot all be laid at his door. Mustafa sits in the dock wanting only to stand up and shout that this man is lying — that he himself is the lie, that he is no Othello, that Othello was a lie, that they should sentence him to death. He shouts nothing. The jury spares him the gallows; he gets seven years.

After prison he drifts across Europe and Asia for years, then disappears, returns to Sudan, settles in a Nile village where no one knows him, marries a local woman and becomes a silent farmer.

Act four · A flood, a letter and a key

After that night Mustafa goes on farming and attending meetings as if nothing had been said. Not long afterward the Nile floods; he goes out to his fields and never comes back. The body is never found. The village says he drowned; the narrator suspects he walked into the river.

He has left a letter appointing the narrator guardian of his two sons, asking him to look after his wife and to spare the boys the itch to leave. With it comes a key. foreshadowing ③ In Mustafa's courtyard stands a small room of red brick with green windows, always locked; no one knows what is in it. Now the key is in the narrator's hand, and still the room stays shut — he does not open it, as if afraid of what is waiting behind the door.

He goes back to Khartoum to a junior post in the Ministry of Education. The capital after independence is full of new elites holding conferences, giving speeches, flying abroad on study tours, all talking about Africa's future. He thinks about Mustafa more and more; at those respectable gatherings every speech he hears starts to sound like another carefully furnished lie.

Each time he goes home he visits Hosna. She is no longer an ordinary village wife: she speaks directly, and there is something in her look that belongs elsewhere — years with Mustafa have changed her too. The narrator finds himself drawn to her and will not admit it. Then the news: Wad Rayyes wants to marry her. He is past seventy and wants her youth and her looks; her family think a widow lucky to be asked. The narrator knows that if he proposed himself, the match would almost certainly be off. Hosna tells him to his face: foreshadowing ④ if they force her to marry the old man, she will kill him and then herself.

He talks it over with Mahjoub, whose view is that this is village business: her father and brothers have agreed, and that settles it; a woman doesn't get to choose. The narrator neither proposes nor intervenes. He tells himself it is not his affair, that he is an outsider now, and returns to Khartoum.

Act five · The wedding night

The wedding takes place. Before long the news reaches Khartoum.

He hurries back. No one wants to talk; in the end it is Bint Majzoub who tells him. For nights after the wedding Hosna would not let the old man touch her. Then one night terrible screams came from the house. When people broke in, Wad Rayyes had been stabbed again and again, and Hosna was dead too — the knife had finished in her own body. The village buried them both that same night, with no wailing and no wake, and agreed never to speak of it.

payoff ④ ↑ She had said it in advance, and she had said it to the one person who might have saved her. At the time it sounded like the wild talk of a cornered woman; nobody took it seriously, the narrator included. Now it has come true to the letter. By having her say it to him rather than to anyone else, Salih puts part of this killing on the narrator's account — and all his later fury is fury at himself.

payoff ② ↑ Look back now at the bed in London and the two deaths line up: a marriage bed, a knife, a woman dead. But in Wad Hamid the knife is in the woman's hand. In London, Mustafa turned colonial violence into a man's revenge on women; at home, a woman traded away by "tradition" uses the same weapon to refuse being traded. The move is beautiful because it needs no speech at all. Salih simply puts two beds in one book and lets the reader see that the South is no more innocent than the North.

For the first time the narrator and Mahjoub truly clash: to Mahjoub she committed a disgrace the village had never known; to the narrator, the whole village drove her to it. Two men who grew up together discover there is a whole river between them.

Act six · The room and the river

That night the narrator finally takes out the key and opens the red-brick room.

payoff ③ ↑ It is pitch dark. He strikes a match and a face glares at him out of the blackness; he thinks it is Mustafa — then sees it is a mirror on the wall, and the face is his own. By matchlight the room assembles itself: an English fireplace, a few armchairs, walls lined floor to ceiling with English books — economics, history, poetry, not one book in Arabic — and on the walls photographs and portraits, Jean Morris among them. In a mud-brick village on the Nile, Mustafa had built an exact copy of an Englishman's study. The room no one could see into held the half of him that never came home. On the table lies a notebook titled My Life Story. Its dedication is addressed, roughly, to those who see with one eye, speak with one tongue, and see the world as either black or white, either East or West. The pages after it are blank.

He sits there almost till dawn, fitting the last pieces of Mustafa's story together and fitting himself in: his years of not acting, the feeling for Hosna he never admitted, his silence in Khartoum. The distance between him and this man is much smaller than he had believed. He considers burning the room down. Then he walks out, toward the river.

In the half-light he strips and wades in and swims for the northern bank. Halfway across his strength gives out; the current drags at him; he floats in mid-river, as far from the south bank as from the north, close to going under. payoff ① ↑ On his first morning home he said he was like the palm tree in the courtyard, rooted and from somewhere. Now he lies on water that belongs to neither South nor North, and nothing holds him in place — that line about roots turns out to have been the consolation of a man just off the boat. What makes the echo work is that Salih explains nothing; he simply moves the man from a tree to the middle of a river.

At the moment of going under he decides: he wants to live. Not because he has found a meaning, but because there are a few people he loves and duties he owes; if he cannot forgive, he will try to forget. With his last strength he kicks upward and, like a comic actor on a stage, shouts across the empty river: Help! Help!

The novel ends on that cry. Whether anyone comes, which bank he reaches, the book does not say.

If you want the fuse, it is not Jean Morris, and not the knife. The fuse is the boy in 1898 whom nobody held back, following a man in uniform into school. That step honed a Sudanese child's mind into a cold blade pointed only at Europe. He used it for revenge in London, he killed with it, and back home he could not pull it out, so he locked it into an English study and sank it in the Nile. Hosna's death is its aftershock, and so is the narrator's cry in mid-river — the next generation takes the same key and in the end does not walk into the room to become a copy, but into the river, and shouts to stay alive.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · The drunken English poem: a man gives himself away in someone else's language

Replay it: a room of farmers speaking village Arabic, date spirit, an oil lamp, talk of the harvest. The quiet stranger suddenly recites English verse, flawlessly, absorbed — then goes back to being himself as if nothing happened. The others laugh at the drunk; only the narrator understands.

Why does it work? First, Salih packs a man's entire secret into an accent. Without a line of exposition we know this farmer has lived long and deep in another world: you can forge an identity, but not the language that surfaces when you are drunk. Second, the point of view is ruthless — there is exactly one person in the room who can understand, and he has just come back from that same world. The secret is not exposed; it is recognized, like two exiles catching each other's eye in a crowd. Third, it sets the rhythm of the whole book: truth only leaks out when control slips — drink, the flood, the cry swallowed in the dock, the screams on the wedding night are all the same kind of leak.

Two · The dock: "I am no Othello"

Replay it: the Old Bailey, wigs and gowns, the jury. Defense counsel — his own mentor — makes a stirring speech: the accused is a product of colonialism, a tragic figure caught between two civilizations. People in the gallery are moved. The accused sits silent, one sentence turning over in his head: I am a liar; sentence me to death.

Why does it work? Because in this scene the speakers and the truth stand on opposite sides. The defense saves him in law and erases him in meaning: it pushes a man who made choices and demands the consequences back into the role of victim — the very script he performed for Englishwomen in his bedroom for years. Salih lets the English complete one last act of colonization through sympathy: you are not even entitled to your own crime. The name Othello is exactly right. Shakespeare's Moorish general, who kills his white wife out of jealousy, is Europe's prototype of the passionate dark-skinned man; what Mustafa rejects is not the charge but the prototype. And yet he never says it aloud. Silence keeps him alive — and casts him, once more, in a role someone else wrote.

Three · Mid-river: a cry for help like a comic actor's

Replay it: almost dawn, cold water, both banks far away. He no longer swims north and cannot get back south; his body is sinking. He thinks: I want to live. And he shouts for help with all he has, like a comedian on a stage.

Why does it work? First, it is an ending built to withhold an ending: the reader is left exactly where the narrator is, in the middle of the river, not knowing the next second. Second, at the most serious moment in the book Salih drops in the image of a comic actor, and the self-mockery cuts the tragedy off. Mustafa's life was a tragedy that demanded a death sentence; the narrator refuses to play it again and would rather play a clumsy, undignified comedy. Third, the cry is the exact inverse of Mustafa's end: one man sank into the flood in silence; the other, in the water, says out loud for the first time what he wants. The narrator has spent the whole book watching, recording, holding back. This last act is the only time he acts on his own behalf.

The book's core devices

This is not a book built on an invented world. Its core devices are two: the double, and the writing back.

First, the narrator and the man he tells about are two versions of one person. Literature calls this the double, or doppelgänger: one character is the shadow, or the alternative fate, of another. The rule here is that both walk the same road — a bright southern boy, the colonizer's school, the North, seven years, the return — and differ only in how far they go. The logic runs: (1) Mustafa takes the journey north to its extreme, turns himself entirely into Europe's fantasy of an African, and destroys himself in revenge; (2) the narrator believes he merely went to earn a degree and came back unmarked; (3) Mustafa's story is a mirror, and the longer the narrator listens the more he recognizes himself — until, in the room, the mirror becomes literal; (4) at the end he must choose whether to repeat Mustafa (lock himself into the study, sink into the river) or find another way. Steps (1) to (3) are solid; every detail Salih plants pushes the two men together. Step (4) is the one to handle with care: whether that cry for help counts as another way is a question the book deliberately leaves open.

Second, it retells Europe's stories about Africa in reverse. "Writing back" — the colonized reworking the colonizer's classics as a reply — works on three levels here. In Heart of Darkness a white man goes up a river south and finds his darkness in the African interior; here an African goes north and finds it in a London bedroom. In Othello a dark-skinned general kills his white wife from jealousy and it is staged as a tragedy of passion; here the killer rejects that script in the dock, if only inside his head. And Europe's Orient of spices, sorcery and sex is laid out by Mustafa in his bedroom as a weapon and turned on Europeans themselves. Edward Said, in Culture and Imperialism (1993), reads it as a key example of this kind of return voyage.

To be honest about which link is there for the fiction: "avenging colonialism by conquering women's bodies" is an allegorical equation, not a finding of sociology. It works because it turns violence between nations into something visible between two people; it also reduces the Englishwomen to a single function — conquered territory — and gives Mustafa a charm that verges on the supernatural. Salih seems to know it. He has Jean Morris refuse to play the territory and Hosna refuse to be traded, which amounts to dismantling his own equation inside the book.

What it forces you to think about

One · The dock: is he a victim of colonialism, or a man answerable for several deaths?

The strongest case for "victim": a man born in the year of conquest, pulled out of his family as a boy, trained into a mind built to understand Europe, displayed by Europe as the hope of Africa and consumed by European women as an exotic — the distortion in him is not his invention. If we look only at the moment he holds the knife, we shrink a century of structural violence into one man's moral failing, which is precisely what suits the structure. Understanding causes is not excusing; it is refusing to be lazy.

The strongest case for "answerable": others went to the same schools and the same North without building a den of lies or driving women to their deaths. Calling him a victim amounts to saying a colonized man cannot choose — more contemptuous of him than the colonizers were. Above all, Mustafa himself rejects the defense; he asks to be hanged. Respecting a person includes respecting his claim to his own guilt.

Where does the author stand? He lets the defense succeed and lets the accused think "liar," sparing neither side; but he gives the sharpest line to the accused. Bring it home: when a man who was beaten as a child beats his own children, and we say "intergenerational trauma," are we helping him understand himself — or helping him skip the apology?

Two · Hosna: should an "outsider" interfere with the rules of home?

The strongest case for staying out: the narrator is half a stranger now. Coming back with European eyes to lecture the elders on their marriage customs is exactly what the colonizers did — appointing themselves the civilized ones and deciding for others what a good life is. The village has its order; a widow's remarriage has its economic logic. Would a man who comes home only for the holidays, marrying her out of passing pity, be anything but another way of controlling her? And no one could have known her words would come true.

The strongest case for stepping in: this is not an abstraction called "culture"; it is a particular person asking him to his face for help and naming the consequence. He had the means — a marriage contract would do — and he was the guardian her dead husband appointed. Respect for custom cannot become an alibi for doing nothing: when a rule requires someone to die, "respect" is complicity. He stayed out not from reverence for the village but from fear of trouble, of his own feelings, of gossip.

The author does not let him off: he has the narrator hear that sentence and then go half mad afterward, which shows Salih knows whose account the debt is on. But he also puts Mahjoub, a good man, on the side of the rules, which shows he did not write the village as the villain. Bring it home: a relative is being pushed into something that will plainly wreck her life; you live far away, and if you speak up you'll be told the city has gone to your head. Do you say it?

Three · Mid-river: is a cry for help between two banks a defeat or a beginning?

The strongest case for defeat: he reaches neither the north bank nor the south; he does not burn the room; he finds no meaning at all and decides to live only because there are "a few people he loves." It is a demotion — an admission that he can be neither Mustafa nor Mahjoub and can only thrash about clumsily in between. A novel about identity ends by handing him none. He himself says the cry sounds like a comic actor's.

The strongest case for a beginning: everyone in the book is driven by a must. Mustafa must take revenge; the village must keep its rules; Hosna can refuse only by dying. Only the narrator, at the end, sets down the question of which side he must belong to, and for the first time asks for something for himself: to live. Choosing no bank is still a choice — and the only one in the book that requires neither killing nor dying. The comic actor is not self-contempt; it is a man finally declining to cast himself as a tragic hero.

Salih leaves it open, but the cry is the only imperative in the book, and its weight leans a little toward beginning. Bring it home: anyone who has lived long enough between two cultures, two cities, two careers, will one night ask where they really belong. Maybe the real question is whether you have to belong somewhere before you can start living.

Motifs: what Salih keeps writing about

Salih spent his career on one village. In an early story, "The Doum Tree of Wad Hamid" (the doum is a kind of palm), the government wants to put a steamer stop and other new works where a sacred old tree stands, and the villagers will not have it — modernity arrives from outside, and what does the village catch it with? was his first question. The novella The Wedding of Zein (1962) is this book's mirror image: Zein, the village's holy fool, ends up marrying the most beautiful girl in Wad Hamid, and the whole community is reconciled at a wedding — very nearly a comedy. In the same village, in Season of Migration to the North, the wedding becomes a murder. The later Bandarshah (in two parts, 1971 and 1976) is about power and time across grandfathers, fathers and grandsons.

Two obsessions run through all of it. One is the returnee — the person who went away to study and comes back carrying another world: how the village sees him, how he sees the village. The other is time stacked in one place — a grandfather's memory, the colonial years, the new bureaucrats of independence, all pressed onto the same riverbank. This book is where he drove the first obsession to its darkest point; he was, after all, the one who went north and never really came back.

Controversy and criticism

Sex and censorship: the novel's sex and violence are explicit, and it drew strong objections from conservative Arab readers; it was at one point banned in Sudan itself. Defenders argue that this explicitness is exactly what makes the power in colonial relations visible.

Are the women just parts of an allegory? Critics point out that the Englishwomen have almost no inner life, existing only as "conquered Europe," their suicides dispatched in a line or two; Jean Morris, written as a near-mythic destroyer, is flattened in another way. Many readers find Hosna the strongest character in the book, yet her resistance can be completed only by murder and suicide, and she reaches us almost entirely through other people's reports, with little voice of her own. Some critics argue the freest woman in the novel is old Bint Majzoub — who gets far too little space.

Mustafa's charm is the stuff of legend: that one man's handful of lies could send a series of women to their deaths strikes some readers as allegory overpowering credibility; and the narrator's motives are kept deliberately murky, so that readers often cannot tell what he is hiding.

Is writing back another kind of dependence? Some argue that a book built point by point against Heart of Darkness and Othello still treats the European canon as its origin of coordinates, and that much of its prestige comes from the English translation's circulation in Western universities. None of this denies its standing, but it belongs on the record.

Crossovers

  • Sociology · Du Bois's "double consciousness": in The Souls of Black Folk (1903), W. E. B. Du Bois describes the despised always measuring themselves through others' eyes. Mustafa weaponizes that gaze, deliberately playing the African the English want to see. Unlike Du Bois's case, his split is not only endured but performed.
  • Sociology · Goffman's dramaturgy: in The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (1956), Erving Goffman likens social life to a stage with a front region and a back region. The London bedroom is a meticulously dressed front stage; the locked English study in the village is his backstage — except that this backstage is itself another set. Unlike Goffman's everyday performances, Mustafa's is an act of revenge.
  • Cross-cultural psychology · the W-curve and reverse culture shock: John and Jeanne Gullahorn (1963) found that returning students often go through a second adjustment slump, when home itself turns strange. The narrator's contentment on his first day back is the honeymoon before the slump. Unlike most returnees in that research, who climb back out, he sinks to the river.
  • Anthropology · Turner's liminality: Victor Turner called the in-between phase of a rite of passage — no longer the old self, not yet the new — liminality. The narrator in mid-river hangs in exactly that state, neither South nor North. Unlike ritual liminality, which a community holds and brings to a close, his has no one presiding; he is alone in the water.
