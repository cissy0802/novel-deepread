Novels, Deep-Read · Hermann Hesse · No.102

Siddhartha

Siddhartha: An Indian Poem · Hermann Hesse · 1922 · two parts, twelve chapters

中文 →

The father wakes once in the night and looks through the small window. His son is still standing in the room, arms folded, not moving.
An hour later the moon has moved on. The son is still standing. Another hour; the sky is full of stars. Still standing.
Near dawn the old man goes in and sees that the young man's legs are trembling, and that there is not a trace of surrender in his face.
At last he says: go.

This piece contains complete spoilers: why Siddhartha refuses to stay with the Buddha, how a Brahmin's son ends up a gambler and a moneylender, how a bird in a golden cage dies and then flies away, what he almost does by the river, how his own son runs from him, and what Govinda sees when he finally kisses his old friend's forehead. Without the last chapter there is no way to talk about the book's real question: whether awakening can be taught.

In one sentence

A young Brahmin leaves home, starves with ascetics, meets the Buddha, sinks into wealth and pleasure, comes to the edge of suicide, and spends the rest of his life as a ferryman on a river. He turns the Buddha down to his face in order to reach the place where the Buddha stands; and when he gets there, he can no longer say how he did it.

Coordinates

Hermann Hesse (1877–1962) was born in Calw, a small Swabian town in southern Germany, into a family of missionaries: his maternal grandfather and both his parents had worked in India. He traveled to Ceylon and Southeast Asia in 1911 but never set foot in India itself. He wrote the first part of Siddhartha in 1919–20, just after the First World War, then stalled for well over a year; he later explained that the book's hero was about to go through something he himself had not yet been through. It came out in 1922, subtitled "an Indian poem."

It sits between Demian (1919) and Steppenwolf (1927), and it is the twentieth century's best-known Western novel about seeking in the East, and the one most often accused of wishful thinking. It is under two hundred pages, its sentences are as plain as scripture, and all the difficulty lives between them.

The story

Act One · The Brahmin's son: loved by everyone, at peace with nothing

Siddhartha is the son of a Brahmin, handsome and quick. His father is proud of him, his mother is glad just to watch him walk, and the young women of the town turn to look when he passes. The one who loves him most is his friend Govinda, who doesn't much want to become a Brahmin. What he wants is to be Siddhartha's shadow.

Siddhartha himself is not happy. He knows the Vedas and the Upanishads, the ancient texts that describe the true inner self, Atman, as one with Brahman, the ground of everything; find it, and you are free. But he keeps asking: who has actually found it? His father is the most learned man he knows, and he bathes, sacrifices and studies every day, and is still not at peace. He has to be washed clean again each morning. The more knowledge Siddhartha piles up, the thirstier he gets.

One day three Samanas pass through town: wandering ascetics who live in the forest, fast and beg, so thin they are nearly bone. That evening Siddhartha asks his father's leave to join them. His father refuses and goes to bed.

Siddhartha doesn't argue, and he doesn't leave. He stands in his father's room with his arms folded and stays there all night. His father wakes every hour and sees him still standing, and his anger turns to unease, and his unease to grief. By morning he understands that his son's heart has already gone. Setup ①He lets him go with a single request: if you find bliss in the forest, come back and teach it to me; if you find disappointment, come back too, and we will make offerings to the gods together again. Siddhartha walks out on legs gone stiff from standing. Govinda follows. On first reading it feels simply like a young man's stubbornness winning the day.

Act Two · The Samanas and the Buddha: the best of teachers, and the student who won't stay

He lives with the Samanas for about three years. He learns to go without food, to endure sun and cold rain, to slow his breath almost to nothing. He even learns to send his self out of his body and into a heron, into the rotting carcass of a jackal. But every time, he has to come back into the same self he cannot shake.

He says something blunt to Govinda: what we learn here, an ox-driver gets from a couple of bowls of rice wine in a tavern. Both are ways of escaping yourself for a while, and both end with you coming back. Asceticism isn't liberation. It is a more respectable form of anesthetic.

Then a name reaches them: Gotama. A man, people say, has attained awakening; they call him the Buddha. Siddhartha decides to go and see. The old leader of the Samanas is furious, and Siddhartha uses a little of what asceticism taught him, fixing the old man with his gaze until he can't speak and can only bow and bless them.

They find the Buddha in the Jetavana grove outside the city of Savatthi. Siddhartha recognizes him at once: a monk in a yellow robe, carrying his alms bowl, walking quietly, with an inward smile on a face that shows neither sorrow nor joy. Nothing about him is looking for anything. What moves Siddhartha is not what the Buddha says but how his hand hangs at his side, how he puts down his foot; even the movement of his fingers seems to speak the truth.

That evening they hear him teach: suffering, the origin of suffering, the path that ends it. When he finishes, Govinda kneels and asks to be admitted to the order. Siddhartha doesn't move. Two friends who have never been apart part that night.

Early the next morning Siddhartha comes upon the Buddha walking alone and finds the nerve to speak. Your teaching is flawless, he says: it shows the world as a single chain of causes without a gap. Yet liberation itself steps outside that chain; it is the one crack in it. And more important: you found what you found by your own seeking, your own thinking, your own sitting under the tree. You didn't get it from anyone's teaching. So he cannot stay. Not because he doubts the Buddha, but because he believes that awakening cannot be handed to another person by means of a doctrine.

The Buddha doesn't argue. He only warns him, calmly: you are clever, young Samana; beware of too much cleverness. Then he walks on. Setup ②Siddhartha watches him go and thinks: I would like to look at people like that, smile like that, sit and walk like that, so free, so serene. There will never be another man before whom I lower my eyes. And: the Buddha has taken my friend from me, and given me back myself.

Act Three · Awakening, and Kamala: it turns out the world has colors

Coming out of the grove, he stops, and sees for the first time what he has been doing all these years: trying to know the self by fleeing it without pause. He has treated the visible world as illusion. Now he looks up. The sky is blue, the river is green, birds are calling in the trees. All of this was always there; he never looked at it. He resolves to learn from no one any more, only from himself. Then a chill: he is no longer a Brahmin, no longer a Samana. He is entirely alone. And then he walks on, faster than before.

That night he sleeps in a ferryman's hut by a river. In the morning the ferryman poles him across, and Siddhartha admits he has nothing to pay with. It doesn't matter, says the ferryman. Setup ③The river has taught me that everything comes back. You will come back too, Samana. Siddhartha takes it as politeness, thanks him, and heads for the city. The ferryman isn't even named. The reader forgets him almost at once.

Outside the city lies a pleasure garden, and on the road he sees a sedan chair carried past. Inside sits its owner, Kamala, the most famous courtesan in town: a clever face, and a mouth as red as a freshly split fig. He decides she will teach him love. He shaves and goes to her still in his Samana's rags. Kamala laughs. If I'm to teach you, you'll need clothes, shoes, money and presents for me. What can a Samana do?

I can think, he tells her. I can wait. I can fast. Kamala sends him to the city's great merchant, Kamaswami. When the merchant asks what fasting is good for, Siddhartha's answer is steady: a man who can't fast has to take whatever work hunger drives him to; a man who can fast can wait for the right thing. Asked to write something, he puts down: writing is good, thinking is better; cleverness is good, patience is better. He is hired.

Act Four · Samsara: a man slowly becomes what he looked down on

He picks up business fast and never takes it seriously. Sent to a village to buy up a rice harvest, he finds it already sold, stays anyway, stands the farmers drinks and hands the children coins, and comes home cheerful while Kamaswami fumes. For him trade is a game; but the people of the city, whom he privately calls "child people," really do weep over money and pride and quarrel over trifles. Between him and them there is a pane of glass.

Kamala teaches him another discipline: how to give and how to take so that pleasure is never perfunctory. One night she tells him: there is a quiet place inside you that you can withdraw into at any time; but you don't love anyone. He admits it. Nor do you, he says, or how could you practice love as a craft? Perhaps people like us cannot love. The child people can; that is their secret.

The glass thins so slowly he never notices. Year by year he learns dice and wine, learns to look hard at debtors, learns to rage over a loss. The greed and fear he once despised seep into his blood, and the inner voice that used to speak so clearly grows quieter and finally stops.

Once Kamala asks him to describe again how Gotama walked. When he's done she sighs: one day, when she's old, she will give this garden to the Buddha. Siddhartha notices a gray hair at her temple.

Setup ④Kamala keeps a rare songbird in a golden cage; it sings every morning. One night, coming home from the gaming table, Siddhartha dreams that the bird has fallen silent. He goes to the cage and finds it lying dead. He takes it out, weighs it in his hand, and tosses it into the street. In that instant he feels he has thrown away, along with the bird, everything that had any worth or meaning. He wakes up sick with disgust. That day he walks out of the garden and out of the city and never comes back. Kamaswami has people search for him for a long time.

When the news reaches Kamala, she isn't surprised. Payoff ④ ↑She goes to the golden cage, opens its door, takes the bird out and lets it fly away. In his dream Siddhartha threw the bird out as a corpse; awake, Kamala releases the same bird as a living thing. One image, two endings in two pairs of hands, and only now does the reader see whose bird the dream was about. She closes her garden to visitors. Some time later she finds she is pregnant.

Act Five · The river: hearing Om at the last moment

He walks through the forest for a day and a night and comes to a great river. A coconut palm leans out over the water; he wraps his arms around its trunk and looks down. There is nothing left of him, he feels, and he wants only for the water to swallow him. He closes his eyes and begins to lean forward.

At that moment a sound rises from some remote corner of his soul, the sacred syllable a Brahmin speaks at the beginning and end of every prayer: Om, which the book glosses roughly as "the perfect," "the complete." He has said it tens of thousands of times since childhood and never understood it. Now, about to drown himself, he hears it for the first time. He collapses under the palm and falls into a deep sleep.

When he wakes he feels as if he has slept for years, or has just been born. A monk in a yellow robe with a shaved head is sitting across from him, keeping watch. It is Govinda. Setup ⑤Govinda doesn't recognize him (the man in front of him wears rich clothes and has scented oil in his hair) and says only that he was passing, saw someone asleep in a forest full of snakes, and sat down to guard him. Siddhartha says his name. They talk a little; Govinda has a pilgrimage to continue and goes on his way.

Sitting by the river, Siddhartha looks back over his life and can't help laughing. He left home in search of wisdom and became a fool. He had to commit every folly himself, had to reach the point of wanting to die, in order to hear that word again. The clever, greedy Siddhartha who thought himself above everyone has died under that tree. He remembers the dream: the bird that died in the cage was the voice inside him, and it is still alive.

Payoff ③ ↑He decides to stay by the river and goes looking for the ferryman, who remembers him. The line from years ago, "you will come back too," read like a courtesy at the time. Here it comes true. And the man who said it is the one person in the book who never changes. His name is Vasudeva.

Act Six · The ferryman: a teacher who only listens

Vasudeva takes him on as his assistant. They work the ferry, grow rice, weave baskets. Vasudeva barely speaks. His one gift is listening: Siddhartha tells him his whole life, and he never interrupts, and once it is told it feels washed. The only thing he teaches Siddhartha is to listen to the river.

The first thing the river teaches is that it is everywhere at once: at the source and at the mouth, at the waterfall, the ferry crossing, the rapids, the sea, the mountains. For the river there is only the present, no shadow of the past and no shadow of the future. From this Siddhartha concludes that his own boyhood, manhood and old age are a single thing divided only by shadows. Time is not real.

Then one day the news comes: Gotama is dying. Followers stream in from every direction to see him one last time, among them a woman no longer young, with a boy of ten or so. It is Kamala. She has become a follower of the Buddha and given her garden to the order. She stops to rest by the river, and a black snake bites her.

Vasudeva carries her into the hut. Siddhartha knows her at once, and sees his own face in the boy's. Kamala comes to and looks at him for a long time: he has found peace, she says; looking at this face is as good as seeing the Buddha. She dies looking at him. They burn her body. His son stays.

Act Seven · The son: at last he becomes a child person

The boy grew up in a rich man's garden. He is spoiled and proud, and he despises the hut and the two old men. Siddhartha loves him helplessly: he puts up with the boy's coldness, lets him smash things and shout, hopes to win him over slowly with patience.

At last Vasudeva speaks: you cannot walk your son's road for him; you are binding him with love more tightly than with chains. Let him go back to the city. Siddhartha has no answer, and still he cannot do it. He used to laugh at the child people who broke their hearts over their children. Now he is the most foolish of them all. And it is that foolishness that lets him understand them, for the first time.

Finally the boy explodes. You're not my father, he shouts, even if you did sleep with my mother. He would rather become a thief and a murderer and go to hell than be like him. The next morning he is gone, along with the ferry's takings; he has taken the boat across and left it on the far bank.

Siddhartha builds a raft and follows him all the way into the city, to the gate of what was once Kamala's garden. Standing there, he understands that there is nothing he can do. He can't drag the boy back, and he shouldn't. He sits down by the gate and stays until dark, when Vasudeva comes and, without a word, leads him home.

The wound won't close. One day he sets out across the river again to look for his son. In the middle of the water he looks down and sees a face. Payoff ① ↑It is his father's. Long ago a son stood all night in his father's room, left the next day, and never went back; and that old man hurt the way he hurts now, and very likely died without seeing his son again. The opening night was written as the son's victory. Here it turns over: what was really recorded that night was a father's loss, and Siddhartha could only read it once he became a father himself. The river laughs at him. The same pain keeps coming round, one generation after another.

Act Eight · Om, and Govinda: something that cannot be said

He goes back to the hut and tells Vasudeva everything, even the parts he least wants to admit. As he talks, he begins to feel that the motionless old man listening to him is no longer a man at all, but the river itself, something eternal.

Vasudeva takes him down to the water and tells him to listen once more. Siddhartha hears it: his father's face, his own face, his son's face flowing into one another; longing and pain, laughter and lament, a thousand voices woven together until good and evil can no longer be told apart. Together they make a single word: Om. He stops fighting his fate. The wound blossoms, and a smile comes into his face. Vasudeva sees the smile, says he has been waiting a long time for this hour and can go now, and walks off into the forest. Siddhartha becomes the ferryman.

Many years later an old monk hears of an aged ferryman whom people take for a holy man, and comes to see him. It is Govinda, who has kept the Buddha's teaching all his life and still has not found peace. Once again he doesn't recognize his friend until Siddhartha speaks. Is there anything you can teach me? he asks.

Siddhartha says things that strike Govinda as very strange. Payoff ⑤ ↑First: perhaps you search too hard; someone who is set on seeking sees only what he is looking for. Just as that time by the river, you sat guarding a sleeping man and didn't know who he was. The scene under the palm looked like a coincidence; here it is named. Govinda has been seeking all his life, and so all his life he has failed to see; and even now, having come specially to visit a sage, he sat across from him without knowing him. Then Siddhartha goes on: knowledge can be passed on, wisdom cannot. The opposite of every truth is just as true, because words can only ever say half, while the world is not half samsara and half nirvana. What matters most is not seeing through the world but loving it. And Gotama's greatness lies not in his words but in what he did and how he moved.

Govinda still doesn't understand. He is old now, and he asks for a single word he can take away. Bend down, says Siddhartha. Kiss me on the forehead.

Govinda does. The moment his lips touch the forehead, he can no longer see Siddhartha's face. He sees a river of faces: a dying fish gaping, a newborn crying with its face screwed up, a murderer driving a knife into a body and the same man kneeling bound for the executioner; lovers, corpses, an ox, a crocodile, an elephant, gods. Every face is dying and being born again in another. Payoff ② ↑And over all of them floats a single smile, quiet, gentle, faintly mocking: exactly the smile Govinda had seen on Gotama's face hundreds of times. In Act Two, watching the Buddha walk away, Siddhartha wished he could smile like that. He left the Buddha in order to get that smile, and in the end it really does appear on his face, recognized by the one man who never left the Buddha's side. Govinda bows to the ground, tears running down his face.

The fuse

The fuse is not the three Samanas, nor Kamala's sedan chair. It is that early-morning conversation in the grove in Act Two, where Siddhartha tells the Buddha to his face: you found your way yourself, which is exactly why I cannot follow it. Once that is said, everything after it is its price. With no doctrine to restrain him, he falls headlong into the city for years; with no order to catch him, he ends up on a palm trunk, nearly letting go; having refused to be a student, he can only learn from a river, a ferryman of few words, and a son who hates him. The smile at the end answers that morning's question. Yes, it arrived. And the way it arrived proves that it could never be taught to anyone, including the old friend now kneeling in front of him.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion one · Standing all night

The point of view here is odd. The scene is about the son's resolve, yet the camera stays almost entirely on the father. Every hour he goes to the window; the moon moves, the stars come out, and each time the figure in the room is exactly the same. Hesse writes time through repetition: "he was still standing" said once is stubbornness; said four times, it is a father collapsing layer by layer. The son's entire part is to stand there. When the old man gives in, what we feel is "he did it." But because the camera was on the father, the loss has been quietly deposited in the reader too, to be withdrawn in Act Seven when that face appears in the water.

Slow motion two · Under the palm

The near-suicide is very short: a leaning tree, green water, a tired body tipping forward. What saves him is not a thought but a sound, and it is the most worn-out sound he knows, one he chanted until it meant nothing. If he had been saved by some newly learned truth, the book would be back on the road of doctrine. Om has been in his mouth all along; he just never heard it. The most familiar thing becomes strange again at the moment of greatest despair and is heard for the first time. That is very nearly the book's whole definition of awakening, compressed into an instant.

Slow motion three · The kiss

The whole book stays close to Siddhartha, until the last chapter, which shifts to Govinda. This is the book's most important technical decision: we never see Siddhartha's awakening from the inside. Like Govinda, we only see it from outside. It mirrors Act Two exactly. Then, Siddhartha watched the Buddha from outside and saw a hand and a smile; now Govinda watches Siddhartha and sees the same smile. The vision rolls on in one breath, the reader is swept along with Govinda, and only at the crest do we see the smile. Hesse never explains it, because the book's whole argument is that it can't be explained, only seen.

The device, worked through: splitting the Buddha in two

The book's boldest move is hidden in its title. The historical Buddha's personal name was Siddhartha (roughly, "one who has achieved his aim"); Gotama was his clan name. Hesse gives the two names of one man to two characters: Gotama, the Buddha already awakened, and Siddhartha, a young man who turns him down in order to awaken on his own. So the book's crucial conversation is, in effect, the Buddha's own name cross-examining the Buddha.

Having split him, Hesse then tells the Buddha's life in a different order. In the traditional account, the prince first lives in palace luxury, then renounces it for asceticism, finds extreme austerity useless, and takes the middle way to awakening under the tree. Hesse's Siddhartha does asceticism first, then pleasure in the city, and awakens by a river. Same three stations, different order, different meaning: renounce the world, discover that renunciation is also an escape, go back and taste the world to the dregs, and only then let go. The names of the two city characters are a reminder: Kamaswami combines the Sanskrit kāma (desire, love) with swami (master), and though Kamala literally means "lotus," readers usually hear kāma in it too.

Does the logic of "going it alone" hold up? Walk through it:

Link one: awakening is an experience, not a proposition. This holds. You can tell someone who has never touched water that water is wet; they can remember it, but they won't know what wet feels like.

Link two: therefore doctrine cannot get you there. Here it starts to slip. That doctrine can't replace experience doesn't mean it can't lead you to the experience. Buddhism itself insists on exactly this: the teaching is often described as one that invites you to "come and see," to verify rather than believe. The "doctrine" Siddhartha rejects is partly a straw man. This is the opposition the novel needs, not necessarily Buddhism as it is.

Link three: the river is everywhere at once, so time isn't real. This is the poetic heart of the book, and the link least able to bear weight. The river is "simultaneously" at source and mouth because it is spread out in space, and the water at either end right now is not the same water. Simultaneity in space doesn't give you simultaneity in time. It is an image, not an argument; its job is to make you feel that the boy and the old man are one person, not to prove it.

Link four: so everything is already the Buddha, and the task is not to seek but to love. That conclusion sounds less like Buddhism than like the Vedantic identity of self and Brahman, or the Daoist sense that all things are equal. Inside the novel it holds, because we have just seen it in that kiss; outside the novel it is a faith, not a conclusion. And then there is the prose itself. Hesse deliberately writes like scripture: repeated sentence patterns, an even rhythm, almost no irony. That voice makes it feel like an ancient book, and it is also what later turned it into a self-help manual.

What it forces you to think about

One · You've met a truly great teacher. Do you stay, or go?

Say you're in your early twenties and you meet a mentor you are sure is far wiser than you, or a method you can't find a flaw in. Your best friend signs up on the spot. What do you do?

The strongest case for Govinda: Siddhartha's going it alone wins in the book, but look at the bill. Years of gambling and lending at interest; a son he never raised; a woman who bore that child alone and died by the roadside. In real life most people who insist on finding their own way don't end up at the river. They end up stuck in the city. A good tradition is the accumulated falls of everyone who went before, taken on your behalf; refusing it isn't independence, it's waste. And when someone decides "this can't be taught to me," it may just mean he is too clever, which is precisely what the Buddha warned him about.

The strongest case for Siddhartha: some things can't be had by following, because following changes the very thing you're looking for. Govinda stays with the Buddha his whole life, faultlessly, and in old age admits he hasn't found peace. He learned everything that could be taught; the last step was one nobody could take for him.

It's clear where Hesse stands, but he doesn't make Govinda a fool: the smile at the end is Govinda's to recognize. Bring it home: are you copying a predecessor's path, or borrowing it to find your own? From the outside the two often look identical.

Two · Your child wants to take a road you know will hurt him. Do you let go?

A boy of ten or so hates you and wants to go back to a city of gambling halls and brothels. You are the only family he has. Vasudeva says: let him go.

The strongest case for holding on: Siddhartha was an adult when he left home, able to choose and to bear the consequences. This is a spoiled child, and when he screams that he'd rather steal and kill, he isn't announcing a life plan, he is throwing a tantrum. To send a child back to the place most likely to ruin him and call it "his own path" sounds like wisdom, and may be an adult's elegant excuse for dropping his responsibility. The novel lets Siddhartha off the hook: we never learn what became of the boy.

The strongest case for letting go: Siddhartha's love is turning into the golden cage. The gentler he is, the more the boy suffocates; the more patient, the more the boy hates him. You can't make someone's mistakes for them. Either he grows up beside you resenting you, or he does what you did and falls his way into a path of his own. Siddhartha's father let him go, and everything that followed came from that.

Hesse has Siddhartha accept letting go, but he doesn't make it easy: in the book this wound hurts longest of all. Every parent watching a child take a detour they once took themselves is inside this question, and the answer is usually not "let go" or "hold on" but which regret you can live with.

Three · Those lost years: a necessary road, or meaning invented after the fact?

Looking back from the riverbank, Siddhartha laughs and says he had to commit every folly and taste every sin to arrive here. Is he right?

The strongest case for him: untested virtue is brittle. Someone who has never tasted desire talking about letting go of desire is like someone who has never been in the water talking about swimming. What he learned in the city wasn't only corruption; it was a real understanding of the child people.

The strongest case against: "it was all necessary" is the most comfortable story a person can tell about himself. The debtors he squeezed didn't get a coin back when he was enlightened. Kamala closed her gate and carried his child alone. Many more people sink in the city and never come up, with no river waiting for them. When someone who made it looks back and says every step was necessary, that is a survivor stamping his own road as approved.

Hesse plainly endorses the detour, but he leaves a crack: Siddhartha is laughing when he says it, with a touch of self-mockery. Bring it home: when you say "all that foolishness when I was young was necessary," are you understanding yourself or excusing yourself? And do the people who paid for your foolishness agree?

Motifs: what Hesse kept writing about

Almost every Hesse novel is about the same figure: someone who walks out of an established order to find himself, and the other half he leaves behind inside it. In Beneath the Wheel (1906) a model pupil is crushed by the expectations of school and father; in Demian (1919) the boy Sinclair is led out of his bright family world toward a darker, truer self. Siddhartha turns the walking-out into an entire road, and gives it an ending.

He also keeps writing pairs who need each other. Narcissus and Goldmund (1930) sets a thinker who stays in his monastery beside an artist who goes out into the world and wanders all his life, very nearly a rewriting of Govinda and Siddhartha. Steppenwolf (1927) has the "man" and the "wolf" fighting inside one middle-aged body, with no river to reconcile them.

The late Glass Bead Game (1943) returns to the start: Joseph Knecht rises to the top of a spiritual order, resigns of his own accord to become a private tutor, and soon afterward drowns in an icy mountain lake. Both men leave a perfect system. Siddhartha reaches a river; Knecht walks into a lake. Twenty years on, Hesse is much less optimistic about leaving.

Controversy and criticism

Whose India is this? The most common charge is that the book's India is a European's imagined India: no caste oppression, no colonial rule, no famine, only a river, a forest, yellow robes and a Brahmin youth free to walk away whenever he likes. Its Buddhism, Brahmanism and Laozi are kneaded into something that belongs to Hesse alone, and it often comes up in discussions of Orientalism as a case of the East serving as a stage on which a Westerner finds himself. Defenders point out that Hesse never claimed to be writing anthropology; the subtitle says "a poem."

Unfair to Buddhism? Readers who know Buddhism point out that Siddhartha's objection treats the Buddha's teaching as a creed to be believed, whereas Buddhism stresses direct verification (see link two above). The debate he wins is partly against an opponent of his own devising.

Women as way-stations? Kamala is one of the most vivid people in the book, yet nearly all her functions serve Siddhartha: she teaches him love, carries his child, and on her deathbed confirms his awakening. Her own conversion and the years she raised their son alone get a sentence or two. His mother vanishes after the first chapter. This road is a man's road.

Profound, or easy profundity? Some German critics long regarded Hesse as a writer for adolescents: the sentences too smooth, the answers too quick. He won the Nobel Prize in 1946, and in the 1960s he suddenly became a sensation in the American counterculture, with Siddhartha and Steppenwolf treated as hippie scripture. A book that says wisdom can't be taught was taken up by millions as a textbook, and that paradox may be the sharpest criticism of all.

Crossovers

  • Philosophy · Polanyi's tacit knowledge: In The Tacit Dimension (1966) Michael Polanyi wrote that we know more than we can tell; riding a bike or recognizing a face can't be spelled out, only picked up beside a master. Same shape as "knowledge can be passed on, wisdom cannot." The difference: for Polanyi, apprenticeship is exactly how tacit knowledge travels, and Siddhartha, having refused the Buddha, ends up apprenticed to a ferryman.
  • Psychology · Carl Rogers's client-centered therapy: Rogers held that the therapist should give no advice, only unconditional acceptance and close listening, so clients find their own answers. Vasudeva is that kind of listener. The difference: Rogers's listening can be trained; Vasudeva's came from a lifetime of listening to a river.
  • Buddhism · the flower sermon: Zen tradition says the Buddha once held up a flower in silence and only Mahakasyapa smiled, so began a transmission "mind to mind," outside words (a story that took shape in Song-dynasty Chan texts). The smile passed on through a kiss has the same shape; but Zen prizes lineage, and Siddhartha's smile came only after he left one.
  • Philosophy · Heraclitus's river: Heraclitus said you can't step into the same river twice. Siddhartha's river says almost the reverse: it is the same river everywhere and at every moment. One image, two claims: "nothing stays the same," and "beneath all change there is only one thing."

Ten lines to take with you

① A son stood all night in his father's room to win his freedom; years later he saw that father's face in the river.

② Asceticism and a bowl of rice wine get you out of yourself for about the same length of time.

③ He told the Buddha: you found your road yourself, which is why I can't walk it.

④ He brought thinking, waiting and fasting into the city; it taught him dice, wine and debt collection.

⑤ The bird in the golden cage died in his dream and flew from her hand.

⑥ He was saved not by a new truth but by a word he had chanted all his life and never heard.

⑦ The ferryman said "you'll come back." He did; it took him half a lifetime.

⑧ He laughed at people who broke their hearts over their children, until he became the biggest fool of them all.

⑨ The best teacher taught nothing. He only listened.

⑩ He left the Buddha to get the Buddha's smile; the one who recognized it was the man who never left.
