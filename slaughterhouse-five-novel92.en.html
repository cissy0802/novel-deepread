Slaughterhouse-Five

Slaughterhouse-Five, or The Children's Crusade · Kurt Vonnegut · 1969 · ten chapters

中文 →

Down in the meat locker, cut deep under the slaughterhouse, a few carcasses hang from hooks, and a hundred or so American prisoners sit among them all night while the city overhead makes noise like giant footsteps.
At noon the next day the German guards let them climb the stairs.
Billy Pilgrim walks out of the slaughterhouse gate and there is no city. No roofs, no windows, no trees. Only smoking stone in every direction, like the surface of the moon.
The four guards huddle together, mouths opening and closing, no sound coming out, like a barbershop quartet in a silent film.

This piece contains complete spoilers: how Billy comes "unstuck in time," what the Tralfamadorians teach him, who dies after the night Dresden burned, how and when Billy himself is killed, and the birdcall the book ends on. The novel gives away its own first and last lines in chapter one; it never runs on suspense. You can't explain why it is built this way without going all the way to the end.

In one sentence

An American soldier who survived the firebombing of Dresden later claims to have been kidnapped by aliens and to bounce at random between the moments of his own life; their doctrine that every moment exists forever is what gets him through the memory of a city burned to the ground. It is an anti-war novel that took its author twenty-three years to write, and its most honest move is admitting there is nothing intelligent to say about a massacre.

Coordinates

Kurt Vonnegut (1922–2007) was born in Indianapolis. In December 1944 he was captured during the Battle of the Bulge and shipped to Dresden as a prisoner of war, where his work unit was billeted in a slaughterhouse. On the night of February 13, 1945, British and American bombers destroyed the city; Vonnegut and his fellow prisoners survived in a meat locker underground, and in the days that followed they were put to work digging bodies out of the ruins. This book is those days.

Published in 1969, it was his sixth novel. Until then he had been filed as a paperback science-fiction writer; arriving in the middle of the Vietnam War, this book made him the writer of a generation. It occupies an odd square on the literary map: one foot in science fiction (aliens, a flying saucer, time travel), the other in war testimony, and the author himself standing in the gap. There was a 1972 film; this piece is about the novel only.

The story

The novel refuses chronological order: one paragraph Billy is in the snow of 1944, the next he is in his postwar office. What follows runs roughly along Billy's life so the causes stay visible, but it starts where the book actually starts, in a first chapter where Billy hasn't appeared yet and the only character is the author.

Act one · A book that wouldn't get written

Vonnegut opens in his own voice: all of this happened, more or less, and the war parts are pretty much true. Coming home, he assumed Dresden would be easy to write, since all he had to do was report what he'd seen. Twenty-odd years later it still wasn't written.

He goes to Pennsylvania to reminisce with his old war buddy, Bernard V. O'Hare. O'Hare's wife Mary is cold to him all evening and finally explodes: they were just babies then. He'll write it as if they'd been men, it will be filmed with Frank Sinatra and John Wayne, war will look glorious, and there will be more of it. Vonnegut promises her there won't be a part for Sinatra or Wayne, and that he'll call the book The Children's Crusade. That is where the subtitle comes from, and it is the book's founding promise: war will not be written up as a manly adventure.

He announces that the climax will probably be the execution of poor old Edgar Derby: a whole city burns, tens of thousands die, and then one American foot soldier is arrested in the ruins for taking a teapot, given a trial, and shot by a firing squad. The irony, he says, is so great.Set-up ① He confesses a habit, too: late at night, drunk, he telephones old acquaintances, and his breath drives his wife out of the room. He describes it as smelling like mustard gas and roses.Set-up ② On first reading it's just a middle-aged man poking fun at himself.

The book is short and jumbled, he explains, because there is nothing intelligent to say about a massacre; afterward everyone is dead and only the birds are talking. So he gives away both ends. It begins: "Listen: Billy Pilgrim has come unstuck in time." It ends with a birdcall: "Poo-tee-weet?"Set-up ③

The chapter closes on Lot's wife, told not to look back at Sodom, who looked back and became a pillar of salt. Vonnegut says he loves her for it, because it was so human. This book is a failure, he says, because it was written by a pillar of salt. The whole novel is one long look back by someone who knows looking back will freeze him in place.

Act two · The Ardennes: a man who neither wants to live nor to die

Billy Pilgrim was born in 1922 in Ilium, New York, the son of a barber. His father once taught him to swim by throwing him into the deep end of the YMCA pool, sink or swim; Billy sank, found it peaceful down there, and had to be fished out. He managed a semester of optometry school before he was drafted and made a chaplain's assistant, an unarmed job nobody respected. Tall and weak, shaped like a bottle of Coca-Cola, he looks superfluous wherever he stands.

In December 1944 the German counteroffensive in the Ardennes shatters his unit. Billy ends up behind enemy lines in the snowy forest with three others: two experienced scouts and an eighteen-year-old antitank gunner named Roland Weary. Weary was the fat kid nobody would play with, and he has built a story in his head in which he and the scouts are the Three Musketeers, comrades to the death. He loves to describe instruments of torture to Billy, at length and with relish.

Billy has no helmet and no weapon. He doesn't want to live and can't be bothered to die; more than once he simply stops in the road. Weary keeps shoving him back into line, hating him for it. The scouts finally ditch them both. Weary is about to beat Billy senseless when German soldiers step out of the trees and take them prisoner.

It is in this forest that Billy first comes unstuck in time: leaning against a tree, he swings through the whole arc of his life, birth to death, and is dropped back into the snow. The novel treats this as literal fact. Which day of his life he'll land in next, Billy doesn't get to choose.

The Germans take Weary's boots and give him wooden clogs. The prisoners are packed into boxcars and rolled east for days. Weary's feet rot in the clogs; gangrene sets in, and he dies in the car, telling everyone in earshot that Billy Pilgrim killed him. A small, vicious soldier named Paul Lazzaro promises the dying Weary he'll get revenge: after the war he'll have Billy shot. Revenge, Lazzaro says, is the sweetest thing in life, and he never forgets anyone who crossed him.Set-up ④

A hobo in the same car tells the others, freezing and starving, that this isn't so bad; he can be comfortable anywhere. Before long he is dead too. Each time someone dies, the narrator adds three words: So it goes.

Act three · Ilium: a respectable optometrist

From the outside, the postwar Billy is a success. Back at optometry school, he gets engaged to Valencia Merble, the owner's daughter, who is heavy and eats candy bars constantly; he never wanted to marry her, and the book calls her one of the symptoms of his disease. In 1948 he breaks down and commits himself to a veterans' hospital psychiatric ward, where he meets another veteran, Eliot Rosewater. Rosewater introduces him to the novels of Kilgore Trout, a science-fiction writer whose prose is dreadful and whose books don't sell but whose ideas are astonishing. Two men who saw things they shouldn't have both needed to reinvent their universe, and science fiction was a big help.

After the hospital he marries Valencia; her father sets him up in practice, and he becomes a rich and respected optometrist in Ilium: a Cadillac, a big house, a son and a daughter. His son Robert is a delinquent teenager who later goes to Vietnam as a Green Beret. On the wall of Billy's office hangs a framed prayer, asking God for the serenity to accept the things one cannot change, the courage to change the things one can, and the wisdom to know the difference.Set-up ⑤ The narrator adds a dry footnote, roughly: among the things Billy could not change were the past, the present, and the future.

There are cracks under the calm. At his eighteenth wedding anniversary party, a barbershop quartet of four optometrists sings an old song about old friends, and Billy goes gray in the face, so plainly stricken that people think he's ill. He can't say what happened to him.

Act four · Tralfamadore: Earthlings in a zoo

In 1967, on the night of his daughter Barbara's wedding, Billy can't sleep. He knows the flying saucer is coming tonight; he always knows. He goes downstairs, watches a late-night war movie about bombers backwards (more on this below), then walks out into the backyard. A saucer descends and takes him.

His abductors are Tralfamadorians: two feet tall, green, shaped like plumber's friends, with a small hand on top and a green eye in its palm. They see in four dimensions; to them a person is not a person at one instant but a long centipede stretching from infancy to old age. Every moment, past, present and future, always has existed and always will exist. When someone dies, he is merely in bad condition in that particular moment; in plenty of other moments he is fine. So when a Tralfamadorian sees a corpse, all he says is: So it goes.

Aboard the saucer Billy asks, why me? The answer, more or less: why you, why us, why anything? Because this moment simply is. Have you ever seen bugs trapped in amber? That's what we all are. They add that in all of the thirty-one inhabited planets they've visited, Earth is the only one where anybody talks about free will.

Billy is put on display in the Tralfamadorian zoo, under a transparent dome furnished like an American home. Later they bring in a young earthling woman, the film actress Montana Wildhack. She is terrified at first, then comes to trust Billy; they become a couple, and she has his child.

The Tralfamadorians know how the universe ends: one of their test pilots, experimenting with a new fuel, presses a starter button and the whole universe disappears. Billy asks why they don't stop him. Because he has always pressed it, and he always will; they have always let him, and always will. The moment is structured that way. The only life advice they give Billy is this: ignore the awful times and concentrate on the good ones.

In the last image of the zoo, Montana sits nursing their baby. Around her neck hangs a silver locket, and engraved on it is the same prayer that hangs in Billy's office.Payoff ⑤ ↑ The prayer from act three reappears in a new place, and the narrator repeats the same footnote: among the things Billy could not change were the past, the present, and the future. A prayer asking for the courage to change what can be changed has traveled from Ilium to Tralfamadore, a universe in which nothing can be changed at all. The words are kept intact and the meaning is scooped out. It is the quietest piece of mockery in the book.

Act five · Dresden: Slaughterhouse-Five

Back to 1945. Billy is first held in a large prison camp alongside a group of long-term English officer prisoners who, thanks to a clerical error at the Red Cross, have hoarded enormous supplies; they are healthy and hearty, like Englishmen on a war poster, and they stage a pantomime of Cinderella for the Americans. Billy laughs hysterically at the show and is sedated with morphine. He wakes up in Cinderella's silver-painted boots and an absurdly small overcoat with a fur collar, and in that costume he is shipped to Dresden.

In the same group is a middle-aged man named Edgar Derby, a high-school teacher from Indianapolis who was old enough to stay home and pulled strings to get into the war. He is decent and earnest, and the prisoners elect him their leader. When Howard W. Campbell, Jr., an American who has gone over to the Nazis as a propagandist, comes to recruit prisoners to fight the Russians on the Eastern Front, Derby stands up and delivers the finest speech of his life, saying he would rather die than join. It is the one moment in the book when someone behaves like the hero of a conventional war novel. Vonnegut gives it to Derby, once.

When the train pulls into Dresden, the city is untouched and lovely, like something out of a fairy tale. In the boxcar someone says, "Oz." That was me, the narrator tells us: the author of this book. In the novel's account the city has almost no war industry, and everyone believes it won't be bombed.

The prisoners are quartered in building number five of the slaughterhouse, Schlachthof-fünf: the title. By day they work in a factory making malt syrup, a vitamin supplement for pregnant women, and everyone sneaks spoonfuls.

On the night of February 13, the bombers come. The prisoners and a handful of guards shelter in the meat locker under the slaughterhouse while the city above becomes a firestorm; Billy hears only a thudding like giant footsteps. At noon the next day they climb the stairs into the opening scene. The death toll the book cites is 135,000 (see the criticism section on that figure).

In the days that follow the prisoners are sent to work the "corpse mines," opening cellars in the rubble and carrying out the people who suffocated or burned inside. A Maori soldier digging alongside Billy starts retching and can't stop, and dies of it. There are too many bodies; eventually the Germans stop carrying them out and send soldiers in with flamethrowers to burn them where they lie. The smell in those cellars, the book says, is like roses and mustard gas.

Payoff ② ↑ In chapter one the author's drunken late-night breath smelled of the same two things, mustard gas and roses. It read like a joke at his own expense; now we know where it came from. It is the smell of the Dresden cellars, and for twenty-odd years it has followed him, rising out of his mouth on every drunken night. Vonnegut never explains it. He just lets the same pair of words appear twice.

During those days of digging, poor old Edgar Derby takes a teapot from the catacombs. He is caught, tried for plundering, and shot. So it goes.

Payoff ① ↑ In chapter one Vonnegut said this would be the climax, that the irony was so great. When he gets there, he spends a few lines on it, wedged between the corpse mines and the end of the war, so flat it's easy to read past. He announces a climax and then refuses to write it as one, because staging it with any grandeur would be doing exactly what Mary O'Hare feared: giving war a beautiful scene. The Derby who stood up to Campbell like a movie hero dies like a footnote.

Act six · 1968 and 1976: telling it, and being killed

In 1968 Billy charters a plane with fellow optometrists to a convention in Montreal. It crashes into Sugarbush Mountain in Vermont, and only Billy and the copilot survive. Billy has a fractured skull; as he is rescued he keeps murmuring one German phrase: Schlachthof-fünf. Valencia, racing to the hospital, has a car accident that breaks her exhaust system, and dies of carbon monoxide poisoning before she gets there. So it goes.

In the Vermont hospital Billy's neighbor is Bertram Copeland Rumfoord, a seventy-year-old Harvard history professor and the official historian of the Air Force, who is writing a history of the Army Air Corps in the Second World War and reading up on Dresden. He takes Billy for a vegetable who only parrots what others say, until Billy speaks: I was there. Rumfoord, after his disbelief wears off, says it had to be done. Billy answers that he knows; everybody has to do exactly what he does, he learned that on Tralfamadore. A lesson from the aliens, coming out of a survivor's mouth, ends up excusing the bombers.

Out of the hospital, Billy starts talking. He slips off to New York, goes on an all-night radio talk show about Tralfamadore and the nonexistence of death, and writes letters to the paper back home. His daughter Barbara treats him as senile. In New York he wanders into a Times Square porn shop, where in the window he finds an old Kilgore Trout novel about an earthling man and woman kidnapped by aliens and displayed in a zoo. A magazine on the rack asks what really became of Montana Wildhack, a starlet who has disappeared; her film is playing in the back of the shop.

Billy says he knows how he will die. In 1976, by which time the United States has been broken up into twenty petty nations, he lectures to a crowd in a Chicago ballpark about flying saucers and time. He tells them he is about to be killed, tells them it is time to go home to their wives and children and time for him to be dead for a little while, and then live again. A laser beam strikes him. The shooter was hired by Paul Lazzaro.

Payoff ④ ↑ The vow Lazzaro made over Weary in that boxcar in act two is kept more than thirty years later: he really does have Billy killed. But it lands as the least dramatic possible fulfillment, because Billy knew all along and doesn't resist. In a conventional novel, a setup paying off gives you the pleasure of fate snapping shut; here it only proves what the Tralfamadorians said, that the moment is structured that way.

The novel ends back in spring 1945. The war in Europe is over. Billy and a few other prisoners ride an abandoned green wagon shaped like a coffin into town to look for souvenirs. A German couple, both obstetricians, stop them and point at the horses: their mouths are bleeding from the bits, their hooves are broken, they are desperate with thirst. Billy hadn't noticed. He goes to look, and he cries. It is the only time in the whole war that he does.

The trees are leafing out. A bird says to Billy Pilgrim, "Poo-tee-weet?"

Payoff ③ ↑ The author handed us this birdcall in chapter one and told us the book would end on it. Now it does: no verdict, no lesson, only a question. After a massacre only the birds are left, nobody understands what they're saying, and what they say is a question mark. That is the one answer Vonnegut could find to "there is nothing intelligent to say about a massacre."

If you're looking for the fuse, it isn't the Tralfamadorians or the plane in Vermont. The fuse is the night of February 13, 1945, which Billy spent underground, seeing nothing. He didn't see the bombing. He saw the moon the next day and the bodies in the cellars, and he could neither understand it, nor tell it, nor forget it. Everything after is aftershock: the 1948 breakdown, the sudden terror at the anniversary party, and after the head injury in 1968, telling the world about aliens. The aliens gave him a doctrine in which a city's destruction becomes "the moment is structured that way." Coming unstuck in time is the only way out a man could think of after getting stuck in it.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion one · The war movie run backwards

On his daughter's wedding night, Billy watches a Second World War bomber movie backwards. German fighters fly backwards and suck bullets out of American planes. Over a burning German city, the bombers open their bomb-bay doors and, by some miraculous magnetism, shrink the fires and gather them into steel canisters, which rise into the bellies of the planes and are stowed in neat racks. Back at base the canisters are dismantled, the dangerous contents separated into minerals and buried in the ground, where they will never hurt anybody again. The fliers turn back into high-school kids, and Billy extrapolates: Hitler turns into a baby, and all humanity runs backwards toward two perfect people named Adam and Eve.

It works because it uses a science-fiction trick to do what no anti-war manifesto can: make the reader actually see a bombing for the first time. Run forward, an air raid is footage we went numb to long ago. Run backward, every step turns strange: bombs are minerals dug out of the earth; the pilots are children. Placed just before the saucer arrives, it previews the Tralfamadorian view of time, and because the aliens will then tell Billy time can't be changed, the reversal is unbearably sad. It is the only moment in the book when time is imagined as something that could be undone.

Slow motion two · The night that waits twenty years

The bombing is the center of the book, and the book keeps not telling it. It is mentioned in chapter one; Billy has long since reached Dresden; well past the halfway point, the night still hasn't appeared. When it finally does, the trigger is the anniversary party: four optometrists singing in close harmony in front of him. Billy can't stand it and flees upstairs, and there, for the first time, he remembers the night. He doesn't time-travel to it; he remembers it, the ordinary way. The meat locker; the moon the next day; the four guards huddled together in astonishment, like a barbershop quartet.

It works because it turns the actual shape of trauma into the shape of the narrative: the most important thing is told last, and it is set off by something irrelevant. Four middle-aged men harmonizing at a party may be the most harmless image in the novel, and Vonnegut makes it the key to the most terrible door. After waiting most of the book, the reader gets not a sea of fire but a man who saw nothing remembering the day after. The bombing itself stays offstage, because Billy was underground and so was Vonnegut. This isn't evasion but honesty: a survivor has the before and the after, never the moment itself.

Slow motion three · Crying, once, for the horses

Billy has seen bodies by the thousand, seen Derby shot, and hasn't cried. After the war, dozing in the wagon in the sun, he is woken by two German obstetricians who make him look at the horses: bleeding mouths, broken hooves. He gets down and looks, and he weeps.

It works because grief has finally found an object it can hold. A six-figure death toll fits in nobody's heart; a horse does. All book long, "so it goes" has pressed every death flat, and here the pressure is released. And the people who wake him are Germans who deliver babies: Vonnegut doesn't cast an accuser, he casts two people who look after life and pierce a soldier's numbness with the plainest common sense.

The idea, taken seriously: unstuck in time, and Tralfamadore

The rules. To a Tralfamadorian, time is not a river but a mountain range: every moment laid out side by side, all of it visible at once if you stand high enough. Hence no free will and no prevention. Billy's being unstuck in time means his consciousness has fallen out of the single line and lands at random somewhere in the range.

The chain of logic. If every moment exists forever, then death is only bad condition in one moment; then grief is a mistake of perspective; then the rational posture is to dwell on good moments and say "so it goes" to bad ones. Each link holds. The problem is at the start: the doctrine quietly swaps "what has happened can't be changed," which is common sense, for "what hasn't happened yet can't be changed either," which is fatalism.

It is the same thing as the book's form. Tralfamadorian novels, Billy learns, are clumps of symbols, each one a brief urgent situation, read all at once: no beginning, middle or end, no suspense, no moral, no causes, no effects, only the depth of many marvelous moments seen at one time. Slaughterhouse-Five is built exactly like this: short sections, time skipping freely, the ending announced in chapter one. It is a Tralfamadorian novel written by an earthling, using an alien form to hold something an earthling's straight-line narrative couldn't.

"So it goes." The phrase appears about a hundred times, after every death: people, horses, lice, even a bottle of champagne gone flat. It is a Tralfamadorian calm that flattens death, and also a counter that makes the reader pause each time. In the last chapter Vonnegut uses it for Robert Kennedy and Martin Luther King, both shot in 1968, and for the daily body counts out of Vietnam. In that chapter it is plainly not numbness.

Is Tralfamadore real? The book won't say, but it lays out the clues. Billy read Kilgore Trout by the armload in a psych ward in 1948; he says he was kidnapped in 1967 but tells no one until after his skull fracture in 1968; earthlings in an alien zoo is the plot of a Trout novel; Montana is a missing starlet from a Times Square peep show. Read that way, Tralfamadore is a refuge a badly hurt man built out of pulp paperbacks. Read the other way, Billy really does know the date of his death. This is a novel's premise, not physics, and the book's cleverest move is that its point survives either reading: whether or not the aliens exist, a man needs them to.

What it forces you to think about

One · Looking only at the good moments: wisdom or anesthetic?

The Tralfamadorians tell Billy that since the bad times can't be changed, he should stop looking at them and spend his attention on the good ones. Picture a parent who has lost a child. Every day they can go back to the afternoon it happened, or go back only to the summers when the child was laughing. Which is right?

Make the case for the good moments as strongly as it can be made. Chewing on pain isn't loyalty; it is a second wound. That afternoon won't change if you relive it a thousand times, and the child won't live a second longer. The summers really happened, and in some sense always will have; they are no less real than the tragedy. From the Stoics to the Buddhists, the advice is the same: don't suffer over what you can't change. If a man tormented by nightmares finds a story that lets him sleep and smile at his children, who are we to accuse him of evasion? Demanding that a survivor keep suffering for the sake of honesty is a luxury of the people who weren't there.

The other side: "don't look at the bad times" doesn't stay a private therapy; it turns into politics. If every death earns only "so it goes," nobody has to answer for it and nobody has to prevent the next one. When Billy tells Rumfoord that everybody has to do exactly what he does, his serenity is doing the bombers' excusing for them. Grief carries information: something here was wrong. Switch it off and you stop receiving the message.

Where does the author stand? In the last chapter he writes that if what Billy learned from the Tralfamadorians is true, that we all live forever, he is "not overjoyed" (chapter ten). Then he lists Kennedy, King and the Vietnam dead one by one, each followed by "so it goes." He uses the phrase, and refuses to live by it. Bring it home: have you ever said of something old, "never mind, it's over," and realized it wasn't letting go, only not daring to look?

Two · "It had to be done" versus "I was there": who gets to judge a bombing?

Rumfoord has every archive and sees the whole board. Billy saw a moon and the bodies in a cellar and can't put a coherent sentence together. On whether Dresden should have been bombed, whom do you believe?

Argue Rumfoord's case better than he does. In February 1945 Germany was still fighting and the camps were still running; every additional day of war meant thousands more dead. Allied commanders answered for their own soldiers, not for enemy cities, and Dresden was a rail hub. A prisoner in a cellar sees the worst corner of the picture, but worst doesn't mean wrong: seen up close, every just war is a massacre. If only those who were there may speak, history is nothing but wounds and no judgment.

The other side: the person who was there knows one thing no archive holds, which is what it was like. Rumfoord's "had to" lives on paper and never has to pass through a particular cellar; the persuasiveness of the big picture often comes from not having a single face in it. And "had to" is a dangerous phrase. It turns a choice into a natural phenomenon, as if bombing were weather.

Where does the author stand? He makes Rumfoord arrogant and cold, but he doesn't let Billy rebut him; Billy only says that everybody does what he must. Vonnegut doesn't write a polemic against the bombing. He puts the two voices face to face and lets us watch the one person entitled to speak choose silence. Bring it home: during layoffs the person reading the spreadsheet says it had to be done and the person laid off says they were in that department. Have you ever seen either side really hear the other?

Three · Is an anti-war novel any use?

In chapter one a filmmaker, hearing Vonnegut is writing an anti-war book, asks why he doesn't write an anti-glacier book instead. Wars come like glaciers; books don't stop them. So was it worth twenty-three years?

Make the case for "no use" at full strength. Humanity has written war tragedies for three thousand years, and not one war was skipped on that account. Worse, however anti-war a war novel is, it turns war into story, and the reader closes the book with aesthetic satisfaction and even a moral comfort, the sense of having thought about it. That is precisely what Mary O'Hare feared, and Slaughterhouse-Five itself became a movie. The likeliest effect of anti-war literature is to give war another life in the culture.

The other side: published at the height of Vietnam, it became the book of a generation of young readers, many of whom learned from it for the first time that the good side also burned a city full of civilians. And it is built the way it is precisely as an answer to the glacier line: no heroes, no climax, Derby dying as a footnote. It writes war so that it can't be enjoyed. It may not stop the glacier, but it refuses to take a flattering picture of it.

Where does the author stand? He put the glacier line in the book, didn't argue with it, and didn't put down his pen. He called the book a failure written by a pillar of salt, and he finished it anyway. Bring it home: when you share a war video on your phone, are you bearing witness or consuming it?

Motifs: what Vonnegut keeps writing about

All his life Vonnegut wrote one situation: small people pushed around by an enormous, indifferent machine (technology, war, the cosmos) who need a story in order to keep living. Tralfamadore first appears in The Sirens of Titan (1959), whose conclusion is harsher still: all of human history turns out to have been arranged to deliver a spare part to a stranded Tralfamadorian messenger. Cat's Cradle (1963) invents Bokononism, a religion that openly admits its teachings are lies, harmless untruths that make people feel better. That is almost exactly what Tralfamadore is for Billy.

Several characters walk in from earlier books: Campbell is the American agent turned Nazi broadcaster of Mother Night (1961); Rosewater comes from God Bless You, Mr. Rosewater (1965); Trout would go on to star in Breakfast of Champions (1973). In the map of Vonnegut's work, Slaughterhouse-Five is where the old themes finally find their source: science fiction, fatalism, harmless lies, the insistence on kindness all turn out to have grown out of a cellar in Dresden.

Controversy and criticism

The death toll. The figure of 135,000 the book uses comes from the British writer David Irving's The Destruction of Dresden (1963). Irving later became a Holocaust denier; in 2000 he lost a libel suit he had brought in London against an American historian, and the court found he had distorted historical evidence. A historians' commission appointed by the city of Dresden reported in 2010 that the dead numbered up to about 25,000. Vonnegut didn't invent his number, but a reader today should know it was badly inflated.

Fatalism, and glibness. Critics argue that the Tralfamadorian philosophy and "so it goes" teach passivity, and that the phrase has been repeated into a T-shirt slogan. Defenders answer that the book displays the attitude rather than recommends it.

The women. Valencia is little more than a running joke about weight and candy bars; Montana is a starlet out of a blue movie, issued to Billy by the aliens as a mate. Defenders quote the book itself: there are almost no characters in this story, because most of the people in it are so sick and such listless playthings of enormous forces. That holds up, but the farce made of Valencia's death still grates.

Banning. It is one of the most frequently banned novels in America. In 1973 the school board in Drake, North Dakota, had copies burned; it was also among the books at issue in the 1982 Supreme Court case Board of Education, Island Trees v. Pico, about removing books from school libraries. The usual complaints were profanity, sex and a lack of patriotism.

Crossovers

  • Psychiatry · trauma flashbacks: A PTSD flashback isn't remembering the past but having that moment happen again now; the diagnosis entered the American psychiatric manual only in 1980, eleven years after the novel. "Unstuck in time" is the same shape. Where it differs: flashbacks drag you back to the worst moment, while Billy also lands in childhood, weddings and his own death, mostly calmly.
  • Philosophy of physics · the block universe: Eternalism holds that past, present and future are equally real, one block of spacetime; after his friend Besso died, Einstein wrote to the family that the distinction between them is only a stubbornly persistent illusion. Tralfamadore's mountain range has that shape; but no consciousness hops around inside a block universe.
  • Stoicism · the dichotomy of control: Epictetus taught that some things are up to us and some aren't, and wisdom is telling them apart, which is the structure of the prayer on Billy's wall. Where it differs: the Stoics left you at least your own judgments; the Tralfamadorians take even those.
  • Psychology · psychic numbing: Paul Slovic found that as victims go from one to many, compassion drops rather than rises. "So it goes" gives every death the same three words, as if acting out that numbness and resisting it at once: no death gets folded into a total.

Ten lines to take with you

① There's nothing intelligent to say about a massacre, so the last line of the book is a birdcall.

② Billy isn't traveling in time; he's stuck in it, and bouncing around is all he can do.

③ The bombing itself never appears; a survivor has only the before and the after.

④ The most important night in the book is unlocked by four optometrists singing in harmony.

⑤ Mustard gas and roses: the author's late-night breath turns out to be the smell of the Dresden cellars.

⑥ Vonnegut promised a climax and wrote it in three lines, because war doesn't deserve one.

⑦ A prayer for the courage to change things, hanging in a universe where nothing changes.

⑧ The aliens taught Billy to answer death with "so it goes"; the author used the same words to call the roll.

⑨ A man who saw thousands of corpses without crying cried for two horses.

⑩ Lot's wife looked back and turned to salt; this book was written by the pillar.
