Novels, Deep-Read · Yasunari Kawabata · No.106

Snow Country

Yukiguni · Yasunari Kawabata · 1935–1948 · short novel · published in fragments, finalized 1948

中文 →

The train has been climbing through the mountains all afternoon. Night comes on, and the window turns into a mirror.
Bored, Shimamura draws a line with his left forefinger across the fogged glass, and a woman's eye floats up in it.
It belongs to the girl in the seat opposite, bent over a sick man. Behind her face the dusk landscape keeps streaming past. She is transparent, the mountains are transparent, and neither blots out the other.
Far off in the fields a light comes on. For one instant it sits exactly in the pupil of her eye, like a firefly burning there, and then it drifts away.

Full spoilers ahead: what Shimamura finds on each of his three trips to the snow country, why Komako refuses to go to a dying man's bedside, whom the word "wasted" finally lands on, and who falls from the second floor of the burning cocoon warehouse. The book has almost no "plot" to spoil, but once you know the ending, that train window at the start is never the same window again.

In One Sentence

A Tokyo man who lives on inherited money and takes nothing seriously rides three times through a long tunnel to a hot-spring town on the far side of the mountains to see a geisha; she stakes everything she has on him, and he simply watches her, beautifully, from a distance. This is a novel about looking: a man who sees a woman's love as a beautiful waste, until he finally recognizes that the waste is himself.

Coordinates

Yasunari Kawabata (1899–1972) was born in Osaka. Both parents died before he was four; his grandmother, his sister and finally his grandfather followed, and by his mid-teens he was entirely alone. In the 1920s he and Riichi Yokomitsu led the Shinkankakuha, the "New Sensationalist" school, which tried to replace logical connection with leaps of sensation. His breakthrough was "The Dancing Girl of Izu" (1926). In 1968 he became the first Japanese writer to win the Nobel Prize in Literature, and the Swedish Academy named Snow Country among the works behind it.

The book came together in an unusual way. From 1935 it appeared as separate stories in different magazines, each readable on its own; a first book edition followed in 1937, the ending was written in the 1940s, and the definitive text dates from 1948. The setting is a hot-spring town in the Echigo-Yuzawa area of Niigata, though the novel never names a single place. Its slot in Japanese literature is the one where haiku's art of omission and pure sensation is stretched to the length of a whole novel: half of everything is said, and the rest is left in the snow.

The Story

Act One · The Window: A Woman Remembered by One Finger

The story opens on the second visit. It is winter, and Shimamura's train passes through the long tunnel at the provincial border into sudden whiteness. In Edward Seidensticker's English, the line nearly every Japanese reader knows by heart goes:

"The train came out of the long tunnel into the snow country."Snow Country, opening line · tr. Edward G. Seidensticker

The train halts at a signal stop. The girl opposite opens the window and calls across the snow to the stationmaster, in a voice so beautiful it is almost sad, a voice that carries a long way. She asks him to look after her younger brother, who works there. Her name is Yoko. Lying beside her is a gravely ill young man; she tucks his blanket, steadies his feet, with the absorption of a young wife, or of a mother.

Shimamura watches her the whole way, but never directly. He watches her reflection. Once the dark falls, the glass becomes a mirror in which Yoko's face lies over the flowing dusk, and that field light passes through her eye. Seed ① The world in the mirror strikes him as unreal, too beautiful to belong to this one. It is the first gesture in the book and the posture of Shimamura's whole life: he looks at people through a pane of glass.

He is going to see another woman. Idling on the train, he had stared at his left forefinger and felt that this finger alone still remembered her clearly, that her touch had stayed on its tip. From that finger the novel turns back to tell how they met.

It was the previous early summer. Shimamura had spent seven days climbing alone in the mountains and came down to this spa wanting a geisha. The one who came was nineteen and not yet a geisha at all, just a girl called in to help at banquets when the town was busy, living at the house of a woman who taught samisen and dance. She was startlingly clean; he would later think she seemed clean right down to the creases between her toes. As a child she had been sent to Tokyo to pour sake at parties, had been bought out of her contract by a man who meant to set her up as a dance teacher, and when he died within two years she came home.

Shimamura enjoyed talking with her about kabuki and Tokyo, decided a friend like this was rare, and so asked her to go and fetch him a "real" geisha. His meaning was plain: he wanted someone to sleep with, and that someone need not be her. She flushed and was offended, but she went. He didn't care for the woman she brought and sent her away. Then, one night, she came reeling into his room, drunk. Not long after, he went back to Tokyo.

Her name was Komako.

Act Two · Winter: One Hundred and Ninety-Nine Days, and Notebooks Full of Titles

Getting off at the station this time, Shimamura notices that the young couple from the train have left at the same stop. At the inn Komako comes to him; she is a geisha now, properly. She says nothing about the months between, only counts on her fingers and works out that it has been one hundred and ninety-nine days.

The novel tells us early who Shimamura is. He has a wife and children in Tokyo, lives on family money, and has no real occupation. He writes a little about Western dance, yet he has never seen a live Western ballet; he works entirely from foreign books and photographs, imagining it on paper. Everything he loves, he can love only with something in between.

Komako talks to him a great deal. She has kept a diary since she was fifteen or sixteen without missing a day. She has also written down every novel she has read, the title, the author, the characters and how they relate, filling more than a dozen notebooks, though her reading is a jumble of borrowed magazines and old books. Listening, Shimamura blurts out a word: wasted. What is the point of recording all that? She never discusses it with anyone; it adds up to no learning; she simply writes it down, line after line. Komako cheerfully agrees: yes, it's wasted. Then she goes right on talking. Part of Shimamura thinks it is pure futility, and part of him is moved by something clean inside that futility. Seed ②

She takes him to see where she lives: the loft of her teacher's house, once a room for raising silkworms. Old newspapers cover the walls and ceiling, the room is cold, and snow lies right beneath the window. He thinks of her as a silkworm, her transparent body living up there. Seed ③ At the time it reads as nothing more than a pretty figure of speech, and neither Shimamura nor the reader thinks twice about it.

Two more people live in the teacher's house. One is her son Yukio, the sick man from the train, home from Tokyo with severe tuberculosis to die. The other, nursing him, is Yoko. From a blind masseuse Shimamura hears the town's version: Komako and Yukio were engaged, and she became a geisha to pay his medical bills. He puts this to Komako, and she flatly denies it. Perhaps the teacher once hoped for it, she says, but there was never anything between her and Yukio. The harder she denies it, the more Shimamura feels something unspoken behind it. The novel never gives the answer: what really binds Komako, Yukio and Yoko, the reader can only guess, like Shimamura, through glass.

One afternoon Komako brings her samisen to his room to play for him. The piece is "Kanjinchō." Shimamura had expected a country geisha's pastime, but at the first notes he can hardly sit still; a chill runs from his spine to the soles of his feet. Komako has had no teacher for this. She practices alone, facing the valley, working from printed scores, and the empty valley is her audience. The air after snowfall is clean, and the music carries far into the mountains. Listening, he feels pinned down by something far stronger than himself.

It is also this winter that Shimamura, still under the quilts one morning, watches Komako doing her hair at the mirror stand. The depths of the mirror shine white: the snow outside. Floating in that snow are her bright red cheeks. The first mirror held Yoko's transparent face over moving dusk; this one holds Komako's burning face over motionless snow. One cold, one hot; one behind a train window, one beside his pillow. With these two mirrors the novel sets two women before Shimamura's eyes.

Act Three · The Station: She Will Not Go to a Dying Man

It is time for Shimamura to return to Tokyo, and Komako walks him to the station. They are talking in the waiting room when Yoko runs in, white-faced, calling to Komako: Yukio is failing, come back now.

Komako won't go. She says she doesn't want to watch someone die. Shimamura urges her: he wants to see you one last time. Still she doesn't move, insisting on seeing him onto his train, her face like someone in a sulk, or someone guarding something of her own. Yoko leaves. Shimamura boards and looks out through the window. He is behind glass again.

The scene is very short, but it is a knot in the book. To see off a man who is certain to leave, Komako gives up her last chance to see a man who perhaps truly loved her, or to whom she truly owed something. Seed ④ Why won't she go? Because she never loved Yukio and has no need to? Or because she did, and doesn't dare? Kawabata explains nothing.

After Shimamura leaves, Yukio dies.

Act Four · Autumn: Moths, Crepe, and a Wrong Word

When he comes a third time, it is autumn of the following year. This stay is long, from autumn into the first snow.

Autumn at the spa is full of dying insects. A moth clings to the window screen without moving, and only on looking closely does he realize it has long been dead. On the tatami of his room small insects flip onto their backs, legs trembling for a while, and then go still. He watches them and thinks quite clearly about the limits of a life, without the least sadness. He only watches.

Komako comes almost every night, often drunk from a banquet, stumbling in at midnight and hurrying out before dawn so as not to be seen. She knows he has a family, knows he will always go back, knows there is no future in this. And she keeps coming.

Yoko comes too. Since Yukio's death she visits his grave every day, and now she helps out at the inn where Shimamura is staying. Bringing him a message once, she talks with him briefly and suddenly pleads: take me to Tokyo. She also asks him to be good to Komako. Shimamura answers half joking, half in earnest. Her eyes are the same eyes as in the train window.

One night, with Komako beside him, Shimamura says offhandedly: you're a good woman. She stops, presses him on what he means, grows angrier the more she asks, and finally cries. What she hears is that to him she is just a "good" woman, convenient, a diversion. Shimamura himself cannot quite say what he meant. This one misheard sentence wounds more than any quarrel, because it shows them both the true face of the affair at once: she has handed over everything, and he cannot even give a single sentence a definite meaning.

Shimamura goes to a nearby town to see chijimi. Echigo chijimi is a crepe woven from hemp for summer kimonos. Traditionally, mountain girls wove it indoors through winter after winter of snow, then spread the finished cloth on the snow to bleach. The lore in this passage comes from Bokushi Suzuki's Edo-period chronicle of snow-country life, Hokuetsu Seppu. The old accounts say the finest cloth came from young girls who poured everything into it, cloth worn in summer by strangers its weavers would never meet. Shimamura thinks of Komako, and at last he understands something:

Komako loves him. Yet he regards that love as a beautiful waste, and the emptiness that can see another person's whole devotion as waste is his own. Payoff ② ↑ He first used that word a year before, about Komako's diaries, judging her from above. After a long circuit it has turned around and landed back on him. What is truly wasted is not the notebooks full of titles or the samisen practiced to an empty valley, but a man who will give nothing and believes he is appreciating everything.

He knows he must leave, for Komako's sake and for his own.

Act Five · Fire in the Cocoon Warehouse: The Milky Way Pours Down

One night after the first snow, the alarm bell rings in town: the cocoon warehouse is on fire. It is where the silkworm cocoons are stored, and that evening a film was being shown on its second floor, packed with people.

Komako and Shimamura run toward it together. Over the snowfield the Milky Way hangs frighteningly low, as if the whole of it were pouring down to wrap them up. Sparks fly upward and seem to scatter into it.

The cocoon warehouse burns. Payoff ③ ↑ Komako lived in a silkworm loft and Shimamura pictured her as a transparent silkworm; the final fire breaks out, of all places, in a building full of cocoons. A silkworm spins silk and seals itself inside it. Kawabata buried this thread in imagery: in the second act it looked like a mere comparison, and only here do we see it was leading to this fire all along.

At that moment a woman's body drops from the second floor. She falls flat, limp, without the least struggle, like a doll. It is Yoko.

Strangely, Shimamura feels neither danger nor horror, only that the moment is unreal, something happening in another world. What rises before his eyes is the train years ago: Yoko's face in the window mirror, the light from the fields kindling right in the middle of her eye. Payoff ① ↑ The light that "burned in her eye" in the book's very first image has become real fire. On first reading it was just a moment of Shimamura's aesthetic pleasure; only at the end do we see that Kawabata set fire in Yoko's eye on page one.

Komako is the first to reach her. She lifts Yoko from the ground and carries her back against her breast, like her sacrifice, or her punishment. Payoff ④ ↑ More than a year earlier at the station, she would not walk back to see a dying man; now she plunges through the crowd and gathers up the very girl who came running to call her that day. The same act, reversed: this time she does not hide. She cries out something like: this child is mad, she's mad.

Shimamura tries to get closer and staggers as the crowd shoves him. He looks up, and the Milky Way flows down into him with a roar.

There the novel ends. Whether Yoko lives or dies, we are not told. What becomes of Komako, we are not told. Whether Shimamura goes back to Tokyo, we are not told either.

Reduce this story to a single fuse: it is not a fire, and not an affair; it is a man choosing to be only the one who looks. That early-summer day, Shimamura sent Komako to fetch him a "real" geisha, placing her on the friendship side of a line so that he would owe her nothing. Later he called her diaries wasted and her love wasted, because only that let him stay behind the glass. The more Komako burns, the more he admires; the more he admires, the less possible it becomes for him to reach out. In the end the fire really does break out, and he still stands in the crowd, seeing only the Milky Way.

Three Slow-Motion Scenes

One · The Evening Mirror: Two Transparencies Laid Together

Replay it: the carriage lamps come on while there is still light outside. The glass holds two worlds at once: outside, mountains and fields under a darkening sky; inside, a girl's lowered profile. Both are transparent, neither hides the other. A light slides in from far away, passes straight through her eye, and slides off. Shimamura holds his breath for fear the picture will break.

Why does it work? First, point of view. Kawabata doesn't let Shimamura look at Yoko directly; he has him look at a reflection. Looking directly would be sizing up a strange woman; looking at a reflection becomes admiring a painting. Put distance in, and desire turns into aesthetics: that is Shimamura's entire secret, disclosed on page one. Second, the timing of information. We don't yet know who this girl is, who the sick man is, or how either relates to Komako, so the scene can only be taken as a pure image; only with the fire at the end do we look back and find the image was a prophecy. Third, rhythm: almost no dialogue, only shifting light, like one long take that never cuts.

Two · "Kanjinchō": A Country Geisha Overpowers Him

Replay it: a winter morning, just the two of them in the room. Komako spreads the score in front of her, tunes the strings, tries a few notes, and begins. Shimamura had come with a city man's faint condescension, expecting a bit of fun. But once the music starts he goes cold, his skin prickles, and he doesn't know where to put himself. Outside the window the snowy mountains are white, and the sound heads straight into them.

Why does it work? It is the one time in the book that Shimamura's place as the watcher breaks. He has been admiring Komako all along; this time what is hers overwhelms him, and he is the one struck. Kawabata writes almost nothing but his body's reactions here, cold, sweat, restlessness, rather than any comment on the music. His body surrenders, conceding that the practice he dismisses as "wasted" is truer than every essay on ballet he ever wrote on paper. The background is placed precisely, too: we already know she taught herself, facing the valley, working from scores, so the scene needn't explain why she plays like this. Solitude itself was her teacher.

Three · The Milky Way: At the Scene of Disaster, a Man Sees Beauty

Replay it: firelight, the alarm bell, shouting, jets from the hoses, snow. A woman falls flat from the second floor like a doll. Another woman runs in and picks her up. A man stands in the crowd, looks up, and the Milky Way flows into his body.

Why does it work? Because it is asymmetrical. The fire is the busiest place in the book, with death, screaming and a woman's collapse; yet what Kawabata gives Shimamura is a nearly serene sensation on the scale of the cosmos. The reader is forced to feel two things at once: this page is breathtakingly beautiful, and the beauty is being drawn from a girl's fall. Kawabata neither defends Shimamura nor judges him; he simply lays out, as it is, this capacity to see beauty in catastrophe, and leaves you to feel the chill yourself. The abrupt stop is a rhythm of its own: by not telling us whether Yoko lives, he keeps us from walking out of the picture.

The Book's Core Device

Snow Country is not a novel of invented worlds. Its core devices are three: a protagonist who only looks, two mirrors, and a technique of omission.

A protagonist who is a mirror. Kawabata later said, in substance, that Shimamura is not himself, that he is merely a mirror reflecting Komako. That remark is the key. Shimamura has almost no inner drama: he doesn't struggle, decide or blunder; he only comes and goes, again and again. Such a character is "flat," of course, but that is how the device works: the emptier he is, the more clearly Komako shows in him. Everything we know of her passes through his eyes, and those eyes are faulty in a specific way: they turn everything into pictures. So reading the book means watching Komako and, at the same time, watching the distortion in the mirror itself.

Two mirrors, two women. Yoko always appears through glass, voice or distance: a reflection in the train window, a sad call across the snow, a figure glimpsed down a corridor. She is barely a person, more a pure, unreachable thing. Komako is the opposite: she gets drunk, cries, flares up, arrives and leaves; she has body heat, bills and a body. Shimamura is drawn to Yoko and sleeps with Komako. The beauty he wants is the kind he cannot reach, and the kind he can have, he looks down on. When Komako lifts Yoko at the end, the two worlds collide.

Omission. Kawabata's boldest move is to leave things out. Did Komako love Yukio? What was Yoko to Yukio? What does Yoko feel toward Shimamura? What becomes of Komako? In a conventional novel these are plot that must be resolved; he leaves every one blank, the way a haiku gives two images and lets the reader build the bridge. Its origin as separate magazine pieces, each self-contained, also makes the book a chain of linked fragments rather than a single line. Some readers compare it to renga, linked verse, where the leap between one stanza and the next is where the poetry lives. That also explains the common complaint that "nothing happens": the plot isn't missing; it is hidden in the gaps the book jumps over.

What It Makes You Think

One · A man who never promises anything: honest, or cruel?

Shimamura never lies to Komako. He never says he'll divorce, never says he'll take her away, never says "I love you." He comes because he wants to; she always knows he will leave. She is an adult; it was she who came to his room at night. So what, exactly, does he owe her?

The case for Shimamura can be made strongly. In a world where men routinely sweet-talk geisha with lies, promising nothing is the cleanest course available. He doesn't exploit her hope or invent a future; he gives her everything he can give: a visit each year, his attention, his ear for her music, a sincere sense that she is beautiful. Her life has no other window, and his arrival is at least one. To demand that someone hand over what he doesn't have before he counts as decent is its own kind of tyranny.

The other side is just as strong. Not promising is not the same as not being responsible. Shimamura knows perfectly well that Komako is sinking deeper, that she turns away customers, stays up all night and drinks herself ill for him. Yet he comes year after year, sets her alight again each time, and leaves. He enjoys the sight of her burning, then calls the burning "wasted." That isn't honesty; it is a man using his honesty as an exemption. "I never lied to you" can be a true sentence and also the coldest one there is.

Where does the author stand? Kawabata hands down no verdict, but he turns the word "wasted" around and drops it on Shimamura, and that is a position in itself. Bring it home: a relationship both people know has no future, which neither will be first to name; a person who keeps saying "I never promised you anything." You have probably seen this in someone else, and maybe in yourself.

Two · Waste: a diary no one reads, music played to a valley. Is that a life thrown away?

A country geisha keeps a diary for ten years and records the characters of a hundred-odd novels, and no one ever reads it. Every day she practices samisen to an empty valley, with no teacher and no listener. She loves a man who comes for a few days a year and is bound to leave. By any standard of usefulness, it is waste.

The side that calls it waste isn't heartless, only clear-eyed. A life is finite; pouring energy where nothing comes back is pouring your life, bit by bit, into sand. Had Komako turned that tenacity toward leaving the town and making a way for herself, she might not be passing out drunk at banquets in her early twenties. Grieving for her is more respectful than praising her. Praise for "the beauty of futility" is so often a spectator's luxury, because the spectator isn't the one who pays.

The other side: what counts as not wasted? Shimamura's ballet essays did get published, yet they come from a man who has never seen a ballet and writes from photographs, far hollower than Komako's diaries. Her music is real, real enough to make a city man shiver; her diary is her testimony to her own life, and in a place where no one takes her seriously, she takes herself seriously. Perhaps the purest part of a life is precisely the part that earns nothing back. The chijimi was woven for others to wear, by hands that never met them, and the devotion really is in the cloth.

Where does the author stand? Kawabata is plainly enthralled by the beauty of futility, yet he lets Shimamura finally see that the one who calls others' devotion waste is the empty one. Bring it home: the notebook you never mean to show anyone, the craft that leads to no certificate, the attachment you know will come to nothing. How do you keep accounts for these?

Three · Seeing beauty in someone else's suffering: a kind of love, or a kind of theft?

At the end, a girl falls from a burning building, another woman runs in screaming, and Shimamura looks up and feels the Milky Way flow into him. It is the book's most beautiful page, and its most uncomfortable.

The case for the aesthetic gaze: being able to see beauty in any situation is a rare quality of attention, and attention is itself a form of respect. The way Shimamura gazes at Yoko's eye, Komako's cheek, the death of a moth, the labor behind a bolt of crepe is more serious than the way most people look at those around them. Seeing someone's pain as beautiful doesn't necessarily cheapen it; tragedy is an art form precisely because human beings need beauty to bear what cannot be borne. Kawabata, who buried his entire family as a child, may have survived by exactly this ability to look through glass.

The other side: the beauty has a price, and the one who pays is always the one being looked at. When Yoko falls, Shimamura "feels no danger." That isn't serenity, it is indifference; while Komako holds Yoko, he is the only one in the crowd taking in the view. To convert others' suffering into your own aesthetic experience is to turn them from people into pictures, and pictures don't hurt. Today we scroll past a photo from a war zone or a clip of a disaster and think "how striking" before we think whose home it was. Shimamura rehearsed that reflex for us long ago.

Where does the author stand? Hard to say. Kawabata writes the scene so beautifully that readers can't help taking Shimamura's place, and that may be the mirror he set up for us. If you find this page beautiful, you are standing behind the same pane of glass as Shimamura.

Motifs: What Kawabata Kept Writing

Kawabata keeps returning to roughly three things: a lonely, somewhat hollow man; a young, pure, usually unreachable female figure; and the chill of beauty pressed up against death.

In "The Dancing Girl of Izu," an orphaned high-school student travels for a few days with a troupe of itinerant performers, adores the young dancer, and weeps on the boat home, a feeling so clean it scarcely has a body. That is the youngest, most innocent link in the chain. Snow Country goes a step further: the man is no longer a boy, the body is present, the promise is gone, and purity has been split in two, the reachable Komako and the unreachable Yoko. In the postwar Thousand Cranes and The Sound of the Mountain the men are older, their desire folded into the tea ceremony, a Noh mask, a daughter-in-law's face. By the late House of the Sleeping Beauties, old men pay to lie in a secret room beside drugged girls, doing nothing, only looking and remembering. "Look but don't touch" has reached its end and become something close to horror. Seen on this line, Snow Country sits in the middle: Shimamura's looking still carries beauty and sorrow and hasn't turned into a sickness, but you can already see where it will go.

The other line is "the beauty of Japan." Accepting the Nobel Prize in 1968, Kawabata titled his lecture "Japan, the Beautiful and Myself," opening with poems by Zen monks such as Dōgen and Myōe and speaking of snow, moon and blossoms, and of emptiness. The snow, hot springs, samisen and chijimi of Snow Country later became a standard set of images of "Japan" for Western readers, a point taken up below.

Controversies and Criticism

Women as objects of the gaze. The most common and most forceful criticism: Komako and Yoko exist only in Shimamura's sight, one a body to possess, the other a pure phantom, and neither has a point of view of her own. Komako's economic situation, a country geisha living by pouring drinks and sleeping with clients, is rendered beautifully, and the beauty also covers the cruelty of that living. Kawabata's defenders say the book is exactly an exposure of the hollowness of Shimamura's gaze, and that Komako's vitality overflows its frame. Critics reply that the exposure is carried out with the same eyes, and what readers finally remember is still "the red cheeks in the snow." Both have a point.

"No plot," and flat characters. Shimamura is nearly hollow, and the story barely advances by cause and effect; many first-time readers feel nothing happens. Opinions divide: admirers call it a novel written in haiku, where the blank is the content; detractors say the flood of sensation covers up character dilemmas the author would rather not face.

Translation and packaged "Japaneseness." Edward G. Seidensticker's 1956 English translation made the book famous in the West and was one of the main routes to Kawabata's Nobel. The celebrated first line posed an immediate problem: the Japanese has no grammatical subject and includes the word for the provincial border; the English supplies "the train" as subject and drops the border, so the image shifts from a person passing through to a train coming out. Choices like that have been debated in English for decades. The larger argument: does the reticence, the snowscapes and the geisha of Snow Country simply feed Western fantasies of a "mysterious East"? When Kenzaburō Ōe accepted the Nobel in 1994, he titled his lecture "Japan, the Ambiguous, and Myself," a deliberate echo of Kawabata's, criticizing the habit of presenting Japan as a mystical, vague beauty. That much is verifiable fact; who is right is left to the reader.

The author's death. In 1972 Kawabata died of gas inhalation in his workroom in Zushi, leaving no note; it is generally taken to be suicide. Many readers like to link this to the aesthetics of death in his books, but that is the readers' association, not anything Kawabata himself left behind.

Crossovers

  • Philosophy · Kant's "disinterested pleasure": In the Critique of Judgment, Kant describes aesthetic judgment as pleasure free of the wish to possess, attending only to an object's form, indifferent to whether it serves us. Shimamura's gaze has exactly this shape, wanting only the picture behind the glass. Where it differs: Kant has in mind judging a flower, while Shimamura shares a bed with the person he admires, and "disinterest" becomes, in him, an excuse for owing no consequences.
  • Psychology · Adult attachment theory: In 1987 Hazan and Shaver applied Bowlby's attachment theory to adult lovers, and later research often describes "anxious" and "avoidant" partners attracting and intensifying each other: one pursues, the other withdraws. Komako and Shimamura move almost in that pattern; but the novel offers no diagnosis. Kawabata writes a man choosing to stay behind glass, not a personality type.
  • Sociology · Emotional labor: In The Managed Heart (1983), Arlie Hochschild studied flight attendants and coined "emotional labor," work that requires you to produce certain feelings for customers. A geisha's trade is exactly such work; Komako's tragedy is that what the job requires her to perform and what she truly gives have grown together until even she cannot separate them.
  • Buddhism · Impermanence and non-attachment: Buddhism teaches that all conditioned things are impermanent and urges letting go of attachment. Shimamura watches moths die and Komako burn without a ripple, which looks very much like non-attachment. But Buddhist letting go is compassion that follows from seeing impermanence clearly; Shimamura's is avoidance, for fear of being hurt. Same shape, opposite direction.

Ten Lines to Keep

② A finger that remembers a woman: the phrase itself tells you what he kept was only a touch.

③ He sent her to fetch a "real" geisha to keep her on the side of the line where he owed nothing.

④ One hundred and ninety-nine days: she counts the days; he counts only seasons.

⑤ A man who never saw a ballet writes about ballet; a man who never gave himself away talks about love.

⑥ Music learned facing an empty valley is truer than anything he ever wrote.

⑦ Two mirrors: in one, a transparency he cannot reach; in the other, a fire he can.

⑧ She would not go back to a dying man, then ran into a fire to lift a falling girl: one woman, two choices.
