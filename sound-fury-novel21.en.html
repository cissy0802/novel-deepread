The Sound and the Fury

William Faulkner · 1929

Beyond a fence there is a stretch of grass, and out on it men are swinging at something. The gaps between the palings slide past one at a time; the men stoop, straighten, move away.
He follows them along the fence until the fence turns and he cannot follow any farther, and then he stands holding the wire, watching.
Somebody out there calls: caddie.
He begins to cry. He is thirty-three years old and he has been crying for thirty of them, and nobody knows what about. That grass used to be his pasture; it was sold. And the word those men keep shouting happens to be his sister's name.

This page contains complete spoilers—including why Quentin drowns himself on 2 June 1910, what Caddy does and where she ends up, what is eventually done to Benjy's body, who takes Jason's money, and which side of the monument the last carriage goes around. Nothing in this novel becomes available until you know the ending: Faulkner told the same story four times because he was betting you would read it twice. If you want the suspense, read the book first—this page will wait.

In one sentence

A rotting Southern family pours all its love, terror and rage into its only daughter, Caddy: she loses her virginity, gets pregnant, is married off in a hurry, divorced, and erased from the house—after which her three brothers each go mad in a different key, one without language who can only howl, one who walks into a river, one who converts the rest of his life into an invoice; and the novel's method is to let the three of them each narrate a single day, fail, and then hand Easter Sunday to a Black servant who is not family, so that somebody can carry the story out.

Coordinates

William Faulkner (1897–1962) came from Oxford, Mississippi, barely left it, and built on paper an entire invented county—Yoknapatawpha, county seat Jefferson—complete with a hand-drawn map. Most of his major fiction happens there, and The Sound and the Fury is the book where the whole apparatus first stands up.

It was his fourth novel, published in 1929, and almost nobody bought it. He said afterward that by the time he wrote it he had stopped expecting a publisher at all, and so wrote it for himself—and that freedom, the freedom of assuming no reader, produced one of the most difficult and most reckless novels in English. He won the Nobel Prize in 1949. Of everything he wrote, this remained his favorite, and he called it his "splendid failure": four attempts, and he still never got the story told.

The title comes from Macbeth: life is a tale told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, signifying nothing. Faulkner took that line apart and used it as architecture—the first section really is told by an idiot.

The story

First the shape of the book, or nothing else makes sense. Four sections, each headed by a date, and the dates are out of order:

What follows retells everything in the order it actually happened. The book gives you fragments, and the fragments are the point—but to talk about the novel you first have to put them back together.

Act one · 1898: a pair of muddy drawers

The Compsons of Jefferson once produced a governor and a general; what is left is a big house, a pasture, and a household of people keeping up appearances. The father is an educated drunk who sees through everything and does nothing. The mother has one occupation—lying in a darkened room complaining that she has not been treated well, and reminding everyone that she was born a Bascomb, which is better than a Compson. There are four children: Quentin, the eldest; Candace—Caddy; Jason; and Benjy, severely cognitively disabled from birth and without speech for his entire life. He was named Maury, after his mother's brother, until at five it could no longer be pretended he was normal and she insisted on a change: she would not have her family's name attached to an idiot. He became Benjamin. seed ①

One evening in 1898 the grandmother—the children call her Damuddy—dies upstairs. The adults push all four children out to the branch behind the house so they will not see. Caddy takes off her dress and wades in, soaking and muddying her underwear; then, wanting to know what is happening upstairs, she climbs the pear tree by the house and presses her face to the window. The three boys stand below, looking up, and what they can see is the mud on the seat of her drawers.

Faulkner said in interviews that the entire novel grew out of that one image. It is the posture of the whole book: Caddy above, looking straight at birth and death; three men below, looking at the dirt on her, and spending their lives explaining it. She is also, at that age, the only one of the four with any courage—she carries Benjy, warms his hands, will not let anyone laugh at him in his hearing. Benjy cannot tell people apart, but he can tell smells: Caddy smells like trees. seed ② The phrase recurs all through his section; it is the most important item in his tiny vocabulary.

Act two · 1909–1910: sell the pasture, marry off the daughter

The ruin is very concrete. To send Quentin to Harvard for one year and to give Caddy a respectable wedding, the Compsons sell the pasture to the golf club next door. That field was Benjy's territory. After it is sold he still stands at the fence every day, holding the wire. seed ③

Meanwhile Caddy, seventeen and then eighteen, starts going out. There are several men in town, and finally Dalton Ames. On the day she loses her virginity she comes home and Benjy blocks the door, sniffs at her, and begins to bawl—he cannot register anything else, only that the smell of trees is gone. payoff ② ↑ Up to that point "Caddy smelled like trees" reads like a slow child's verbal tic, and most readers file it under limited vocabulary; here it turns into the one instrument in the book that cannot lie. Faulkner can neither let Benjy understand chastity nor let him describe it, so he converts the entire event into the presence or absence of a smell. Benjy shoves his sister toward the bathroom, wanting it washed off. It does not wash off. The only person in this house without language is the only one who knows immediately what has happened.

Quentin's response is a different sickness. He finds Caddy, holds his knife to her throat, says they will die together; she does not move, and his hand goes slack. He confronts Dalton Ames, who takes out a pistol and shoots two pieces of bark off the river's surface just to demonstrate his aim; Quentin swings, is held off almost gently, and faints—the thing he will never forgive himself for.

Then he goes to his father and says the child is mine, I committed incest with her. It is a lie. He wants the mess promoted into a sin so enormous that he and Caddy would be sealed inside it together, permanently, with no one else admitted. His father sees through it instantly: you cannot even lie well; what you cannot bear is not what she did but that you did nothing; and in any case time will flatten all of it—everything gets diluted, including whatever is killing you now. To Quentin that consolation is the worst thing he has ever heard. Not being diluted is precisely what he wants.

Caddy is pregnant and needs a husband fast. The candidate is Herbert Head, a handsome young Indiana banker who was once thrown out of Harvard for cheating. The April 1910 wedding is lavish. At it, Herbert claps Jason on the shoulder and promises him a place in his bank. seed ④ Jason files that sentence away as a deed of title to his own life.

Within a year Herbert works out that the child is not his and puts Caddy out. Mrs. Compson's solution is to delete her daughter's name from the house: no one is permitted to say "Caddy" in her presence again. The baby is brought home and named Quentin—the name of the uncle who has just drowned himself, given to an illegitimate girl.

Act three · June 2, 1910: a man at war with a watch

This is the densest and most beautiful section: Quentin's last day in Cambridge, from waking to dusk. The reader does not at first know this is a suicide note, but every event is walking toward it.

The first thing he hears on waking is his watch ticking. It was his grandfather's; his father handed it over with a speech whose gist is: I give you this mausoleum not so you will remember time but so you can forget it now and then, and not spend all your breath trying to conquer it, because no battle is ever won. seed ⑤ Quentin's first act of the day is to break the crystal and twist the hands off. The watch keeps running. All day he dodges the chimes of the hour and steps around his own shadow—the other clock, the one the sun keeps on him—treating time as something that is trying to kill him.

He buys two flatirons, heavy, wrapped in paper. He rides a streetcar out of town and drifts. He picks up a small Italian girl who has no English and will not stop following him; he buys her a bun, calls her sister, and walks her from door to door looking for her home—he is taking a lost little sister back where she belongs, because his own went missing years ago and he failed to stop it. Her brother arrives, accuses him of abduction, and Quentin is hauled before a justice of the peace and fined six dollars. Classmates driving past get him out of it. One of them, Gerald Bland, is boasting about how he handles women; Quentin interrupts to ask whether he has a sister, then swings at him and is beaten bloody. What he is thinking about while he gets up is a different fight: the riverbank, Dalton Ames, those two shots. Everything he does on his last day is an attempt to redo something he once failed at, and he fails again.

Back in his room he washes off the blood, changes, brushes his teeth, packs his trunk neatly, and writes two letters—one to his father, one to be delivered to his roommate tomorrow. He leaves no explanation. At dusk he goes out toward the bridge. Only now does the reader learn what the flatirons are for. payoff ⑤ ↑ His father had said: don't spend yourself trying to conquer time. That is exactly what the whole day has been—first the hands twisted off, then iron tied to the body and the body into the river, stopping time at his end of it. The elegance is that the ruined watch, still ticking since morning, had already written the ending: he can break it but he cannot stop it, and the only thing he can stop is himself.

Act four · April 6, 1928: Jason's ledger

Eighteen years pass. The father drinks himself to death in 1912. In 1913 Benjy gets out through a gate somebody left open and rushes at some schoolgirls going home, trying to speak—the one thing he can hold in mind is that Caddy used to come home along that road. Nobody reads it that way. He is taken away and castrated, and after that the gate stays locked. payoff ① ↑ At five they took his name away, because an idiot should not wear an uncle's name; at eighteen they took his capacity to reproduce, because an idiot should not have descendants. This family's method with a problem never changes: not to solve it, but to cut one more thing off him.

Jason is head of the house now. He clerks in a farm-supply store and hates the job, the town, his mother, his dead brother, and everyone who drives better than he does. The first line of his section finishes the portrait:

"Once a bitch always a bitch, what I say."— Jason, opening line of section three

Why so much hate? His own account is perfectly clear: a position was taken from him. The pasture was sold to buy Quentin one year at Harvard, after which Quentin jumped off a bridge; the wedding was thrown, and then Caddy voided the marriage that came with his job. payoff ④ ↑ Everyone else forgot that hand on the shoulder at the wedding; he has been compounding interest on it for eighteen years. The maddest person in this book is not the one without language. It is the one who keeps the clearest accounts.

His revenge is a long-term business. After Caddy is expelled she sends money every month for her daughter; Jason cashes the checks into his own strongbox and hands his mother a forged one of the same amount, then watches that proud old woman burn it—she believes she is refusing her fallen daughter's dirty money, and is in fact countersigning her son's embezzlement. He runs this for fifteen years. There is worse: once Caddy sneaks back into town and begs on her knees for a look at her child, offering a hundred dollars; he agrees, tells her where to stand, and has the carriage driven past at a gallop. She gets one second of a baby.

The 1928 day is Good Friday. Jason spends it doing three things: raging at the store; fighting the New York cotton futures market by telegram (cursing the "Eastern Jews" for cheating him while pushing his savings deeper in); and chasing his seventeen-year-old niece Quentin, who has skipped school and is around town with a man in a red tie from the traveling show. He drives all over Jefferson after her, the gasoline fumes bringing on his headache, hand pressed to his temple, and loses her. That evening he takes two free show passes out of his pocket and feeds them into the stove one at a time in front of Luster, the Black boy who has spent the whole day hunting for a nickel to buy a ticket. It is the only thing he succeeds at all day.

Act five · April 8, 1928: Easter, and a carriage that turns the wrong way

The last section has no "I." Faulkner pulls the camera out of everyone's head and photographs the house from the outside for the first time—and you discover that the house which loomed like a universe for three hundred pages is a peeling, sagging wreck. The day begins with Dilsey, the family's cook and servant, old now, her husband and children and grandchildren all having worked this yard. She opens the door into a gray light with cold rain in it, builds the fire, makes breakfast, and answers the voice calling down from upstairs.

Then Miss Quentin's door is opened: gone, window standing wide, the pear tree just outside it—the same tree her mother climbed in 1898—and a ladder left leaning against the wall. The mother climbed down that tree into the world; the daughter climbs out of it. She has taken the money from Jason's locked box: nearly seven thousand dollars, skimmed over fifteen years from the support checks meant for her, plus his own savings.

And here is the beautiful part of the arithmetic: Jason cannot report the theft. To report it he would have to explain where the money came from, and the money was the girl's to begin with. So he drives after her himself, reaches the next town, picks the wrong wagon at the show, gets driven off by an old man with a hatchet, and finally—too dizzy to drive—pays a Black man to take him home to Jefferson. The man who had the whole world entered in his ledger is settled with, once, by his niece, using his own books, and recovers nothing.

The same morning Dilsey takes Benjy to the Black church for Easter service. Her daughter is embarrassed—what does it look like, bringing a white idiot here—and Dilsey shuts her down: God does not care whether he is smart. The visiting preacher, Reverend Shegog, has come from Saint Louis; he is small, unimpressive, and opens in a white man's voice, and the congregation's hopes drop. Then partway through, the voice changes—another instrument, another language—and the church begins to rock and answer him, and people weep. What he preaches is a son nailed up and a mother watching him die. In four hundred pages this is the only time grief is said out loud and caught by other people. Dilsey comes out crying and lets the tears run without wiping them, and when asked what is wrong she says:

"I seed de beginnin, en now I sees de endin."— Dilsey, section four

She saw the Compsons begin and she is watching them end. The only person in this book entitled to the whole view is not a member of the family.

The last scene: Luster drives the old surrey, with Benjy in it, toward the cemetery, past the Confederate soldier on his monument in the square. For thirty years that carriage has gone around the monument to the right. Today Luster shows off and turns left. Benjy erupts into a howl with no rise or fall in it, inhuman, and the whole street stops. Jason comes out of nowhere, hits Luster across the face, grabs the reins, and hauls the horse back onto the old line.

Then Benjy goes quiet. Rooflines and shopfronts, trees, windows and doorways slide past him smoothly again, every one of them back where it belongs. payoff ③ ↑ What he wanted was never the sold pasture; it was sequence. Men beyond the fence shout the word that sounds like his sister, and he cries; a carriage turns the wrong side of a statue, and he cries. He has no memory, only order—and after this family took the sister, the pasture, his name and his body away from him one at a time, "things must be where they were" is all he has left. The last image in the novel is an idiot who stops screaming because the world has been briefly put back the way it was. That is the whole of the comfort Faulkner can offer these people.

Set the five acts side by side and nothing is accidental: every collapse hangs on the same fuse—dirt on a girl. The muddy drawers of 1898 become a real loss of virginity in 1909; the pasture is sold for it, Quentin gets his one Harvard year and his two flatirons, Jason loses the bank job and converts his life into an invoice, Benjy cries at a word shouted across a fence, and Miss Quentin is born carrying a dead man's name and escapes down the same pear tree. Caddy appears only inside other people's memories, never says a full paragraph, and is the reason for every breath drawn in four hundred pages.

Three slow-motion replays

1 · Benjy's opening: sensation now, meaning two hundred pages later

The first sentence tells you only that men are hitting something on the far side of a fence. Thirty pages on you work out that it is a golf course; much later you learn why the land was the family's, why it was sold, and why he is standing there crying. The technique has a name—delayed decoding: give the raw sensory material, withhold the explanation.

Why is it worth it? Because it forces the reader to become Benjy. His condition is "sees everything, understands nothing," and the confusion you feel reading his section is not a typesetting accident—it is his twenty-four-hour experience relocated into you. When you finally assemble the fact that caddie and Caddy are the same sound, you have not received a piece of information; you have had an experience of empathy—you have said, on his behalf, the thing he will never be able to say.

Technically Faulkner uses one switch: italics. Time jumps, typeface changes. He originally wanted different colored inks for different years and his publisher could not afford it. Part of this novel's meaning is carried by its typography rather than by its sentences.

2 · Quentin's watch: nailing an abstraction to an object you can hold

"A man cannot endure the passage of time" is a philosophical proposition, and written badly it is hot air. Faulkner's solution is to convert it into a watch. The father's nihilism—time flattens everything, so stop fighting—is handed over as an heirloom; the son's answer is to twist off the hands; the watch runs anyway, so that every few pages for the rest of the day the reader is reminded by a ticking, handless object. By the time he stops at the jeweler's window full of clocks all showing different hours and asks whether any of them is right, you need no interior monologue to know this man is finished. Reducing an abstract proposition to something that fits in a pocket and keeps ticking is the single most teachable move in the book.

Then the Italian child. Faulkner does not let Quentin sit by the water remembering his sister; he lets him find a substitute in the world—a lost girl with no shared language who needs walking home. He calls her sister all afternoon and the reader knows exactly who she is. And he is arrested for that tenderness: a man trying to protect a sister, charged with molesting a child—the cruelest and most accurate summary the book makes of him. His love was shaped like a crime from the start.

3 · The church: letting sound do the plot's work

Shegog arrives as a disappointment: small, and speaking in a white man's cadence. Faulkner flattens expectation first, then lets the voice deform inside the prose—pitch, syntax and vocabulary sinking level by level until preaching has become lamentation, the congregation's responses driving in like a drumbeat, pulpit and pews fused into one thing. The reason this has to come in section four is that the first three sections demonstrate three kinds of inarticulacy: Benjy has no language, Quentin's language is entirely inward and self-strangling, Jason's is only for reckoning and insult. None of them can get the pain out. Not until Faulkner gives the floor to an old woman whose name is not on the Compson family tree, in a borrowed church, does the thing finally get said—not by argument, but in an idiom a white narrator cannot follow and can still weep at.

Put it beside the closing carriage: on one side grief fully expressed; on the other an idiot who stops howling because a route was corrected. Those are Faulkner's two endings—the real redemption happens outside this family's door, and the people inside get order instead.

The central device: four tellings of one story, and why it had to be built this way

The signature of this novel is not a premise; it is the structure itself. Faulkner's own account: he wrote it once (Benjy) and it wasn't clear; wrote it again (Quentin) and it still wasn't; a third time (Jason), still no; and finally stepped in and told it himself—and the four together still failed, which is why he called it a splendid failure. That sounds like modesty and is actually the operating manual. Laid out:

The decisive fact is the empty row: Caddy gets no section of her own. This is the boldest decision in the book. She is everyone's center, every page is about her, and she never once holds the narration—she exists only as other people's remembered images: a wet dress, a smell of trees, a fit of defiance, a hand mailing checks.

Faulkner's explanation was that Caddy was too beautiful for him to write directly, and he would only look at her reflection in others. It is a moving line and it is also the origin of the book's biggest fight. Technically the decision works: a character who never appears gets rinsed through three contradictory descriptions until she becomes an image no reader can dismantle. The person you remember most clearly after closing the book is the only one who never spoke a paragraph.

One more layer that often goes unnoticed: the four sections are ordered by difficulty, not chronology—hardest to easiest, which is nearly the reverse of how novels work. The bet is that you grope in the dark first and get light handed back a layer at a time, so that the relief of section four, where you can simply see, is itself part of the content. The cost is that a great many readers quit at page thirty. He took the bet, and the first edition did not sell.

And one honest label: Benjy's section is a literary device, not a clinical record. A consciousness without language could not render light, smell and sequence with that syntactic precision; the syntax and vocabulary are Faulkner's, with the explanatory words stripped out. It is a translation, not a recording.

What it forces you to think about

Question 1
Giving the novel's center no voice at all: is that the highest respect, or the most complete dispossession?

The defense is strong without relying on the author's own line. First, the form enacts the content: Caddy's actual position in that society is "discussed, disposed of, deleted, never consulted"—her mother forbids her name, her brother defines her chastity for her, her other brother pockets her support payments, even her daughter is named by someone else. Denying her narration is not an oversight; it transplants her social position into the book's architecture. Second, it is a form of protection: everyone Faulkner puts inside stream of consciousness gets seen through completely. Caddy is the only figure never opened up, and therefore the only one not pinned down by the projections three men lay on her.

The other side is just as hard. You can read the novel straight through as three men mourning a woman's body for four hundred pages, and mourning themselves. Quentin's grief is not that his sister suffered but that the family's "honor"—concretely, her virginity—was not preserved; Jason's rage is not that his sister's life collapsed but that it cost him a job. Everything Caddy actually lived through (what an unmarried pregnancy meant in the 1910 South, being put out by a husband, losing her child, buying one second of that child a decade later for a hundred dollars) happens offstage, because the book is not interested in that; it is interested in what those events stirred up in men. And "she was too beautiful to let her speak" is an ugly sentence when applied to an actual person: I value you too much to let you open your mouth. In the 1945 appendix, Caddy's last known location is a photograph taken in occupied France—seated beside a German general, expressionless, beautiful. Even her ending is a picture somebody else took.

The same question appears in another key with Benjy: Faulkner borrows his eyes, fits them with his own syntax, and has a man who cannot contradict him supply the book's most honest testimony. How far apart, in effect, are a voice completely borrowed and a person completely silenced? Bring it home: every family has someone who is discussed constantly and invited to speak never, and about whom the household holds a remarkably unanimous, remarkably confident, entirely untested account of what he really thinks. The authority of speaking for someone comes from their inability to object. This novel turns that mechanism into a work of art, and works of art do not have to be verified—which is both its achievement and the reason to keep a little of your skepticism after the last page.

Question 2
Jason is the only Compson who gets up and goes to work. Is it fair that the book makes him the villain?

Argue for Jason, better than he could argue for himself. The father drank himself to death, the brother drowned himself, the youngest needs watching, the mother is in bed performing victimhood, the sister ran off and left a daughter to raise—and out of that entire household, exactly one person gets up early to do work he hates so everybody eats. Readers who despise him as vulgar are enjoying a Quentin-shaped luxury: a man can die for honor provided somebody else in the family is earning. And what was taken from him is real: the pasture went to fund a brother who lasted one year and jumped; the wedding went ahead and cost him the bank. He is the only person in this family who received nothing and inherited all the debt, and half his cruelty is the interest on it.

Now nail him down. None of that explains what he actually does: the forged checks his mother burns with her own hands, fifteen years of a child's support money in his pocket, a mother charged a hundred dollars for one second of her daughter at a gallop, show tickets burned one at a time in front of a boy who spent the day hunting for a nickel. That last is the worst, because there is no profit in it—it is done purely so another person will be disappointed. Grievance cannot be exchanged for a license against someone weaker; that conversion does not clear on any ledger. And his accounting is wrong anyway: what he mourns is the life he was supposed to have, and that life was underwritten by selling land, trading a daughter into marriage, and a collapsing old order—he is billing a world that never owed him.

Worth pausing on: the three brothers are three versions of one illness—none of them can accept that Caddy grew up and acquired a body and a life. Benjy uses howling, Quentin uses death, Jason uses money. Faulkner does not let any of them be nobler than the others; he simply lets the best accountant outlive the rest. And in life, the capable, hardworking one who holds the household together is often also the meanest person in it—the combination is not rare, and frequently has a single cause.

Question 3
Can an elegy for a family built on slavery hold together as an elegy?

This is the least comfortable layer and the one that most needs saying. The Compson land, house and gentility have a documented origin: the plantation economy of the slaveholding South. And the people working in that house in 1928 are the Gibsons—Dilsey, her husband, her children and grandchildren, generations of them cooking and driving in that yard. The Compson decline is written from inside and with enormous pathos; the Gibsons are written from outside throughout. They appear when the family requires them, and their interiors stay shut.

The defense is genuine: there is not one sentence in this book that flatters the old South. Its aristocrats are drunks, hypochondriacs, suicides, embezzlers, and a castrated man; that famous code of honor is what kills Quentin, deforms Caddy, and turns Jason into a small cruelty machine. Handing section four to Dilsey's day is itself a verdict: the only thing still standing in this house is the person the house used up. And in the 1945 appendix, where everyone else gets a long entry, the Gibsons get two words—"They endured."—which in Faulkner's private hierarchy is the highest praise available.

The counter is equally real. "Endurance" as an accolade is a problem in itself: it defines virtue as the capacity to absorb injustice rather than to refuse it. Dilsey is written loving, tough, devout, uncomplaining—very nearly the textbook version of the noble old servant—and her function is to supply the book's moral coordinates and a bass line under a white family's collapse. What does she want? Has she ever hated anyone? The book does not ask. Writing a Black character as a moral standard is nearer than it looks to writing one as a prop: neither version requires her to have an interior. The racial slurs and casual contempt in the text come mostly from Jason's mouth, and Faulkner is plainly indicting him with them—but some of it is also the narrative habit of the period, and there is no reason to cover for it while reading.

Bring it forward: nostalgia for an order that rested on somebody else's pain is a mistake almost everyone makes. The old rules back home, the way the company used to be, that era when people still looked after each other—a portion of the warmth being remembered was carried by people who had no choice about carrying it. This novel is remarkable because it writes that nostalgia well enough to make you cry while laying the invoice on the same page. Its limit is that it never quite draws the faces of the people named on the invoice.

The obsession Faulkner kept writing

Line his books up and one sentence keeps recurring in different forms: the past does not pass. He wrote its most quoted version in another book—

"The past is never dead. It's not even past."— Requiem for a Nun (1951), not this novel

The Sound and the Fury is the purest experiment in it: Quentin's time stops in 1910, Benjy has no concept of time at all, Jason lives inside an eighteen-year-old unpaid debt—none of the three is in 1928.

Seven years later, Absalom, Absalom! (1936) drags Quentin back: not yet dead, sitting in a Harvard room telling his Canadian roommate the story of an old Mississippi planter and gradually telling his own case history instead; at the end the roommate asks whether he hates the South and Quentin says "I dont hate it" over and over—too fast, too many times, which is what hatred looks like. The same move takes a different shape in As I Lay Dying (1930): a family hauling their mother's coffin across the county for days, fifteen narrators taking turns, and the figure at the center is dead—another center that cannot speak, a formal variant of Caddy. By Light in August (1932) and Go Down, Moses (1942), the past that refuses to pass has been given plainer names: bloodline, land title, and the debt left by slavery.

There is a quieter obsession too: he always gives the last section to whoever has the least standing to speak. In Faulkner's world the articulate are in charge of ruining things, and redemption belongs to the people who only work.

Criticism and controversy

  • It is hard, and deliberately so. The standard complaint is that the novel locks most readers out and the first fifty pages are barely readable; asked what a reader who can't follow it should do, Faulkner said read it four times. You may call that fidelity to form, or call it outsourcing the author's job to the audience. The first edition's dismal sales are a matter of record.
  • Caddy's silence. The densest point of feminist criticism: desire, sin and beauty are all projected onto a female body that is never permitted to explain itself. The defense is that this is precisely the mechanism being exhibited. Neither side is a straw man.
  • Benjy and disability. The word "idiot" runs from the title through the text, and Benjy's plot function is to symbolize the family's decay and to be disposed of (renamed, castrated, finally shouted quiet). Writing a disabled character as an emblem of moral purity is its own way of turning a person into an abstraction—and the celebrated lyricism of that section belongs entirely to the author's syntax: a translation, not a recording.
  • Race and Dilsey. The enduring-Black-servant figure has been examined critically for decades. One relevant fact: Toni Morrison wrote her master's thesis on Faulkner and Woolf, spoke repeatedly about how much he mattered to her, and understood the limits of this mode as well as anyone. The two things are not in conflict.
  • The author's own politics. In 1956 Faulkner published a letter to the North in Life urging that integration "go slow," which drew criticism from Black writers and civil-rights figures; the same year, in an interview, he said that if it came to conflict between Mississippi and the United States he would fight for Mississippi, even to the point of shooting Black people in the street. The remarks caused an uproar; he afterward said he had been misquoted and that he had been drinking. These are verifiable public records, stated here without a verdict.
  • The 1945 appendix. For The Portable Faulkner he wrote a Compson genealogy running from 1699 to 1945, accounting for everyone's fate, and for a while considered it the key to the whole book. The trouble is that it contradicts the novel on several details, and it fixes Caddy's ending as that photograph with the German general—which many readers feel closes off something the novel deliberately left open. Editions handle it differently: some print it first, some last, some omit it. Which Sound and the Fury you have read depends on a decision an editor made for you.

Ten sentences to take away

1 · The whole book grew from one image: a girl climbing a tree to look at death, and three brothers below who can only see the mud on her drawers.

2 · Caddy never speaks a full paragraph and is the reason for every breath in four hundred pages—the book's biggest technical gamble and its biggest quarrel.

3 · Benjy is not crying for a pasture; he is crying for sequence. He has no memory, only order, and order is all this family left him.

4 · Quentin twists the hands off the watch and the watch keeps running; the only clock he can stop is himself, and the flatirons have been waiting since morning.

5 · "Time dilutes everything" is offered by the father as comfort and received by the son as a death sentence.

6 · Jason is the only one in the house who works and the only villain in it; those two facts sit in one man without contradiction, and often cause each other.

7 · The three brothers are one illness in three keys—none can accept that their sister grew up—expressed as howling, as suicide, and as money.

8 · The single moment when grief is spoken aloud and caught by other people happens outside the Compson house, in a Black church.

9 · "They endured" is the highest praise Faulkner had to give, and the line in this book most worth interrogating: why should endurance be the virtue?

10 · The last page is not redemption but order: the carriage swings back to the right, the storefronts return to their places, and the idiot stops crying.
