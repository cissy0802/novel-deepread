Novels, Deep-Read · Albert Camus · No.41

The Stranger

L'Étranger · Albert Camus · 1942

中文 →

The two o'clock bus, fifty miles, then a mile on foot from the stop. Geraniums in the courtyard of the home, tar going soft in the sun.
The caretaker asks whether he wants the coffin opened to see his mother. He says no. The caretaker brings him a cup of white coffee; they smoke a cigarette together and sit with the body until morning.
Years later, in a courtroom, that cup of coffee and that cigarette are produced as evidence that he is a murderer. He is sentenced to be beheaded in a public square in the name of the French people.
He did kill a man. But he is killed for drinking coffee beside his mother's coffin.

This page contains complete spoilers — how the five shots on the beach are actually fired, what the trial is really about, what he screams at the chaplain on the last page and what he sees in the sky afterwards. Our conviction: everything worth discussing about a novel sits on the far side of its ending. If you want to keep the suspense, read the book first; this page will wait.

In one sentence

A young clerk in Algiers does not cry at his mother's funeral; weeks later, on an afternoon of murderous sun, he shoots an Arab man dead on a beach. The court barely investigates the shots. Instead it spends the whole trial proving that he is the kind of monster who stays dry-eyed at a burial and goes swimming and to a comedy the next day — and it sentences him to death. To the very end he refuses to invent a single line that would have sounded better.

Coordinates

Albert Camus (1913–1960) was born in French Algeria. His father died at the Battle of the Marne before he was one; he was raised in a poor quarter of Algiers by a grandmother and a mother who was nearly illiterate and partly deaf. L'Étranger was published by Gallimard in 1942, when he was twenty-nine; the same year brought the philosophical essay The Myth of Sisyphus, which is the other face of the same idea. He won the Nobel Prize in 1957. In January 1960 the car he was riding in hit a plane tree on the way back to Paris and he was killed instantly, at forty-six.

Its place in literary history is not in dispute: it is the most widely read French novel of the twentieth century, and the moment the word "absurd" walked out of philosophy and into the living room of fiction. Sartre wrote a famous explication of it in 1943; Roland Barthes later gave its temperatureless, explanation-free sentences a name — écriture blanche, white writing, writing at degree zero. One thing gets confused constantly and shouldn't: Camus rejected the label "existentialist" all his life. He said he wrote about the absurd, and he and Sartre broke openly in 1951 over The Rebel. Selling the two of them as a package was the work of publishers and textbooks, not of either man.

The story

Act One · Marengo: a funeral with no tears

The first sentence is the fuse for the whole book:

"Maman died today. Or yesterday maybe, I don't know."— opening lines (Matthew Ward's 1988 translation)

The man saying this is Meursault, a clerk in a shipping office in Algiers, not yet thirty. He asks his boss for two days off and reads something like reproach in his face, so he adds, "It's not my fault." He takes the two o'clock bus to Marengo and sleeps most of the way. The director tells him his mother had been at the home three years, cried at first, then got used to it. Meursault listens without arguing and without grief. The caretaker asks whether he'd like the coffin unscrewed; he says no, and when asked why, he can't say — he just doesn't want to. The caretaker keeps him company through the vigil and brings him a cup of white coffee, because the nights are cold, and he drinks it; he offers the caretaker a cigarette and they smoke together. Setup ① The old people of the home file in to sit with the body; one woman weeps steadily and he finds it grating. Toward dawn he dozes off.

Then the burial. What weighs most in this scene isn't tears, it's the weather: the sun climbing fast, tar melting underfoot, heat pushing up from the ground; the nurse explaining that if you walk slowly you get sunstroke and if you walk fast you sweat and catch a chill in the church — no way out either way. Setup ② In the cortege is an old man named Thomas Pérez, lame, dropping behind, cutting across the fields to catch the hearse, falling behind and catching up again; Meursault sees tears on his face that can't run down because there are too many wrinkles, so they just sit there. It's the most convincingly grief-stricken thing in the book, and the man doing it isn't the son. He goes back to Algiers the same day, thinking mainly that he can now sleep for twelve hours.

Act Two · An Algiers summer: how a man becomes an accomplice without noticing

Saturday. He swims at the public beach, runs into Marie Cardona, a former typist at his office, they play in the water, and in the evening they see a Fernandel comedy; she comes home with him. Keep this list: sea, comedy, woman. In Part One it is an unremarkable weekend. In Part Two it is the sharpest knife the prosecutor owns.

Two neighbours share his landing. Old Salamano keeps a spaniel with a skin disease; man and dog have loathed each other for eight years — he curses it, yanks it, beats it daily, and the two have grown to look alike. Setup ③ The other is Raymond Sintès, who the street says lives off women. Raymond feeds Meursault blood sausage and wine and explains his problem: he has a Moorish mistress, he suspects she has been taking his money, he beat her, her brother has come around. He wants to humiliate her once more — lure her back, and at the last moment spit in her face and throw her out. He can't write a letter. Would Meursault write it? Meursault writes it. His reason: there was no reason not to. Setup ④

The letter works. On Sunday afternoon the whole landing hears screaming and blows through the wall, and a policeman comes up and slaps Raymond. Afterwards Raymond asks whether Meursault will testify that the woman had cheated him, and Meursault says sure. It never occurs to him that the word "sure" has just put him on somebody's side.

Two smaller things happen on the same nerve. His employer offers him a posting in Paris, a whole new life; he says it's all the same to him, that people never really change their lives, that one life is as good as another. Setup ⑤ Marie asks whether he loves her; he says the question means nothing but probably not. She asks whether he'll marry her; he says if she likes. She says marriage is a serious business; he says it isn't. She decides to marry him anyway.

And then Salamano's dog goes missing. The old man stands in the doorway helpless; told that the pound charges a fee and destroys unclaimed dogs, he freezes and says he can't pay that much for a dog. That night, through the wall, Meursault hears him crying. Payoff ③ ↑ The man who beat that animal for eight years and called it a filthy brute weeps all night for it — and on the other side of the wall Meursault, for no reason he can name, thinks about his mother. Camus buries the novel's entire defence case in that wall: tears and love have never run on the same line. The court will not entertain this, but the novel has already said it.

Act Three · The beach: the sun, and the four shots after the pause

Sunday, Raymond takes Meursault and Marie to a friend's beach shack — Masson's. On the way out they notice a group of Arabs across the street watching them; one is the brother of Raymond's mistress. The morning is genuinely good: swimming, fish, wine, laughing. Camus makes the happiness solid on purpose, because everything is about to be destroyed under the same sunlight. After lunch the three men walk the sand and meet the two Arabs at a spring at the far end. There's a fight; Raymond is cut on the arm and mouth and taken to a doctor. He comes back white in the face and insists on walking again.

They find the two men lying behind a rock by the spring. Raymond pulls out a revolver: shall I shoot him now? Meursault says he hasn't spoken to you yet, it would be low to shoot like that; if you shoot, wait until he draws. Then he says the sentence that kills him: give me the gun — with the gun in his own hand, nobody will fire. Raymond hands it over; the two men slip away.

Back at the shack Meursault faces three wooden steps and suddenly cannot go up them, cannot talk, cannot deal with the women's questions. It is the first purely willed choice he makes in the entire book, and its content is: turn around and walk back into the sun.

He returns to the spring. The Arab is lying there alone, sees him, puts his hand in his pocket. The next two pages are the hottest writing in the book, and every line of it is physiology: sweat running from his eyebrows into his eyes in a warm film; the sun pressing exactly as it pressed on the day of the funeral; the blood beating in his temples. Payoff ② ↑ The nurse's no-way-out heat comes back two hundred pages later, unchanged — Camus refuses to let the funeral and the killing share a psychology; he makes them share a sun. The reader's body recognises the temperature before the reader recognises the day. The Arab draws his knife, the blade throws light straight into Meursault's eyes, and he feels the sky split open and pour fire.

He pulls the trigger. Then comes the turn of the whole novel, and it takes a single sentence: he fires four more times into a body that is no longer moving — four short knocks, as he describes them, on the door of misery (end of Part One). The first shot might be passed off as self-defence or sunstroke. The four cannot. Camus separates what can be forgiven from what cannot with a pause, and then switches off the lights.

Act Four · The trial: nobody is really trying the five shots

The examining magistrate asks why there was a pause between the first shot and the rest, and why the four — the one time in the book anyone asks the right question, and Meursault cannot answer. The magistrate grows agitated, finally brandishes a silver crucifix and demands to know whether he believes in God. He says no. The magistrate says he has never seen a soul as hardened, and afterwards calls him Monsieur Antichrist.

The investigation drags on eleven months. Under his straw mattress he finds a scrap of old newspaper: a man goes off, makes money, comes back to his village after twenty-five years without saying who he is, planning to surprise his mother and sister, and takes a room in the inn they run; they kill him with a hammer for his money and throw him in the river; the next day his wife arrives and explains, the mother hangs herself, the sister throws herself down a well. Meursault reads the scrap a thousand times. His verdict: the traveller had it coming, a little; you should never play games. It's the cruellest mirror in the book — that man dies for refusing to say plainly who he is; Meursault dies for refusing to pretend to be who others need him to be.

The trial opens in high summer, the courtroom packed and hot as an oven. Meursault has the absurd sensation that everyone here knows everyone, and he is the one person too many. Then the coldest thing in the novel happens: all morning, nobody seriously discusses the gun. The director testifies that he had not wanted to see his mother's body and could not say how old she was. The caretaker testifies that he drank white coffee, smoked, and slept beside the coffin. Payoff ① ↑ The coffee was the caretaker's own offer; the cigarette they smoked together — the same act, and on the defendant it is called coldness while on the witness it is called nothing at all. "Evidence" here isn't a set of facts; it's a position in someone's narration. Pérez is asked whether he saw Meursault cry, and says no; the defence asks whether he saw him not cry, and the old man says no to that too. Somebody in the gallery laughs.

The afternoon belongs to his friends. Céleste, who runs the restaurant where he eats, says he's a man, says the whole thing was bad luck, and can produce nothing more, his hands shaking. Marie is asked about that Saturday: when the affair began, what film they saw. She answers — the day after the funeral, swimming, a comedy. The prosecutor need say nothing further; he simply sets those three items in front of the jury. Marie cries and says it wasn't like that, there was more to it. Nobody is listening.

Raymond testifies that it was all coincidence. At which point the prosecutor produces the letter. Payoff ④ ↑ The letter written to bring a woman back so she could be spat on — a small favour at the time, not worth refusing — is now documentary proof that the accused is the accomplice of a pimp. An act with no weight acquires weight the moment it is placed inside a different narrative: that is the whole machinery of this novel.

Two lines finish him. The first: that this man buried his mother with the heart of a criminal. The second is more radical — tomorrow this same court will try a man for killing his father, and the prosecutor declares that the man in the dock today is morally guilty of that crime too, since a man who kills his mother in spirit belongs to the same family as one who kills his father with his hands. When the defence rises, Meursault's own lawyer speaks in the first person: "I killed a man." Meursault feels himself pushed one step further out — even the man arguing for him has to replace him first in order to speak. Once, he tries to say something himself, and what he says is:

"It was because of the sun."— Meursault, asked in court why he killed (Part Two)

The courtroom laughs again. It is the most misread line in the book: it sounds like evasion, and it is the only entirely honest piece of testimony given — he genuinely does not know why he fired, and he will not manufacture a presentable motive to settle everyone's nerves. The verdict: to be beheaded in a public square in the name of the French people.

Act Five · The cell: the chaplain, and the gentle indifference of the world

On death row he thinks about very concrete things: that the guillotine isn't raised on a scaffold as it is in pictures but stands level with the ground, so a man simply walks across to it, which disappoints him a little, since climbing steps at least has some ceremony to it. Every day he has to treat "appeal denied" as settled before he can think about anything else — the instant he lets himself entertain a pardon, the blood rushes to his head.

The chaplain comes three times; three times he refuses; the fourth time the chaplain simply walks in. Every condemned man turns to God in the end, he says; there is a divine face to be found in the stones of the cell. Meursault says he has stared at those stones for months and looked for exactly one face there, Marie's, and never found it. The chaplain says his heart is blind and that he will pray for him.

Then comes the only loss of control in the book. Meursault seizes the chaplain by the collar of his cassock and pours out everything he has stored up for a lifetime: that none of the priest's certainties is worth one strand of a woman's hair; that the priest is living like a dead man and isn't even sure he is alive; that he himself, who looks empty-handed, is sure of everything — because he has hold of this one truth: everyone dies, some sooner and some later, and since the same sentence has been passed on all of us, it makes no difference how you live, whom you love, whether you go to Paris. Payoff ⑤ ↑ Every maddening "it's all the same to me" in the preceding two hundred pages finally shows its foundation. He isn't numb; he has simply known all along that the ledger ends at zero, and has never explained it to anybody. The first explanation is also the last.

The chaplain is taken away and Meursault, gasping as if he had been crying, falls asleep. He wakes with stars on his face. Smells of salt, earth and sea come in with the night, and the whole sleeping summer rises like a tide; a siren sounds somewhere, someone setting off for a world that no longer has anything to do with him.

He thinks about his mother, and suddenly understands why, so near the end, she took a "fiancé" at the home and played at starting over: that close to death she must have felt free, ready to live it all again — and nobody, nobody at all, had any right to weep over her. A book that begins "Maman died today" travels two hundred pages back to the same woman and re-reads its own first, temperatureless sentence: he doesn't cry not because he doesn't love her but because he finds tears an insult to her. Then he opens himself to the world, finds it so like himself, so brotherly, and admits that he had been happy and was happy still. The novel's last wish is this:

So that it might be finished, and so that he might feel less alone, he hopes for a great crowd of spectators on the day of his execution, greeting him with cries of hatred. What has just given everything back to him he calls "the gentle indifference of the world."— end of the novel (paraphrase; the phrase in quotation marks is the standard rendering)

Put the five acts together and nothing in this book is an accident. Everything hangs on one fuse: on page one a man tells the plain truth ("Or yesterday maybe, I don't know"), and from then on he never dresses up a single sentence for anybody. The excuse to his boss, his feelings for Marie, his reason for writing Raymond's letter, "because of the sun" — each harmless on its own, and together a complete criminal file. Because what a society actually demands is not that you avoid lying; it is that you say your lines. The five shots merely gave it grounds to open a case.

Three slow-motion replays

1 · The sun on the road to the grave: body temperature instead of interiority

The craft here is that Camus replaces every sentence that ought to describe a mind with a sentence describing skin: softening tar, the smell of leather and horse dung, the nurse's remark about there being no way out, tears stranded in the wrinkles of Pérez's face. A whole funeral, and not one line saying he was grieving — or that he wasn't.

Why does this work? Because it hands the judging over to the reader, and the reader takes the job instantly. That faint discomfort you feel at "he drank the coffee" is the entire arsenal the prosecutor will use later. And the scene ends on his thought that he can now sleep for twelve hours — closing on a bodily need, which is colder than any lyricism and also truer.

2 · Five shots on a beach: a whole novel compressed into two seconds

Those two pages accomplish something very difficult: they make a killing with no motive feel unavoidable. Three techniques. First, the grammatical subject moves from the man to nature — not "he decided to fire" but the sea gasping, the sky splitting, the light stabbing, the sweat running; the sun is written as the agent. Second, sentence density: the closer it gets, the shorter and faster the sentences, until at the flash of the knife the tempo is out of anyone's control. Third and most important, Camus does not let the shot solve anything. In conventional fiction the gunshot is release; here the suffocating heat has only just been broken, and the next line is the most lucid in the chapter: he knows he has destroyed the balance of the day.

Then the four shots after the pause. The moral weight of the entire novel is inside that pause. The first shot can be given to the sun, the knife, the heat. The pause means he has come back; it means what follows has a subject. Camus offers no explanation for the four — not because he forgot, but because the moment he explains, this becomes a different book. What makes Meursault moving, and dangerous, is that he will not even tell the lie that would reduce his own sentence.

3 · The outburst at the chaplain: the one time the grammar breaks

Look at the shape of the sentences. For two hundred pages: short units, one event per sentence, no causal connectives between them — this is what Barthes meant by white writing, a record with every "therefore", "because" and "and so" deleted. In the chaplain scene the sentences abruptly lengthen, accelerate, snowball, one clause dragging the next, almost without breathing.

This isn't the author getting carried away; it is structural payment coming due. A man who has never explained anything explains once before he dies, so the shape of his speech has to change. Only here does the reader discover that the apparently hollow man has been standing on very solid ground the whole time — nobody had ever asked him where he stood.

The device at the centre of this book

The machine: it makes you convict him, then puts you on trial

The core device isn't a plot element, it's the two-part structure: Part One is a life; Part Two is the same life re-narrated by other people as a story. Part One gives facts and withholds meaning — the narrator reports what he sees, hears and feels, and never supplies causes. Part Two collects those facts and re-sorts them by "what a person ought to be like": coffee, cigarette, comedy film, a letter, strung into a chain of character whose end is a death sentence. And the reader is caught in between: you had already quietly convicted him in Part One (you frowned when he said "probably not"), so Part Two is trying more than Meursault. It is also trying that frown.

The elegance of the machine is that it requires nothing out of the ordinary: no conspiracy, no frame-up, no corrupt judge. Everyone does their job properly — the witnesses tell the truth, the prosecutor cites real events, the jury applies common sense. Meursault is killed by correct procedure and reasonable inference working together.

Where the device stops: Meursault is not a diagnosis

Worth saying plainly: reading Meursault as a clinical case (autistic, sociopathic, depressive) demotes the novel. No real person narrates himself this way; nobody spends eleven months in custody without producing a single line of self-justification. He isn't a man, he is a formal position: Camus turned "refusal to supply meaning" into a narrator and watched how long it could survive in society. The answer is: less than one summer. That is also the technical seam in the device — the silence is an extreme feat of authorial control, not a personality. And one further problem can't be waved away: this is a first-person retrospective narration, yet the narrator is on the last page waiting to be beheaded. Who wrote this, and when? The book doesn't answer. You can call that a flaw, or you can call it the absurd making its final appearance: a statement with no addressee and no date.

Its relation to The Myth of Sisyphus

The essay published the same year is the manual for the novel: the world offers no meaning, human beings insist on meaning, and the collision is the absurd. The right response is neither suicide nor a leap into faith that installs a meaning by force, but lucid life plus contempt for the situation — Sisyphus must be imagined happy. But for nine-tenths of the novel Meursault is not that lucid man; he is only a man who won't lie, and he reaches the essay's position only on the last night, cornered by an execution date. Which is why the word "happy" at the end is not irony: he has just become Sisyphus.

What it forces you to think about

QUESTION 1
Should a society judge a person for what they did, or for what kind of person they are?

Put the court's case at its strongest first — it is far stronger than most readers assume. Character evidence is not superstition; it is how we read the world. Criminal law has to distinguish intent from accident, and intent is invisible, so it must be inferred backwards from patterns of behaviour. And the prosecutor's central point is genuinely unanswered: nobody ever explains why he fired four more times after a pause. If the court must choose between "because of the sun" and "this man is cold to the bone", choosing the second is not absurd. A justice system that admits only physical evidence convicts innocent people too.

Now Meursault's side at its strongest. Once you can convict on what sort of person someone is, the people convicted will always be the ones who don't fit. A son who doesn't cry, a lover who won't claim love, an employee who turns down a promotion — none of it is illegal, but together it trips an older mechanism: this one is not like us. The nominal subject of the trial is the five shots; the actual subject is his failure to perform the right emotion at the right moment. The cruellest feature of that standard is that it is never written into any statute, so there is nothing to appeal against.

Bring it up to date. Almost every system we use to evaluate people now runs on character evidence — "culture fit" in an interview, "attitude" on a performance review, a ten-year-old post surfaced by a stranger, a silence when everyone else was posting. You can have done nothing wrong and simply failed to be visibly sad at the appropriate time, and a verdict can be forming somewhere out of sight. You won't even know when your trial began.

QUESTION 2
Refusing to say the expected thing: courage, or a cheap kind of fastidiousness?

Camus answered this himself. In the preface he wrote for an American edition in 1955:

"In our society any man who does not weep at his mother's funeral runs the risk of being sentenced to death."— Camus, 1955 preface to the American edition of The Stranger

In the same preface he calls Meursault "the only Christ we deserve", on the grounds that this is a man who refuses to lie — not merely about facts, but by declining to claim one feeling more than he has. The defence has force: what a society punishes is often not the crime but the refusal to join the performance everyone else is putting on. When we say someone is "a bit off", we frequently mean only that he failed to take the line we handed him.

But the counter-case is hard too. Meursault's honesty costs him almost nothing, because he doesn't care. Real honesty is refusing to lie when you badly want to; he has no motive to lie in the first place, which looks less like virtue than like dullness. Worse: his truth-telling has never once protected anybody. "I love you" would have been a lie to Marie, but "the question means nothing, probably not" is not obviously more respectful — it transfers the cost of the truth onto her and collects the moral high ground on the way. And when Raymond needed a letter written to lure a woman into being spat on, the principle of not lying made no sound at all: he wouldn't shade a single word for a woman who loved him, but he would draft a trap for a thug. A principle that only shows up when it's free is barely a principle.

In ordinary life the dilemma takes this shape: is displaying more grief at a funeral than you actually feel hypocrisy, or manners? Most etiquette is a small emotional concession made on someone else's behalf — you tell the bride she looks beautiful, you keep your bad news to yourself while someone else celebrates. In Meursault's world all of that is lying. The question is whether a society in which everyone reported only their true feelings would be kinder, or merely colder.

QUESTION 3
If the universe is indifferent to everything, what exactly forbids killing?

This is the largest hole in the book, and Camus knew it. Meursault's final reasoning is: everyone dies, therefore nothing you do makes a difference. But if nothing makes a difference, the four shots make no difference either — the absurd looks as though it issues a licence for violence.

The rebuttal is sound: you cannot derive "anything goes" from "nothing means anything." "Anything goes" is itself a value claim and needs grounds, and nihilism supplies no grounds at all — it neither forbids killing nor permits it; it says nothing. Camus wrote The Rebel (1951) to plug exactly this hole: the first response to the absurd is to say no, and inside that no there is already a yes — the rebel refuses in the name of something that must not be trampled; and if he grants that this something has value in himself, he has to grant that it has value in everyone. So revolt ends not in killing but in the refusal to kill. The book cost him his friendship with Sartre.

The other side is equally strong: does that argument grow out of the absurd, or is it bolted on from outside? If the universe really is wholly indifferent, where does "there is something in a person that must not be trampled" get its warrant? It reads rather like a man unwilling to accept the consequences of his own premise, going back to build a seawall. The question of what grounds morality in a universe with no referee is, genuinely, still open.

And it isn't abstract: it is the account every person who no longer believes in any final authority has to settle the next morning. If nobody is keeping score, why do you still hand back the extra change, still step aside for a stranger at midnight? For most people the real answer isn't an argument but something closer to a constitution — you simply can't do it. That is what Camus finally bet on: not that a law forbids killing, but that once you actually see the other person, your hand won't move. It is a fragile answer. It may also be the only honest one.

QUESTION 4
The murdered man has no name in the entire book. Can a great novel have a hole at its moral centre?

The facts first: the Arab man Meursault kills has no name, no line of dialogue, no family weeping in the courtroom; the Moorish woman who is beaten has no name either. In 1942 the majority of the population of Algiers was Arab and Berber; in a novel set entirely in Algiers, they are scenery. Edward Said made the point in Culture and Imperialism; in 2013 the Algerian writer Kamel Daoud answered with a whole novel, The Meursault Investigation, narrated by the dead man's brother and giving the dead man a name: Musa.

The defence is not weak. The narrator is Meursault, and this is genuinely how the world looks to him. The man has no name precisely because, in the consciousness of a French clerk in a colony, he has none — displaying that blindness is not the same as endorsing it; it puts it under glass. And since the novel's whole indictment is of how monstrous it is to reduce a person to a label, the fact that readers ache for Meursault and not for the man on the sand is itself an experiment the book runs on its readers, with unflattering results.

The other side is just as strong, and cannot be waved off with "point of view": Camus had a choice. A single clause — his name, whose brother he was — would not have damaged the white writing. He didn't write it, which tells you that in that particular gaze the information registered as unnecessary. Charging the narrator's blind spot entirely to the character, with nothing on the author's account, is too convenient a defence.

What's left for the reader is a very ordinary capacity: can you hold that a work is great and that it is blind in one eye, without having to choose? Public argument now runs to canonisation or demolition, whereas adult reading looks like this: the book taught me to recognise the machinery that reduces a person to a label, and the book is itself an instance of it. Both true; neither cancels the other.

The obsession: what Camus kept writing

Lay the work out and the route is clear, and its direction is from one man's absurd towards many people's solidarity. The Stranger is the first stop: the protagonist is alone and his single talent is not lying. The Plague (1947) is the turn — a city is sealed off and Dr Rieux does no philosophising, he simply makes his rounds every day. Faced with suffering that has no justification, the only decent response is not to explain it but to fight it, together with other people. The Fall (1956) is his most sinister book: Clamence, the judge-penitent, is Meursault's exact inverse — a lawyer who talks incessantly, explains everything, and turns his own guilt into a line of business. Camus spends a whole novel demonstrating that something is worse than explaining nothing: explaining everything.

A more private line runs the length of the work: the mother. The Stranger opens with her death and ends with him finally understanding her; the unfinished manuscript found in the wreckage of his car and published in 1994, The First Man, is about exactly that illiterate mother, the father he never met, and a childhood poor in everything except sunlight. Underneath all his large statements about the silence of the universe lies a very small fact: his mother barely spoke, and he spent his life looking for a language on her behalf.

Criticism and controversy

Without flattery, several of the objections to this book are real and the critics have the better of them:

  • The colonial gaze. The nameless victim, and the complete absence of interiority for Arab characters, is what the novel is most persistently held to account for (see the previous section). It is an unavoidable part of reading The Stranger now, not the hobbyhorse of a few.
  • Flat women. Marie has essentially two attributes, a body and loyalty; the Moorish woman Raymond beats is pure plot furniture, and the largest consequence her beating produces in the book is that a policeman's visit spoils an afternoon. Meursault's total indifference to it reads far worse today than his dry eyes at the funeral.
  • Philosophical thinness. Many philosophers have argued that Camus's account of the absurd is conceptually loose — more a literary sensation than a defensible position — and Sartre's camp attacked him for founding morality on feeling. Camus never claimed to be a philosopher.
  • Over-romanticised. Many young readers take Meursault for a hero of nonconformity, when the man in the book kills someone and then fires four more times into the body. Reading the novel as a manifesto for being yourself is a lazy misreading: what Camus defended in his preface was the refusal to lie, not the five shots.
  • The author's own politics. During the Algerian war Camus satisfied neither side: he opposed French repression and torture, opposed the FLN's bombings of civilians, argued for a civilian truce, and finally chose public silence. In Stockholm in 1957, challenged by an Algerian student, he said something that has been quoted ever since — in substance, that people were planting bombs in the tramways of Algiers, that his mother might be on one of them, and that if that was justice he preferred his mother. It is usually compressed to "between justice and my mother, I choose my mother", and remains the focus of arguments about his politics. Stated here as verifiable fact, without a verdict.
  • Translation. The word maman is a genuine problem. Stuart Gilbert's 1946 version gave "Mother died today", flattening the intimacy, and the book appeared as The Outsider in Britain and The Stranger in America; Matthew Ward's 1988 translation restored "Maman". A large part of your first impression of this novel is the translator's doing.

Ten sentences to take away

1. This is not the story of a man who kills someone; it is the story of a society sentencing a man to death for not being like us — the five shots merely gave it grounds to open a case.

2. The fuse is that clause on page one, "or yesterday maybe, I don't know": from then on he never dresses a sentence up for anybody.

3. The harmless small things of Part One — a coffee, a cigarette, a comedy, a ghost-written letter — become, item by item, the evidence of Part Two. The facts never changed; the narrator did.

4. The four shots after the pause are the moral centre of the book: the first can be blamed on the sun, the four cannot, and Camus refuses to invent a reason for them.

5. "It was because of the sun" sounds like evasion and is the only fully honest testimony in the trial — he really does not know why.

6. Salamano beat that dog for eight years and wept all night when it was lost. Tears and love never did run on the same line, and courts admit only tears.

7. His own lawyer says "I killed a man" — even the person arguing for him has to replace him first.

8. Meursault is not a diagnosis but a position: the refusal to supply meaning, made into a narrator, to see how long it survives. It survives less than one summer.

9. His honesty appears only when it is free: not one soft word for the woman who loved him, and a prompt draft of the letter that trapped another woman. That is the crack the book leaves in itself.

10. What stays with you is the last night: stars, the smell of salt and earth, a siren far off, and a man to be beheaded in the morning saying he is happy — he has only just become Sisyphus.
