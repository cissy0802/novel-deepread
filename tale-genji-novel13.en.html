Novel Deep-Read · Murasaki Shikibu · No.13

The Tale of Genji

Genji monogatari · Murasaki Shikibu · written c.1000–1012 · 54 chapters

中文 →

A March afternoon, cherry petals coming down over the Rokujō estate. Young noblemen are kicking a ball around in the garden, raising dust; nobody is looking at the veranda.
Behind the blind on that veranda lives a small imported cat on a long cord. The cat bolts, the cord goes taut, and the blind is dragged up from its lower corner.
For those few seconds the woman standing inside is visible to the men outside. She has no time to withdraw. The young man who sees her is the son of his host's closest friend.
He will die of it. She will take vows because of it. And the master of the house — the most fortunate man of his age — begins, from that afternoon, to come apart an inch at a time.
Nobody ever blames the cat.

This page contains complete spoilers — who really fathered Fujitsubo's child, how the same act comes back to Genji in exactly the same shape twenty-odd years later, Murasaki's death, the chapter in this book that is a title with no text under it, and where Ukifune ends up on the bank of the Uji River. The reason is the usual one here: everything worth discussing in this novel sits on the far side of "and then what happened." Without the ending, all you can do is praise the prose.

In one sentence

An emperor's favourite son is demoted out of the imperial line and given a commoner's surname; he spends his life moving between love and power. He fathers a child on his father's consort, that child becomes emperor and lifts him to the summit of the court — and then, twenty-odd years later, the young principal wife he takes at forty bears another man's child, which he holds in his arms in precisely the shape of the thing he once did. The woman he valued most dies before him; his own death the author refuses to write, leaving a chapter that is nothing but a title. And there are still thirteen chapters to go, about his nominal son, who drives two women to the end of their options by gentler and slower means.

Coordinates

Murasaki Shikibu (c.973–c.1014; both dates uncertain) is a name we invented for her: "Shikibu" from her father's office, "Murasaki" most likely from her own character, or from the purple of wisteria. Her real name is not recorded. She was the daughter of mid-ranking provincial nobility, began the tale after her husband's death, and was summoned to court on the strength of it by the great power-broker Fujiwara no Michinaga to serve as a lady-in-waiting to his daughter, Empress Shōshi. She also left a diary and a small collection of poems.

The tale was written around 1000–1012, runs to fifty-four chapters, spans four generations and some seventy years, carries over four hundred characters, and has 795 waka poems embedded in its prose. Its place in literary history needs two separate sentences. Calling it "the world's first novel" depends entirely on how you define novel — Greek and Roman prose fiction came first, and the slogan gets thrown around loosely. But calling it one of the earliest long narratives in world literature with full psychological interiority is barely contested: what it records is not events but the unsaid parts of events — who understood what at which moment, and who pretended not to.

One more thing has to be said up front: this book was written in kana, the phonetic Japanese script. In eleventh-century Japan, Chinese was the public language of men and serious writing; kana was considered private, soft, women's script. The longest and deepest book in Japanese was written by a woman in the script her culture did not take seriously — which is not a curiosity but part of the explanation. With no official register to maintain, there is no posture to keep up.

The story

Act I · A boy who cannot be emperor, and the woman he must not love

The Kiritsubo Emperor has many consorts and loves the lowest-ranked of them. In a court where rank governs everything, imperial favour lifts her far above what her family can support, and the women's quarters begin to grind her down: obstacles left in corridors she must walk, filth thrown on her robes, a faction led by the Kokiden Consort — daughter of the Minister of the Right, future empress mother — treating her as a target. She bears an extraordinarily beautiful son and dies three years later.

The emperor wants the boy made crown prince, but he has no maternal clan behind him, and in this world nine-tenths of a prince's future is written in his grandfather's office. Then a Korean physiognomist examines the child and reports: he has the face of a sovereign, but should he reign the realm will suffer; yet a mere subject's life does not fit him either. The emperor takes the point — the boy can be neither raised up nor left in the succession as a target.seed ① So he strikes him from the imperial line and gives him the surname Minamoto, or Genji: from now on he is the Shining Genji, "shining" being the court's own flattery.

The emperor cannot let his dead consort go. Someone tells him a late emperor's fourth daughter, Fujitsubo, looks remarkably like her. Fujitsubo enters the palace as the new favourite. She is five years older than Genji, who grows up in her rooms with his father's arm around him; people at court joke that the two of them look like brother and sister. At twelve Genji comes of age and is married to Aoi, daughter of the Minister of the Left — a political match. She is four years older, dignified, guarded, incapable of unbending first; the two of them are as polite to each other as two stones. So Genji looks elsewhere.

(The famous rainy-night ranking of women falls in these years: Genji and Tō no Chūjō — Aoi's brother, his lifelong friend and rival — and a few other young men talk till dawn, sorting women into three grades by birth. The conversation reads like a table of contents; nearly every woman in the next few dozen chapters can be found somewhere in it.)

Then comes the transgression his life is built on. One night, while Fujitsubo is home from the palace recovering from illness, he bribes her attendants and gets into her room.seed ② She conceives. The child is born looking so like Genji that it is frightening, and the emperor holds this "tenth prince" in delight and remarks how much he resembles his shining son. The whole court repeats the compliment; exactly two people hear it and go cold. The boy is made crown prince and eventually reigns as Emperor Reizei.

In the same period Genji, ill, goes up to the northern hills to recover, and through a hedge sees a girl of about ten in tears — the sparrow she was keeping has been let out of its cage.seed ③

"Suzume no ko o Inuki ga nigashitsuru" — Inuki has let my sparrow out.— the girl's first words in the "Young Murasaki" chapter

One look at her face and he knows: she resembles Fujitsubo. She turns out to be Fujitsubo's niece. He asks her grandmother for her and is refused as though he were joking — the child, the old woman says, is far too young for such a conversation. When the grandmother dies, he takes the girl before her father can collect her, installs her in his own house, and raises her: her handwriting, her music, her taste, all shaped to his. This is Murasaki — the most deeply written character in the book, and the one modern readers cannot sit still for.

On another line runs a lover he is neglecting: the Rokujō lady, widow of a former crown prince, older than Genji and his superior in both rank and pride. On the day of the Kamo festival everyone competes for kerbside space to watch the procession. She comes incognito and parks among the crowd; Aoi's train arrives later, and her retainers shove the other carriage out of the way, snapping its shafts, until the Rokujō lady is wedged behind other vehicles, watching Genji ride past through a gap in the crowd — a public humiliation carried out entirely by where a carriage is allowed to stand, without a single rude word.seed ④

Soon Aoi is pregnant and suddenly gravely ill. Rites, incantations, exorcists; the spirit afflicting her is forced to speak through a medium, and what it says carries the Rokujō lady's phrasing and the Rokujō lady's grievances. Aoi bears a son (Yūgiri) and dies. Elsewhere, the Rokujō lady is ill too, drifting, convinced her own spirit has been leaving her body; and when she wakes she smells something in her hair and clothes that will not wash out — the poppy seed burned on an exorcist's altar.payoff ④ ↑ She never admits to anything, and the author never delivers a verdict; but once that smell is on the page, she and the reader know at the same instant what has happened. The most frightening piece of evidence in this book is an odour.

Act II · Exile: hitting bottom is where he picks up his real asset

The old emperor abdicates and dies; the Minister of the Right's faction takes full control. At precisely this moment Genji's affair with Oborozukiyo — that minister's daughter, and a favourite of the reigning Emperor Suzaku — is walked in on by her father. This is no longer gallantry, it is leverage. Rather than wait to be dealt with, Genji exiles himself to the coast at Suma, and later to Akashi.

The Suma chapters are the barest stretch in the book: no procession, no attendants, a house that lets the wind through. Then a storm sets in for days and lightning sets a gallery on fire. In the middle of it Genji dreams of his dead father, who tells him to leave this place. At dawn a small boat comes in: the Akashi Novice, an old man who long ago resigned office and took vows and has been keeping to this coast, reports a dream with the same content.seed ⑤ He takes Genji to Akashi and half-manoeuvres, half-delivers his daughter, the Akashi lady, into his hands. By the arithmetic of rank the match is preposterous — a disgraced nobleman and a provincial governor's daughter — and the old man is staking his entire fortune and standing on it.

The Akashi lady bears a daughter. Not long after, the capital is troubled by portents and Suzaku by an eye disease, and Genji is recalled. Reizei — the "tenth prince" — takes the throne, and Genji rises to Palace Minister and then Chancellor. He was removed from the imperial line so that he could never rise; his son now sits on the throne and he himself has become the actual centre of the court.

Later Reizei learns from an old priest whose son he really is, and in his horror tries to hand the throne back. Genji refuses absolutely and accepts instead a rank that has never existed: honorary retired emperor — the standing and treatment of an abdicated sovereign, without being one.payoff ① ↑ The physiognomist's "neither sovereign nor subject," forty years on, is discharged as a specific court title. The prophecy is not fulfilled by any miracle; it is assembled, step by step, out of one adultery, one exile and one child's secret.

Act III · The Rokujō estate: everyone gathered into one house

With power secured, Genji builds the Rokujō estate, four blocks of grounds laid out as the four seasons: Murasaki in the spring quarter, Hanachirusato in summer, the autumn quarter for Empress Akikonomu (the Rokujō lady's daughter, whom Genji has installed as Reizei's empress), the Akashi lady in winter. He collects the women of his life, and the tensions between them, into a single garden where it is always every season at once.

These are the brightest chapters: flower viewing, incense competitions, poetry matches, coming-of-age ceremonies for daughters. The Akashi lady's daughter is taken to be raised by Murasaki for a very practical reason — her birth mother's rank is too low, and the girl will be judged for it at court. The snowy day on which a mother hands over her own child is the most understated heartbreak in the book. That daughter later enters the palace, bears a prince, and becomes empress. When the Akashi Novice hears of it he leaves a letter explaining that as a young man he dreamed his line would produce the mother of an emperor, and that every reckless thing he ever did was for that dream — then walks into the mountains, and no one sees him again.payoff ⑤ ↑ An old man everyone took for a provincial lunatic made a bet with a three-generation horizon and won it; and the author arranges for the bet to pay out just as the winner stops meaning to collect.

Somewhere in these chapters Genji teases his ward Tamakazura for reading monogatari — made-up tales — all day, then turns around and defends them in earnest: the official histories record only the great public matters, and everything fine-grained and worth keeping about human beings survives only in invented stories. A thousand years before the novel had any standing as a craft, a novelist used a character's mouth to file a defence of her own trade.

Act IV · The account settles, in the same shape

In Genji's fortieth year his brother, the retired Emperor Suzaku, is preparing to take vows and cannot bear to leave his favourite daughter, the Third Princess, unprotected; he entrusts her to Genji as principal wife. Genji agrees. This is the hinge of the book, and the arithmetic behind it is cruel: Murasaki has no formal standing at all, and her position rests entirely on Genji's love; the Third Princess is an imperial daughter, and outranks her the moment she arrives. Murasaki says nothing, and begins to be ill. She asks two and three times to be allowed to take vows, and Genji refuses every time — he cannot give her up.payoff ③ ↑ The girl who wept over a sparrow in the northern hills three decades earlier never does get out of her own cage: the bird flew, she did not. The cage is built out of devotion, which is why it cannot be dismantled.

Meanwhile, the cat. Kashiwagi — Tō no Chūjō's son — sees the Third Princess when the blind is pulled aside during that ball game, and never recovers. He contrives to obtain the cat and keeps it, sleeping with it beside him. Years later he bribes an attendant and gets into her rooms. She conceives.

The discovery is a small thing: Genji finds one of Kashiwagi's letters under her cushion. He does not confront anyone, does not accuse, does not tell a soul. At a banquet he presses wine on Kashiwagi in front of the company with a few remarks that sound like teasing — something about how age makes a man an amusing drunk, and how the young should take care. Kashiwagi understands. This is capital punishment, Heian style: you do nothing, you simply let him know that you know. He sickens and dies within months. The Third Princess bears a son and takes vows immediately — she is barely in her twenties.

At the child's fiftieth-day celebration Genji holds the baby, and the text is explicit about what is in his mind: this is exactly how my father held the child Fujitsubo and I made, and he was just as delighted.payoff ② ↑ This is the hinge of the entire work and its most merciless structural stroke: the reckoning is not a disaster arriving, it is the same act performed again from the other direction, with you in the seat of the man being deceived — and more precisely, the author makes him see the symmetry himself. The pain is not in the event; it is in the comprehension. The child is named Kaoru ("fragrance"), born giving off a scent of his own, and the second half of the book belongs to him.

Murasaki does not recover. She does manage one last thing: she sponsors a great dedication of the Lotus Sutra, like a woman packing for a journey. That autumn she lies against an armrest with Genji beside her and her foster daughter, the Akashi Empress, in the room; the three of them exchange three poems, all built on dew. She dies near daybreak. The largest death in this novel is three remarks about dew.

For a full year afterwards Genji works his way through the calendar month by month — the cherries, the summer rains, the star festival, the anniversary, the year-end rites — burning Murasaki's letters one by one, settling his affairs, preparing to renounce the world. The chapter reads like a diary of dates.

Act V · Vanished into the clouds, and thirteen chapters at Uji

The next chapter is called "Kumogakure" — Vanished into the Clouds, a polite way of saying that a great person has died. It consists of a title and no text. How the most famous man in Japanese literature died, what he said at the end, whether he ever did take vows: not a word. The blank has generated centuries of theories — lost, deliberate, never a chapter at all — but whatever caused it, the effect in the reader's hands is fixed: a novel that writes every parting in exhaustive detail hands in a blank sheet for its hero's death.

Then the story restarts with different people, out of the capital, at Uji — close to the city, but treated by its inhabitants as the back of beyond. Two young men: Kaoru, nominally Genji's youngest son and actually Kashiwagi's, dimly aware that something is wrong with his parentage and therefore permanently occupied with renunciation, truth and the next world, preceded everywhere by his own natural fragrance; and Niou, Genji's grandson by the Akashi Empress, direct, sensual, accustomed to taking what he wants, who perfumes his robes at enormous expense specifically to out-scent Kaoru. One is born fragrant and wants release; the other manufactures his fragrance and wants possession.

Kaoru goes to Uji to study Buddhism with a reclusive prince and, on a moonlit night, hears music through a fence and sees the prince's two daughters. After their father dies he falls in love with the elder, Ōigimi. She refuses to marry him — not from dislike, but because she sees the situation too clearly: in this world marriage means handing yourself over to somebody else's keeping. She pushes her sister forward as a substitute; Nakanokimi ends up married to Niou, who shortly also marries a minister's daughter. Ōigimi watches every single thing she predicted come true, stops eating, refuses medicine, and is dead within months. The author never writes the word suicide, and makes it perfectly clear: she has decided not to live.

Kaoru cannot let her go. Hearing there is a half-sister who resembles her, he finds Ukifune — daughter of the same prince by a gentlewoman, raised in the eastern provinces, with no standing and no protector — and installs her at Uji. Niou finds out and comes one night imitating Kaoru's voice; by the time she understands, it is too late. What follows is the most modern-feeling stretch of the novel: she is held by two men at once, one offering a dignified and chilly maintenance, the other an ardent and irresponsible obsession, each becoming aware of the other. There is no one to consult — her mother only weeps, her nurse only urges her to seize the opportunity. The river runs loudly all night. The only way out she can think of is death. One night she walks out and is gone. No body is found, and they hold a funeral without one.

She is not dead. She is found unconscious under a tree and taken in by a bishop and his party. When she wakes she refuses to say who she is and asks repeatedly to be made a nun, and the bishop shaves her head. When Kaoru finally traces her and sends her young brother with a letter, she stays behind her blind and says there is no such person: will not acknowledge him, will not see him, sends the letter back unopened. In a world where marriage is ownership and being seen is a scandal, cancelling herself is the only button she can actually press — and that button exists precisely because pressing it threatens no one.

The last sentence of the book has Kaoru wondering whether some other man is hiding her. Fifty-four chapters, and that is where it stops: no resolution, no accounting. Many readers think it was never finished. Many others think nothing could end it more exactly.

Seen whole, the causal chain of this book is a single fuse, and the fuse is a line of illegitimate blood. Genji fathers a child on his father's consort; that secret buys him the court, because it is Reizei on the throne that makes him master of the Rokujō estate — and it is exactly that eminence which leads Suzaku to entrust him with the Third Princess, which starts Murasaki dying, and which puts Kashiwagi under the eaves of his house on the afternoon the blind goes up. Twenty-odd years later the same act returns in the same shape, with Genji in the deceived man's seat. The last thirteen chapters are the aftershock: Kaoru carries that blood to Uji looking for a reason his life should make sense, fails to find one, and pulls two more women into the same river.

Three slow-motion scenes

1 · The carriage quarrel: an execution in the street with no rude words in it

What actually happens on the day of the festival is a fight over parking. The Rokujō lady outranks Aoi, but she has come incognito and is the discarded lover; Aoi is the wife, daughter of the Minister of the Left, with more retainers and drink taken. There is not one line of abuse in the whole confrontation, only movement of position: her carriage pushed to the rear, its shafts snapped, until she is stuck in the crowd watching Genji ride past — and Genji has no idea she is there.

The craft here is that the humiliation is written in space rather than in language. A Heian noblewoman is essentially never seen; between her and the world there is always a blind, a fan, a carriage. Her place in the world is therefore, quite literally, where her carriage is allowed to stand — and pushing it to the back is a public announcement of who she is. The author does not use a single adjective, and the reader swallows the insult perfectly. Better still is how the consequence is attached: the injury does not turn into revenge, it turns into an illness, a dissociation, an odour that will not wash out. Between "pushed to the back of the crowd" and "another woman dies in childbirth" there is not one causal connective.

2 · The cat and the blind: three seconds of sightline that kill three people

The engine of Heian fiction is kaimami — glimpsing a woman through a gap in a fence, a blind, a screen. Genji glimpsing the child Murasaki is kaimami; Kaoru glimpsing the Uji sisters is kaimami. The author used the device her whole life and finally used it to deliver the fatal blow of the book, and what makes it work is that the responsibility has been distributed until none of it lands anywhere: Kashiwagi is not spying, he is watching the game; the Third Princess is not being improper, she is standing in her own room; the cat has no malice, it is a cat. We were even told in advance that the animal is a foreign breed, kept on a long cord, and skittish. Every link is reasonable; the sum is a catastrophe. If nobody has done anything wrong and this is the result, the subject of the book is not morality — it is probability and position.

And then a cruelly economical detail: unable to have the Third Princess, Kashiwagi acquires the cat and sleeps with it. What he is in love with is those three seconds of sightline, and the cat is the only portion of them he can take home. The passage is nearly comic, and the moment you have finished smiling you understand why he dies: a man who takes a substitute seriously will eventually go for the real thing.

3 · The rites: three poems about dew

Murasaki's death is the slowest passage in the book. The preparation is a great sutra dedication in which she completes her business one item at a time. Then autumn, a high wind, the bush clover in the garden flattening and springing back, dew shaking on it and ready to fall. She can no longer sit up; she leans on an armrest, with Genji beside her and her foster daughter the Empress present. The three exchange poems on a single image — dew. She says she is the sort of thing one gust removes; Genji answers that they should go together, then; the Empress answers, what in this world is not dew.

The craft is handing the largest grief in the book to the smallest shared metaphor. None of the three discusses feeling; each says one thing about one object. That is the etiquette of poetic exchange and also this novel's governing principle for emotion: never describe the state, trade the image, and let the reader compute the temperature. Murasaki's death is written at this magnification, and Genji's is a blank page — the author answers the question of where this book's centre of gravity lies by allocation of space alone.

The core apparatus: how this book is built

1 · People without names

Almost nobody in the original has a name. Characters are "the Kiritsubo Consort" (the pavilion she lived in), "the Captain" (his office), "the Third Princess" (her birth order), "the Akashi lady" (where she is from); a promotion changes what you call someone, so one person may go by five or six designations. Even "Murasaki," "Yūgao" and "Ukifune" are largely nicknames later readers lifted out of the poems.

This is not medieval carelessness, it is the actual construction of that world: a person is their position. Using someone's given name was close to an insult. So the book states its theme at the level of grammar before it states it anywhere else — every tragedy here is a tragedy of position. The Kiritsubo Consort is ground down because her rank is too low; Murasaki is overtaken because she has no formal standing; Ukifune can be installed anywhere by anyone because she has no birth to speak of. You cannot read their names because they were never treated as names in the first place.

2 · Blinds, screens, fans: an economy of vision

The physical world of this novel is made of obstruction — blinds, curtained frames, screens, fans, darkness, bamboo fences. Seeing a face is an event; being seen is an accident. The fuel of every plot turn is who saw what, and when. Kashiwagi's catastrophe comes from three seconds of a raised blind; Kaoru's fixation from one night of music through a fence; Genji's entire life begins with a girl crying on the far side of a hedge. Read the other way round, that same obstruction is an information system: a woman who cannot go out or be seen deals with the world through letters, poems and intermediaries, which is why the decisive actions in this book are rarely embraces or arguments but rather a letter falling into the wrong hands — Kashiwagi dies of a sheet of paper under a cushion.

3 · The poems are dialogue, not decoration

Those 795 poems are not ornament. In this world anything important must be said in thirty-one syllables, and the paper it is written on, the flowering branch it is tied to and the quality of the handwriting are all part of the meaning. Courtship, refusal, probing, apology and rupture are all conducted by exchange. It amounts to an encryption layer over the whole novel: nobody says the thing, they refract it onto a natural object — dew, bush clover, wild geese, the tide — and whether and how the other party catches it is the entire content of the conversation. Any number of turning points between characters look, on the surface, like two short poems about plants.

4 · The woman doing the telling

The narrator is not an omniscient god. She interrupts to say that she had this from the older gentlewomen, that this part is too trivial to relate, that she no longer remembers what came next. Japanese criticism calls these intrusions sōshiji. They construct a fictional frame of transmission: none of this is invented — it is old business from the palace, being passed on to you by a lady-in-waiting.

The frame does two jobs. Technically it lets the author adjust distance at will: skip what she likes, comment where she likes. Politically it gives a woman writing a very long book about emperors, consorts, adultery and a contaminated imperial succession a protective shell: these are things people say happened, long ago, not events of the present reign. The most dangerous material is filed behind the most modest tone of voice.

5 · The blank page

"Kumogakure" is a title with nothing under it, and it is the book's final device: written out, a death is still a scene, still something that can be admired and savoured; unwritten, it is an actual disappearance.

What it forces you to think about

Question 1
A man raises a child into the partner he wants. If she does come to love him, and does end up better off than any of her alternatives, does that settle it?

Take the defence of Genji at its strongest first. One: the real options for a girl of her class were extremely narrow. Murasaki's father's house had no interest in her, and once her grandmother died she had no guardian; her actual alternative future was to be collected by that father, married to whoever the family picked, and to spend her life contesting rank with other wives. Two: Genji gave her things a woman of that world almost never got — education, cultivation, real authority over a great household, and three decades of being treated as the most important person in a man's life. She is not written in the posture of a victim; she is the effective mistress of the Rokujō estate. Three, and most seriously: the author does not think she is describing a crime, and convicting an eleventh-century text in twenty-first-century legal language forfeits any chance of understanding it.

Now the other side at its strongest. The most damaging evidence is not from our century — it is inside the book. The author does not let Murasaki grow old contented. She has no child, no formal standing; at forty she is outranked overnight by a younger, better-born wife, and she begins to sicken. She asks three times to be allowed to take vows and is refused three times. Taking vows here means "I would like to withdraw from this identity" — and she is not permitted even that. Which makes "but she loved him too" a suspect sentence: in a person shaped by one man since the age of ten, how much of the love is hers? That is precisely what makes the arrangement so convenient for the one doing the shaping — he can cite the feelings of the person he shaped as proof that he did nothing wrong.

None of this is safely ancient. Any badly unequal relationship contains the line "everything you have, I gave you" — which may be entirely true, and is harder to answer because it is true. What deserves suspicion is never the malicious kind of control; it is the kind that really did provide, really is owed something, and sincerely believes itself to be love. Murasaki's life is the full experiment: the person who treated her best is also the person who left her nowhere to go.

Question 2
Is Kaoru a good man — and if a scrupulous, self-examining man who never indulges himself ends up destroying more women than the rake does, what exactly is his self-examination worth?

Kaoru is an anomaly in the history of the novel: he has an interior life in the modern sense. He does not believe the world is worth committing to, he doubts his own parentage, he longs to renounce and cannot decide to, and when he wants someone he first works out whether the wanting is real. He is the only male lead in the book capable of doubting himself.

In his defence: he never seizes anyone. Ōigimi will not marry him and he waits; Nakanokimi marries elsewhere and he withdraws; with Ukifune he provides a household and a decent position, and does not do what Niou does under cover of a borrowed voice. If morality is a matter of lines not crossed, Kaoru essentially never crosses one. And his hesitancy has a cause: he half knows he is not Genji's son, and a man whose very existence is evidence of a crime is poorly placed to demand happiness.

The other side holds too, and cuts deeper. Ōigimi starves to death inside Kaoru's patient, restrained courtship. He never forces her; he also never leaves, keeping a woman besieged for months in a situation she can neither accept nor escape. He installs Ukifune at Uji because she resembles Ōigimi — she is a substitute and he knows quite well that she is one — and everything "decent" he provides for her stops short of asking what she wants. What pushes her into the river is not only Niou's impersonation; it is also Kaoru's permanently correct, permanently sorrowful, permanently non-releasing love.

So the question is not whether he is a good man but this: if self-examination never changes behaviour, is it anything more than a more refined form of self-care? Niou damages people because he doesn't think; Kaoru damages people because he won't — won't admit what he wants, won't carry responsibility for anyone else — so the entire cost of his indecision is paid by other people. The type is everywhere now: the man who agonizes sincerely over every injury he causes and then causes it again in the same form. Compared with the man who never agonizes at all, what is he actually offering? Murasaki Shikibu finished this character a thousand years ago, and granted him no indulgence whatsoever.

Question 3
Mono no aware turns everything into beauty. Is that wisdom, or an anaesthetic for the people doing the damage?

The eighteenth-century scholar Motoori Norinaga proposed a reading that still governs the field: the heart of this book is neither moral instruction nor Buddhist retribution but mono no aware — a sensitivity to the fact that everything passes, and a fellow-feeling that follows from it. On that reading, Genji's value has nothing to do with whether he is good; it lies in feeling more finely than the people around him.

It is a powerful reading. It explains why the book never delivers verdicts: every character — the Rokujō lady, Kashiwagi, even the malicious women of the palace — is given sufficient interior reason. Aware is an ethic; it is simply an ethic whose centre is not "what is right" but "noticing that other people are suffering too." In a hierarchy nobody could imagine changing, that may be the highest available good.

The counter-argument has to be put just as hard: in this book, the person with the finest perception is usually the person doing the harm. Aoi, Yūgao, the Rokujō lady, Murasaki — at every funeral he helped bring about, Genji is the most stricken figure present and the one best able to phrase his grief in poetry. Aestheticization has a side effect: it quietly converts "what I did" into "what I felt." Kashiwagi's death is exquisite, and the man who killed him is Genji; the Third Princess takes vows in her early twenties and is given a cold, beautiful snowfall for a backdrop. When suffering is rendered this well, readers sink into it alongside the participants instead of asking whose account it should be charged to.

The shape of that is very familiar today: describe a structural problem as fate, describe a chargeable act as impermanence, and let everyone regret it together. Regret is cheap precisely because it requires nobody to move. And yet the other side is real too — Norinaga was solving a genuine problem, because if a novel's value depends on its protagonist's morals, literature is reduced to a set of verdicts. There is no clean answer here. The greatness of this book and its dubiousness grow on the same stalk.

Recurring themes: a writer with one book

Murasaki Shikibu left very little: this novel, a diary, a small poetry collection. So her obsessions have to be read out of those three, and they point the same way: she always wrote from a position where you can see without being seen.

The diary records Michinaga's great occasions and the birth of an imperial prince, and also her own awkwardness at them — she notes that colleagues found her hard to approach, and that the socializing wore her out. She also left an unusually unkind verdict on a fellow writer, remarking that Sei Shōnagon (author of The Pillow Book) and her pleased-with-herself habit of showing off Chinese characters would come to no good end. She was almost physically wary of self-display.

More revealing still: she knew Chinese. As a child she picked it up faster than her brother while listening to their father's lessons, and he is recorded lamenting that she was not a boy. At court she deliberately hid the accomplishment, pretending she could not read the characters on a screen. The unobtrusive omissions in this novel, and that "I only have this at second hand" tone of voice, belong to the same woman who hid what she knew.

Inside the fiction, three preoccupations recur: position determines fate — from the Kiritsubo Consort to Ukifune, every suffering grows out of which slot you occupy; time completes every symmetry — not retribution so much as settlement; and what matters most cannot be transmitted — the crucial information in this book is almost never spoken, only seen, smelled, or inferred from a poem about a plant. Across the three parts: the first is about acquisition, the second about beginning to lose what was acquired, and the third about the desire to acquire becoming suspect in itself. What Kaoru wants was never a particular woman; it was a reason to believe a life is worth living, and the book's answer is that there isn't one.

Criticism and controversy

To put it all on the table, several of the objections to this book are real, and "classic" is not an answer to them:

  • Genji and Murasaki: grooming. The hero takes a girl of about ten into his house and raises her into his wife. The usual excuse is "different era," but note that the characters themselves treat it as out of order: the grandmother refuses the proposal on the grounds that the child is far too young, and the removal itself is close to abduction. This is not a charge modern readers imported; the text is aware of it.
  • The Third Princess and Kashiwagi: this is a rape. The prose is oblique, the facts are not: she is unprepared, terrified, and takes vows the moment she has given birth. Yet the episode has for centuries been read as "Kashiwagi's tragic love," with the sympathy going almost entirely to the man who dies. Current scholarship is right to press on that reading tradition.
  • Authorship. The Uji chapters differ in style from what comes before, and doubts about single authorship are old. The mainstream view still credits the whole work to her, but the question is not closed. In addition, no authorial manuscript survives: our standard text descends from recensions assembled by Fujiwara no Teika and others, and the earliest surviving copies postdate composition by a century or two.
  • Which book did you read? The original is effectively a foreign language for modern Japanese readers, most of whom read a modern translation — Yosano Akiko, Tanizaki Jun'ichirō, Enchi Fumiko and Setouchi Jakuchō all made one, and they differ substantially. In English, Arthur Waley (1925–33) is gorgeous but freely rewritten and omits material outright; Seidensticker (1976) is accurate at the cost of flattening; Tyler (2001) preserves the indirection and the shifting titles at the cost of being hard going. Two people who say they have read The Tale of Genji have frequently not read the same book.
  • It genuinely is difficult. No names, designations that change with office, dense allusion and poetry, glacial plotting: this is a book that asks a great deal, and a high abandonment rate is not the reader's fault.

Ten sentences to take away

1 · The subject is not romance but position: every pain in the book grows out of which slot you occupy, and even love is issued by rank.

2 · One concealed adultery first carries Genji to the summit and then, two decades later, returns in the identical shape — the reckoning here is not catastrophe, it is symmetry.

3 · Holding that baby, he finally stands where his father stood: the heaviest punishment this book administers is comprehension.

4 · A cat, a cord, three seconds of sightline, three dead people — nobody did anything wrong, so this is not a moral tale but a tale of probability and position.

5 · The Rokujō lady's resentment never announces itself, it only takes effect; the most frightening evidence in the book is a smell that will not wash out.

6 · Murasaki was given nearly everything a woman of that world could not have, except the right to withdraw.

7 · Kaoru proves that self-examination which never alters behaviour is just a more refined way of looking after yourself.

8 · The final action of the novel is a woman already recorded as dead saying no to a letter.

9 · Mono no aware makes the book merciful to everyone and unusually lenient about responsibility — the finest feeler is repeatedly the one who did it.

10 · A book that spends a thousand pages on disappearance leaves its hero's death as a blank page: written out it would still be a scene; unwritten, it is the thing itself.
