Novels, Deep-Read · Liu Cixin · No.83

Engineering Spectacle

Butterfly · Sun of China · Cannonball · Mirror · Liu Cixin · 2002–2004

中文 →

On the Antarctic ice, a Yugoslav meteorologist walks out of a hut carrying a drum of gasoline. No more coordinates are coming. The computer on the other end has gone silent, and he no longer knows where to light the fire, or at what minute. He walks for a long time. Then he stops and sets the gasoline alight. The flame bends in the wind.
Thousands of miles away, the sky over Belgrade has cleared. His wife and his daughter are dead.
This fire can change nothing. Every fire he lit before it may have changed far too much.

Full spoilers ahead — including why Alexander's plan to defend his country with the weather fails, and how his wife and daughter die; where the orbiting mirror Shui Wa spent years polishing finally goes; why Shen Huabei, newly thawed, is thrown down a tunnel through the Earth, and what that tunnel becomes; and why the "mirror" that can see everything leads a corrupt official, just before his suicide, to make the hardest argument in the whole set to refute. Four short stories, each carrying its full weight in the ending. Without the endings there is nothing to talk about.

In One Line

Four short stories, four ideas pushed until they hit the wall: a man tries to change the clouds over a whole country with a single fire; a peasant's son polishes a mirror all the way from a parched village to interstellar space; a father's invention lets his son bore a tunnel through the belly of the Earth, and makes them both the most hated men alive; a software engineer rebuilds the universe inside a supercomputer, and after that nothing is secret. All four ask the same thing: when an idea is pushed all the way, where does it take people, and what does it leave by the side of the road?

Bearings

Liu Cixin (b. 1963) wrote these four while still working as a computer engineer at the Niangziguan power plant in Shanxi, in the years just before he started The Three-Body Problem. "Butterfly" appeared in early 2002 in Science Fiction King; "Sun of China" ran the same year in Science Fiction World and won that year's Galaxy Award; "Cannonball" was published in the September 2003 issue of Science Fiction World and also won a Galaxy Award; "Mirror" followed in Science Fiction World in 2004. An earlier English translation of "Cannonball" circulated as The Longest Fall.

They belong together because each is a whole story held up by a single machine or algorithm: the butterfly effect, an orbital reflector, a tunnel through the planet, a computer that simulates the universe. But the scale of the ideas is not the interesting part. What matters is that every one of them sends its bill to a particular person.

The Story

Act I · Butterfly: Weather as a Shield

On March 24, 1999, NATO begins bombing Yugoslavia. Alexander is a meteorologist in Belgrade, and his household is already carrying another weight: his daughter Katya is seriously ill and has had a kidney transplant. The kidney came from her mother, Elena. Setup ① After a transplant the great danger is rejection, and Katya cannot go a day without her drugs.

Alexander studies chaos. The atmosphere is a system with extreme sensitivity to initial conditions: a tiny disturbance, given enough time and enough amplification, can become a storm on the other side of the planet — the famous "butterfly effect." In his models he has found something he calls atmospheric sensitive points: particular places, at particular moments, where the slightest nudge is enough to send downstream weather onto a different track.

His idea is simple to the point of innocence. NATO's precision bombing depends on clear skies, so put clouds over Yugoslavia. He and his colleague Lieyiqi (the name as rendered in Chinese) split the work. Lieyiqi stays behind and fights for time on a supercomputer to calculate the location and moment of the next sensitive point; Alexander leaves his bombed country to go around the world flapping wings. His route takes him to Africa, to Japan, and finally to Antarctica. At each point the act required of him is absurdly small: at the exact minute the machine names, in the exact place, make a little heat — light a fire, say. Setup ②

For a while it seems to work. The clouds over Yugoslavia thicken. But every calculation takes a long run on a supercomputer, and for a country under bombardment that kind of machine time is the hardest thing in the world to get.

Act II · Butterfly: The Last Fire

Alexander waits in Antarctica for the last set of coordinates. They never come. Lieyiqi's computing job has failed. Without the disturbances, the overcast over Yugoslavia slowly breaks. The sky clears.

In those same days Katya's body begins to reject the kidney, and her fever will not come down. Elena goes to the hospital for medicine and, on the way home, is killed in a bombing raid. A few days later Katya dies of the rejection too. Payoff ① ↑ At the start, the kidney reads as background, one more hardship in a family's life: a mother gave part of herself to her child. Here it turns out to be the first link in the whole chain of death. Because it was a transplant, there was rejection; because there was rejection, there had to be medicine; because there had to be medicine, she was on that street at that moment. The kidney the mother gave away is what put the mother on that road. Liu places it at the very beginning because, in a story about initial conditions, it is the initial condition.

Lieyiqi tells him everything. Alexander carries a drum of gasoline out of the hut, walks across the ice for who knows how long, and lights it. Payoff ② ↑ As an action this fire is identical to all the others, and it means the opposite. Each earlier fire was aimed at a sensitive point and a precise instant: one man giving orders to an entire sky. This one is aimed at nothing at all. The same gesture, repeated, has gone from "changing the world" to "changing nothing" — and this is chaos at its cruelest: you can never know which kind of fire any given one was.

The story's last scene cuts to the other side of the ocean. An American general and a lieutenant colonel are discussing the weather over the Balkans these past weeks: abnormal, hard to forecast, enough to have thrown off their plans. They have no idea who Alexander is. Some readers find a colder layer in the exchange: that the man-made clouds shifted the bombing schedule, so that one particular raid happened to fall at the moment Elena was walking home. The story stops at the hint and never says it outright. In a story about the butterfly effect, not saying it is the honest choice, because not even the author can lift cause and effect cleanly out of chaos.

Act III · Sun of China: A List of Goals

The second story begins in a desperately dry mountain village in China's northwest. Shui Wa — his name means roughly "water boy" (the English translation renames him Ah Quan) — grew up drinking bitter water. When he leaves home, his first goal is modest: drink water that isn't bitter, and earn a little money. Setup ③

He works at a mine, then shines shoes in town, and with every move the goal pushes one notch outward: a city with more lights and sweeter water, more money; then a bigger city, to see the world. At last he reaches Beijing and takes up high-rise cleaning — dangling from a rope outside glass curtain walls dozens of floors up, washing windows. In Beijing he gets to know a down-and-out young researcher named Zhuang Yu, who is tinkering with an ultra-thin nano-mirror film.

That film becomes the key material of a national project: the Sun of China, a giant reflector in orbit that bounces sunlight onto chosen regions of the ground to adjust the climate and bring rain to places that have none. Once it is built, a problem follows. Particles in space dull the mirror's surface and its reflectivity slowly falls. Someone has to go up and clean it. People who know how to work hanging off skyscrapers are suddenly the people space needs most.

So Shui Wa goes up, and becomes a mirror cleaner. The job gets a nickname that is both rustic and exactly right: mirror farmers, peasants working a reflective field that stretches past the horizon.

Act IV · Sun of China: Sailing the Mirror to the Stars

In orbit Shui Wa meets someone he could never have imagined meeting: Stephen Hawking. In the story, Hawking lives in space, where the low gravity is kinder to his body, and his favorite pastime is going for "walks" on the back of the mirror. Shui Wa is assigned to go with him.

The mirror's front faces the Earth; its back faces the universe. Walking beside the old man who can speak only through a machine, Shui Wa hears for the first time how large the universe is and how small humanity is, and hears the question: why did people stop after reaching the Moon? He once put the same question to an official, and got an answer that amounted to this: ideals are fine, but economic return is better.

Meanwhile, down on the ground, the Sun of China does what it was built to do. With the reflector's help, Shui Wa's bitter-water village in the northwest is no longer in drought. Payoff ③ ↑ This is the quietest payoff in the whole set. The boy who left home wanting only water that wasn't bitter never went back — and sent the rain home from orbit. His first goal has been met, and the way it was met is that he has traveled so far that the first goal is no longer a goal for him at all.

The Sun of China reaches the end of its service life. Speaking for the mirror cleaners, Shui Wa makes an outrageous proposal: don't scrap it — turn it into a ship. A huge, featherlight reflector is already a solar sail. Sunlight pushes with almost no force, but it never stops, and that is enough to ease the mirror out of the solar system. He wins over Lu Hai, now the minister in charge of the space industry. A few mirror farmers volunteer to go. The mirror passes Mars, passes Jupiter, crosses the orbit of Pluto, still accelerating; the crew rotate between hibernation and waking, and anything they send toward Earth will wait an immensely long time for a reply. Shui Wa says goodbye to his parents. It is a one-way trip.

The list of goals never had an end. Each one met became the starting line for the next. Liu writes a life as a radius that keeps growing, from the village gate to the stars.

Act V · Cannonball: The Father Wakes

The third story opens in an age of nuclear disarmament. The physicist Shen Huabei works on destroying dismantled warheads by detonating them deep underground. In the process he discovers something: if a material is wrapped in a special "sugar coating" and then crushed by the pressure of a nuclear blast, it stays afterward in an extraordinarily dense, extraordinarily hard state. He calls it the new solid state. Setup ④ It is the proudest discovery of his life.

But he has leukemia, already advanced. He chooses hibernation, to wait for a future medicine that can cure him.

He wakes seventy-four years later. There are no flowers and no welcome. A group of young people drag him out of bed and shove him to the floor, and someone tells him he owes society, and now he can finally pay back a little.

It takes him a long time to piece together what happened while he slept. His son, Shen Yuan, led a project without precedent: the Antarctic Courtyard — a dead-straight tunnel from Mohe, at China's northern tip, through the Earth to Antarctica. A train drops in at one end, gravity accelerates it, momentum carries it up the far side, and you are in Antarctica in forty-two minutes. The southern continent became China's back yard, and people poured in to exploit it. The tunnel walls were made of Shen Huabei's new solid state. Payoff ④ ↑ The opening chapter read like neat technical preamble; only now does it show its weight. The debt the mob demands from him is not entirely misplaced anger: without the father's invention, there is no son's tunnel. A substance discovered while locking away the force of nuclear bombs became the road to a different disaster.

Disaster followed disaster. During construction magma burst into the tunnel; to save the people above, a safety gate was shut, sealing more than a thousand workers below. Another accident in the tunnel destroyed trains. Antarctica was overexploited and the environmental costs came due; the tunnel was eventually closed, and a long economic catastrophe and social upheaval followed. Shen Yuan became one of the most hated people in history. He is dead, so the hatred has landed on his still-living father.

The story also hides a detail that makes readers of "With Her Eyes" go cold: Shen Yuan's daughter, Shen Jing, is the sole survivor aboard a geo-ship that sank to the center of the Earth. Many readers recognize her — the young woman who borrowed a stranger's eyes for one last look at the grasslands has, here, a name and a grandfather.

Act VI · Cannonball: The Longest Fall

The sentence the crowd hands down uses his son's tunnel. He is sealed into something like a spacesuit and dropped down the shaft toward the center of the Earth.

He begins to fall. In a straight tunnel with no air resistance, a body keeps accelerating, shoots through the lowest point, decelerates up toward the far end, then falls back again — in principle forever. That is the "longest fall" of the early English title. Deng Yang, one of the angry crowd, talks to him over the radio.

His suit does not have enough power left for another trip back. He is pulled out at the Antarctic end.

In the epilogue the cursed tunnel finds a second fate. People realize that a vacuum tube through the planet is already a gun barrel: drop a ship in at Mohe, keep accelerating it electromagnetically on the way down, and by the time it leaves the Antarctic end it is fast enough to escape the Earth — without burning a mountain's worth of fuel. The Antarctic Courtyard becomes the Earth Cannon, a door for humanity into space. Shen Huabei lives to see it. He believes what his son did will not be forgotten.

Act VII · Mirror: A Man Who Knows Too Much

The fourth story opens with a frame-up. Song Cheng is an official investigating a corruption case, and just as the case gets close to something, he is set up: a male dancer dies in Song's car, after calling the police to say Song is going to kill him. Song goes to prison.

Then something strange begins. Someone keeps phoning the police chief and laying out the inside of the case and the police's own plans in perfect detail — things the chief believed only a handful of people in a single room could know. The chief is baffled, and frightened. Setup ⑤

The man who comes to visit Song in prison is young: Bai Bing, a software engineer at a weather-simulation center. He admits the calls were his.

His explanation is stranger than any conspiracy. He got access to a superstring computer, a machine of absurd computational power, and used it for something apparently pointless: simulating the Big Bang. Feed in a set of initial parameters, let a universe evolve from a singularity, see what grows. He tried set after set; most universes collapsed or dispersed at birth. Then, once, by sheer chance, he hit the initial parameters of our own universe. The simulated universe matched the real one exactly: the same galaxies, the same Earth, the same cities, the same people doing the same things at the same moment. He could push the camera into any place at any time, see any face, hear any sentence. He calls it the mirror.

Now the phone calls have their answer. Payoff ⑤ ↑ The chief's fear first reads like a stock thriller device — an all-knowing mole. The truth is far worse than a mole: there is no longer any such thing as "something only a few people in the room know," because the room itself is inside the mirror. Liu hides a cosmic-scale premise inside the most clichéd hook in crime fiction. The reader, by habit, guesses at who leaked; the answer is that secrets no longer exist.

Act VIII · Mirror: The Chief's Last Lesson

Bai Bing is no crusader. His original plan was to use what he saw in the mirror to blackmail corrupt officials and get rich. But the deeper he looked, the further the web led — all the way to the top, to a senior provincial official referred to only as "the Chief." Now he is being hunted, and he has come to Song for help.

Song reports Bai Bing and the mirror to his superiors, and the central leadership decides to bring Bai to Beijing. The web tears. The Chief knows he has lost.

Before the end he does something unexpected: he uses the mirror to watch again the happiest times of his own life. Then he meets Song, calmly concedes that they have won, and tells him the mirror's destructive power goes far beyond ruining men like himself. He describes the world after the mirror goes public: every act of every person retrievable; no crime that can stay hidden, and so no one daring to commit any — and no one daring to do anything out of line at all. It would be, he says, a dead society. He leaves Song a final instruction: destroy the mirror, delete every backup, get rid of Bai Bing. Then he ends his life with a bullet.

The story does not stop there. It adds a brief coda: across the ocean, an American professor is hard at work, independently arriving at the same invention. You can smash a mirror, but the road that leads to it is already built. Whatever Song decides, he can only affect where it appears, and how many years later.

One Fuse

Put the four stories side by side and the fuse is the same: it isn't technology itself that does the damage — it's that each technology finds, in some person, the direction it most wants to go, and is pushed all the way. The butterfly effect is a theory proving weather can't be predicted, and Alexander uses it to command the sky. The mirror was built to make rain, and Shui Wa sails it to the stars. The new solid state was meant to lock away nuclear blasts, and Shen Yuan uses it to bore through the planet. Bai Bing was only playing with the Big Bang, and ends up abolishing secrets. Every time, the idea travels farther than the person who made it, and the bill is always sent to whoever stands closest — a wife, a son, a father, a Chief.

Three Slow-Motion Scenes

Slow Motion I: Two Lines Collide in One Moment

"Butterfly" cuts back and forth between two lines. One follows Alexander around the globe and grows ever more abstract — coordinates, timestamps, models. The other stays in the Belgrade apartment and grows ever more concrete — pills, temperatures, sirens.

Its force comes from the order in which information arrives. Elena's and Katya's deaths are not staged in the Belgrade line as they happen. They reach Alexander secondhand, through Lieyiqi, at the bottom of the world. So the reader learns of them with him — on the ice, as far from home as he can be, at the very moment he has lost his power over the sky. Liu lays the moment of greatest helplessness exactly on top of the moment of greatest need. Only then comes the drum of gasoline. That fire takes the breath away because it is the one thing a scientist can still do after giving up on science.

Slow Motion II: A Walk on the Back of the Mirror

The finest image in "Sun of China" is not the mirror throwing sunlight onto the ground. It is two people walking slowly along the mirror's back: the most famous physicist in the world, confined to a wheelchair and speaking through a machine, and a cleaner from a northwestern village who has barely been to school.

The scene works by keeping the point of view very low. Liu never lets Hawking give a cosmology lecture; we only ever hear the part Shui Wa can follow — the universe is huge, humans are small, why did humanity stop? Heard through the ears of someone who knows almost no physics, the big questions lose their academic varnish and become one man's questions. His own life is a specimen of refusing to stop. The mirror's front solves the most concrete problem there is (rain for the northwest); its back hosts the most abstract conversation there is (where humanity goes). One sheet of film, a different life on each side.

Slow Motion III: The Chief Watches His Life

The most surprising stroke in "Mirror" is giving the villain one turn at the mirror, at the very end — not to spy on anyone, but to watch the happiest days of his own life.

What it does is present a person before the verdict. Until then the Chief is the top of a web, a frightening shadow. After watching his life, he becomes someone who remembers, who has aged. Immediately he delivers the weightiest speech in the story. Coming from a repellent crook, a reader could dismiss it as special pleading; coming from a man who has just said goodbye to his own life, you can't. Liu makes you pity him for one second so that you have to take him seriously. It is the story's slyest ethical move, and its most honest.

The Big Ideas, Taken Seriously

Sensitive points: the theory is real, the remote control is fiction. Chaos is a genuine mathematical property. In the early 1960s the meteorologist Edward Lorenz found in a computer simulation that a difference in the fourth or so decimal place of the starting data could make the weather weeks later unrecognizable. But the theorem cuts both ways: precisely because the system is so sensitive to tiny disturbances, you cannot calculate what your own disturbance will do — the precision and computing power required explode as the forecast reaches further out. "Find a sensitive point, flap once, precisely, and make it cloudy over a chosen country" cannot be done; it is chaos theory run backwards. Interestingly, the story's own ending sides with the science: Alexander ends up controlling nothing, and cannot even know whether his disturbances killed his family.

Orbital reflectors and light sails: real precedents, fictional scale. In 1993 Russia actually unfurled a reflector about twenty meters across in orbit (the Znamya experiment) and swept a faint spot of light across the ground. Radiation pressure is real physics too: photons carry momentum, and Japan's IKAROS demonstrated solar-sail propulsion in 2010. What fails is the scale. To change rainfall over a large region, a mirror would need to cover thousands of square kilometers, stay thin and undistorted, and aim precisely; pushed by sunlight alone to another star, the trip takes millennia. The story covers that time with hibernation and honestly doesn't pretend it is quick.

Forty-two minutes: the hardest real physics in the set. Assume an Earth of uniform density and a frictionless, airless tunnel: an object dropped into any straight tunnel through the planet reaches the far end in about forty-two minutes, regardless of the tunnel's length and whether it passes through the center. This is the classic "gravity train" of physics classes. Mohe and Antarctica are not antipodes, so the Antarctic Courtyard runs along a chord that misses the center — still forty-two minutes. What fails is the tunnel itself. Thousands of kilometers down are temperatures of thousands of degrees and pressures of millions of atmospheres; a "new solid state" that shrugs them off for good is a story premise. And turning a gravity train into a cannon means leaving at more than 11.2 kilometers per second, Earth's escape velocity. Free fall alone can't provide that, which is why the story adds electromagnetic acceleration — an honest touch.

The mirror: three walls it can't climb. First, quantum mechanics says microscopic events are genuinely random; identical initial parameters need not produce a simulated universe that tracks the real one. The mirror assumes a nineteenth-century worldview — Laplace's demon, an intellect that knows every initial condition and so can compute all of history. Second, a machine that simulates the universe particle by particle is itself inside the universe; it would have to be at least as large as the universe. Third, the parameter space is infinite; the odds of "hitting it by chance" are effectively zero. So the mirror is a thought experiment, not a forecast: its question isn't "could we build it?" but "what would society become with no secrets left?" Note, too, that Bai Bing's day job is weather simulation — like Alexander, he models the weather. In one story a meteorologist learns he can't even forecast tomorrow's rain; in the other a meteorologist computes the entire universe. Liu lets each worldview run to its end.

What It Forces You to Think About

Question 1 · Would you choose a society with no secrets?

Make it concrete. Suppose that starting tomorrow anything anyone has ever done can be looked up — yours, your boss's, the Chief's — and the technology already exists. Do you vote to make it public, or to smash it?

The case for making it public: the opening of the story is the answer. Song Cheng is caught in a web, a killing is pinned on him, and nobody can prove his innocence. In a world with secrets, secrets protect, first of all, the people able to manufacture them — the powerful, the rich, the well-connected. What is an ordinary person's privacy worth, set against that? The wrongly accused and the wronged need nothing so much as a mirror. The Chief says transparency would kill society — but that is the verdict of a man who lived his whole life off opacity.

The case for smashing it: yet the Chief's argument doesn't depend on who he is. A world where every act is recorded and verifiable would indeed have no crime — and no transgression, no trial and error, no heresy either. Every new idea is out of order when it is born; every social advance has a stretch of history that could only be said in private and done in the dark. Total transparency abolishes not only evil but also "the good that hasn't been permitted yet." In a society where everyone is always watched, everyone becomes the safest version of themselves. That is what a dead society looks like.

Where does Liu stand? He gives the Chief the strongest argument, then uses the American professor in the coda to tell you that you may never get a vote. The structure plays out in your own life daily — phone location, chat logs, cameras, facial recognition. We never voted for transparency all at once. We let it happen piece by piece, each time for some specific benefit.

Question 2 · A great project kills more than a thousand people. Should the man who led it be hated?

Put it in the story's terms. Shen Yuan's tunnel killed more than a thousand people, hollowed out an economy and wrecked a continent's environment; seventy years later the same tunnel is humanity's gateway to space. If you were a descendant of someone who died in it, would you hate him? If you rode the Earth Cannon into orbit, would you thank him?

The case for hatred: the gains of the living can't be used to settle the deaths of the dead. Those thousand-plus workers never agreed to be the price of a great undertaking, and neither did their families. When the gate closed, someone decided their lives were worth less than the lives above. "History will prove him right" is a sentence only survivors get to say. And crediting the founder with a use that only appeared later is writing a blank check for every reckless project to come.

The case against: by that standard, the leaders of nearly every great human project deserve hatred — every railway, every dam, every voyage had its accidents and its dead. No one knows before breaking ground what a project will finally become; demanding that a project prove its worth at the outset is demanding that humanity only do what has already been proven. A society that takes no risks doesn't lose those thousand lives, and it never gets an Earth Cannon either. And pouring an era's rage onto a dead man and his newly thawed father is scapegoating in its own right.

Where does the story stand? English-language reviewers complained that society's fury at the Shen family is never fully justified in the text, that it looks like a hunt for a scapegoat — but that may be the point. The closing belief that his son's work won't be forgotten is a father's judgment, not the story's verdict. You meet the same structure in life: a reform makes one generation suffer and the next one prosper. Does the first generation have the right to hate it?

Question 3 · If you can't calculate the consequences, should you act at all?

Everything Alexander does rests on a paradox he understands better than anyone: the theory he is using tells him that what his disturbances will cause is unpredictable. In his place, would you light the first fire?

The case against acting: if you know the consequences of your action can't be calculated, you are not rescuing anyone; you are rolling dice, and other people will live with the roll. He wants clouds to shield his country, but clouds also change rain elsewhere, harvests elsewhere, flight plans elsewhere; his disturbances may, as some readers suspect, have put the bombs on his own wife's road home. Tampering with a chaotic system means making a decision for the whole world that you cannot answer for.

The case for acting: but not acting is also an act. The bombs fall every day; if you make no clouds, the outcome is just as unpredictable — you simply don't have to own it, so you feel better. Nearly every important decision — surgery or not, a new job or not, resist or not — is made without knowing the consequences. If "the outcome is unknowable" were a reason not to act, no action would ever be justified. Alexander at least used the one thing he knew how to do for the people he loved.

Liu barely takes a side. He lets Alexander fail completely without ever mocking him. That last fire in Antarctica can be read as satire of every "scientific intervention," or as an elegy for a man who tried what he knew could not be done. Both readings have their defenders.

Motifs: What This Writer Keeps Writing

Placed back in Liu's larger body of work, these four show two obsessions he returns to again and again.

The first is the will of an idea. In Liu's fiction technology is never just a tool; it behaves as if it had a direction of its own, and once built it grows along its own logic while humans run after it — the sophons of The Three-Body Problem, the macro-atom weapons of Ball Lightning, the button in The Cretaceous Past that no one dares to press. These four stories are the motif at its barest: smash one mirror and a second appears; open a tunnel through the Earth and sooner or later it becomes a cannon.

The second is the urge outward. Shui Wa walking from his village gate to interstellar space is Liu's plainest statement: a humanity that stays on Earth is staying in a valley. In "Mountain" the same urge is voiced by an alien civilization; in the third volume of the trilogy it becomes something a person can give centuries to. His "far away" is rarely romantic. It always costs a home, a family, the return ticket — but he almost never doubts that the bill is worth paying.

Controversies and Criticism

  • More exposition than fiction. The most common English-language complaint about "Cannonball": long stretches of technical and historical explanation crowd out characters and scenes, so it tells rather than shows. The Chief's long speech in "Mirror" has the same problem.
  • A rushed ending. Many readers feel "Mirror" wraps up too fast and too clumsily: a web investigated for half the story collapses almost instantly once it is "reported upward," and the Chief's speech is made to carry too much of the argument.
  • Sentimentality and the national story. "Sun of China" is often described as warm, inspiring and a bit sentimental soft SF. Some critics find a peasant's son rising to the stars by way of a state megaproject too neatly aligned with a development narrative, smoothing over the real lives of migrant workers. Hawking's cameo splits readers: moving to some, celebrity endorsement to others.
  • The point of view in "Butterfly." The story stands entirely with Yugoslav civilians under bombardment; the other context of the 1999 war — the ethnic conflict in Kosovo and what happened to Kosovo Albanian civilians — is almost absent. In its defense: the story is about one family, not a full account of a war. But that choice is itself a position, and readers should see it.
  • Women on the page. Elena and Katya function almost entirely as sufferers; no woman in the four stories drives the plot. The familiar criticism of Liu still holds here.
  • Controversy around the author. Some of Liu's statements in interviews on contemporary Chinese political issues have drawn controversy in the West and have shaped how some readers approach his work — including stories like these, which involve state megaprojects and international conflict. This is stated as verifiable fact, without judgment.

Ten Lines to Take Away

① Four ideas, four people who pay: a wife, a son, a father, a Chief.

② The kidney Elena gave her daughter is the true initial condition of a story about initial conditions.

③ The last fire in Antarctica is the same gesture as all the others with the opposite meaning — and chaos means you can't tell which fire was which.

④ Never saying whether his clouds brought the bombs is the story's most honest tribute to chaos theory.

⑤ Shui Wa wanted water that wasn't bitter; he never went home, but he sent the rain home from orbit.

⑥ A list of goals that kept moving outward, from the village gate to the stars — one way.

⑦ The new solid state was made to lock away nuclear blasts and ended up boring through the planet; the debt the mob demands is not all misplaced anger.

⑧ Forty-two minutes is real physics: free fall through any straight tunnel through the Earth takes the same time.

⑨ The mirror abolishes secrets and with them "the good not yet permitted" — the Chief's argument, hard to refute because it doesn't depend on who he is.

⑩ Smash one mirror and there will be another: in Liu's fiction, ideas always travel farther than the people who make them.
