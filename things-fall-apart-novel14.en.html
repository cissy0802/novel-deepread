Novel Deep-Read · Chinua Achebe · No.14

Things Fall Apart

Things Fall Apart · Chinua Achebe · 1958 · 25 chapters

中文 →

A forest path, most of a day's walking. The boy in front carries a pot on his head and moves easily, because the men have told him he is being taken home.
As he walks he sings a childhood song to himself. The song is also a game: whichever foot comes down on the last syllable decides the question. Right foot, his mother is alive. Left foot, she is not. He finishes the song. It lands on his right.
Behind him walks a line of men carrying machetes. The one at the very back is the man he has called father for three years.
When the pot hits the ground and breaks, the boy turns and runs towards that man, calling out to him.
The man raises his machete. What he is afraid of is not the child's blood. It is that the men beside him will think him weak.

This page contains complete spoilers — how Ikemefuna dies, why Okonkwo is exiled for seven years, why Nwoye is baptised, whose head the last machete stroke takes off, which tree Okonkwo is found hanging from, and what the District Commissioner on the final page intends to do with all of it. The reason is the usual one here: this novel's force is entirely a matter of sequence. You have to be convinced the world is whole before you watch it come apart. Without the ending, all that's left is a set of ethnographic notes.

In one sentence

In an Igbo village in eastern Nigeria at the end of the nineteenth century, the most respected man of his generation is driven his whole life by one thing: never to become his father, who was soft, in debt, and denied even a grave. That drive wins him everything he understands how to win — wrestling, yams, titles, war honours — and it also makes him cut down a boy who called him father, and then, at a funeral, kills another boy when his gun bursts, which costs him seven years of exile. He comes home to missionaries, loses his eldest son to them, and on the day the whole village declines to fight he beheads a court messenger and then hangs himself from a tree behind his compound — a suicide his own people are forbidden to touch, because it is an offence against the earth. And there is still one page to go: the English District Commissioner, standing under that tree, thinks the man might be worth a paragraph in the book he is writing, The Pacification of the Primitive Tribes of the Lower Niger.

Coordinates

Chinua Achebe (1930–2013) was Igbo, born at Ogidi in eastern Nigeria. His father was a teacher and catechist for the Church Missionary Society — which is to say he was not observing this collision from outside it; he was born in the seam it left: scripture at home, the old order still running a few compounds away. He was baptised Albert, after Queen Victoria's consort, and dropped the name at university, keeping only the Igbo one.

He read English at University College Ibadan. By his own repeated account, what pushed him into writing fiction was the colonial novels on the syllabus — Joyce Cary's Nigerian book Mister Johnson above all. He noticed that in those books he had been assigned to the scenery, and that the authors evidently thought this was fine. Things Fall Apart was published by Heinemann in London in 1958 in a first printing of about two thousand copies; four years later Heinemann launched the African Writers Series and made it number one. The publisher and press accounts put it since past twenty million copies and fifty-odd languages.

The title is borrowed from Yeats's "The Second Coming," written in 1919: Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold. The move is worth registering — an African novelist naming the collapse of an African village with an Irish poet's line about the collapse of Europe. His second book, No Longer at Ease, took its title from Eliot. Its position in literary history is one sentence long: not "the first novel written by an African" (that claim doesn't survive contact with the facts), but the first novel to put the other side of the colonial encounter in the narrating seat and be read inside the English-speaking world as literature rather than as evidence. What it changed was not technique. It was who gets to speak.

The story

Act One · A man afraid of turning into his father

Umuofia is a federation of nine villages on the lower Niger. Okonkwo made his name at eighteen by throwing Amalinze the Cat, unbeaten for seven years; word travelled beyond the nine villages. Twenty years on he is one of the most substantial men in the place: three wives, two full barns, two titles, five human heads taken in two inter-village wars, and at the great feasts he drinks his palm wine from the first of those skulls. This society honours competence, courage and men who keep their word, and it measures all three in yams, titles and enemy heads.

All of it starts from a negative. His father Unoka was the village joke: a flute player, a talker, a man who borrowed and never repaid, who died without a single title. Igbo has a word, agbala, that means woman and also means a man who has taken no title. It landed on Unoka, and it lodged in his son. Unoka died of a swelling sickness held to be an offence against Ani, the earth goddess who governs land and morality and matters more than any other deity here. Such a body may not go into the ground: he was carried into the Evil Forest, where the village discards what is unclean, and left there. No funeral, no grave. Seed ①

Okonkwo started from nothing: he begged eight hundred seed yams from a rich man, Nwakibie, on a sharecropping basis. The first year happened to be the worst in living memory — drought, then rain that rotted the crop in the ground. A man in the village hanged himself that season. Okonkwo survived it, and concluded that having survived that year he could survive anything. There is a proverb here: when a man says yes, his chi says yes also — a chi being the personal god each person is born with, roughly the part of your fate that countersigns your decisions. People used the proverb about Okonkwo. Seed ②

The cost is that nothing in this man gives. He dislikes his eldest son Nwoye, who would rather hear his mother's stories than his father's campaigns; he favours his daughter Ezinma, and expresses it by sighing that she should have been a boy. During the Week of Peace — the days before planting when all quarrelling is forbidden lest the earth goddess be offended — he beats his youngest wife for being late with his meal anyway. Ani's priest comes to his compound and rebukes him, and fines him a she-goat, a hen, a length of cloth and a hundred cowries. He pays, and never says he was wrong. What the village remembers is not the offence. It is the look on his face as he paid.

Act Two · The boy who called him father

Someone from the neighbouring village of Mbaino killed a woman of Umuofia at the market. Umuofia sent Okonkwo with an ultimatum: war, or a young man and a virgin in compensation. Mbaino paid. The girl went to the dead woman's husband; the boy, Ikemefuna, was lodged with Okonkwo while the clan took its time deciding what to do with him. "While" lasted three years.

Those three years are the only warm stretch in the book. Ikemefuna knows things, makes things, is easy company; Nwoye follows him around like a younger brother and opens up. Okonkwo is genuinely fond of the boy and never once shows it, because in his vocabulary showing affection is weakness. The boy calls him father.

That year the locusts came. They came down over everything, settling on roofs and branches, and the whole village went out laughing to gather them by the basket, dried them and ate them fried. Here locusts are not a plague; they are dinner falling out of the sky, and the children were too excited to sleep. Seed ③

Within days the Oracle of the Hills and the Caves — Agbala, whom the clan consults before anything serious — gave its ruling: Ikemefuna must be killed. Ezeudu, the oldest man in the quarter, walked to Okonkwo's compound with his staff and put it as plainly as it can be put. He would not argue with what the clan had to do, but that boy calls you father, so have no hand in his death. Seed ④

The next day Okonkwo went along. A party of men with machetes, and a lie told to the boy about going home. Deep in the forest the man behind him struck first; the pot fell and broke, and Ikemefuna turned and ran towards Okonkwo.

"My father, they have killed me!"— Chapter 7, the last thing Ikemefuna says

Okonkwo cut him down. The novel supplies exactly one reason: he was afraid of being thought weak.

For two days afterwards he cannot eat. He drinks, and sits up at night. He goes to his friend Obierika — the clearest head in the book — hoping to be told it was nothing, and is told this instead: it is the kind of act the earth goddess punishes by wiping out whole families; the Oracle said the boy would die, it did not say you should be the one to kill him. Okonkwo answers that the clan decided and he carried it out. Obierika does not argue further. He says only: then I would not have gone.

And in Nwoye something snapped. The novel says he had felt the identical thing once before: not long earlier he had happened to hear infants crying in the bush — by local rule twins are an abomination, and are put in a pot and left in the Evil Forest at birth. He had questioned nothing. But the sound stayed in him. Seed ⑤ The night Ikemefuna did not come back, the two breaks joined up.

Act Three · A gun goes off

Life continues. Ekwefi, the second wife, has borne ten children and buried nine; only Ezinma survives, and the village says she is an ogbanje, a child who keeps being born and keeps dying to torment its mother. One night the priestess Chielo is seized, announces that the Oracle wants Ezinma, and carries the child on her back around all nine villages in the dark, with Ekwefi following alone at a distance (that night gets its own slow-motion below).

In these same chapters the novel shows, unhurriedly, how this society runs: a bride-price negotiated by two families passing a bundle of sticks back and forth until they settle and start drinking; and the egwugwu — masked ancestral spirits, played by titled men of the clan, an open secret it is sacrilege to state aloud — sitting as a court of nine. The case that day is a wife-beating: the woman's brothers have taken her home. The spirits rule that the husband must bring wine to his in-laws and ask her back. The point is not local colour. The point is that there is law here: procedure, a neutral party, a ruling that can be enforced. That is precisely what gets dismantled later.

Then Ezeudu dies. The oldest man in the quarter gets an enormous funeral — dancing, drums, guns fired into the air. Okonkwo's gun bursts, and a fragment of iron goes into the heart of Ezeudu's sixteen-year-old son. Payoff ④ ↑ The old man who came specially to tell him not to kill a boy is the man at whose funeral he kills a boy. What makes the stroke beautiful is that it looks nothing like retribution: it is an accident, a purely mechanical failure. The heaviest causal link in the book is placed inside the most meaningless piece of chance available.

By Igbo reckoning this is a "female ochu," an inadvertent killing (deliberate killing is male), and the penalty is seven years of exile for the whole household. They leave that night. In the morning the men of the village come to his compound, burn the huts, kill the animals and level the barn — not out of hatred, but to cleanse the earth, a procedure somebody has to carry out. Obierika is among them. When it is done he sits down and asks himself, for the first time, a question he should not be asking: why should a man suffer so heavily for an offence he committed by accident? He thinks about his own twins, thrown away — what had they done? He has no answer and tells nobody. The first person in this clan to start doubting it is one of its most loyal members.

Act Four · Seven years: the motherland, and a new song

Exile is Mbanta, his mother's village, where his uncle Uchendu takes him in. There is a saying here, Nneka — Mother is Supreme. Uchendu assembles the young men of the family and asks Okonkwo in front of them why a woman, when she dies, is carried home to be buried among her own kin. Because your fatherland is where you live when things go well, and your motherland is what takes you in when they don't. Then he recites his own ledger: I have buried twenty-two children. Your grief is not the largest one here; stop treating it as a privilege.

Somewhere in these seven years the narrator stops and says something cold. Okonkwo's early success was always credited to the proverb — when a man says yes, his chi says yes. But look at the facts: a man cannot rise beyond the destiny of his chi; here is one who said yes and whose chi said no. Payoff ② ↑ The same proverb, unaltered by a syllable, is praise on the way up and a verdict on the way down.

Then the white men. The news arrives first from Abame, told by Obierika on a visit: a white man appeared in the village riding an iron horse — a bicycle, which nobody had seen — and the Oracle said he would break their clan and spread destruction, and that these were locusts, and this one was the scout sent ahead. The villagers killed him and tied the iron horse to their sacred tree. Months later three white men and a large body of other men picked a market day, surrounded the market, and shot almost everyone in it. Abame is now empty. Seed ⑥Payoff ③ ↑ The locusts the whole village once gathered up and ate now mean something else — the first arrivals really were a scouting party, and locusts were never a metaphor for catastrophe. They are simply what quantity looks like.

The missionaries reach Mbanta. They ask for land to build on, and the elders confer and give them the Evil Forest — the ground where twins and unclean dead are discarded. The elders count the days privately: twenty-eight at the outside, and the gods will have dealt with them. Twenty-eight days passed and nobody died. That one fact was worth a hundred sermons.

The first to come across are the people this society has no place for: the efulefu, men of no weight, men without titles; then the osu, outcasts dedicated to a deity, forbidden to cut their hair, forbidden to marry the freeborn, thrown into the Evil Forest when they die. The church takes them in and lets them cut their hair. And then a woman pregnant with her fifth child, the previous four having all been twins, all of whom she threw away herself, as required.

This is how Nwoye goes. What reaches him first is a hymn about brothers who sat in darkness. He cannot explain it; he only feels that something permanently lodged in his throat has somewhere to go at last: what the infants in the bush were crying about, and why Ikemefuna had to die. Payoff ⑤ ↑ He is not persuaded. He is answered — and the church wins because it catches the people and the questions this society threw out. Okonkwo takes his son by the throat to interrogate him and is stopped by his uncle; after that he treats the boy as nonexistent, and thinks of himself as a living fire wondering how it begot cold ash.

As the seven years end, Mbanta gives him a farewell feast. An old man says a few bleak things over the wine: a man can now leave his father and his brothers — can we still put our hands together and do one thing the way we used to? What worries him is not theology. It is the drive shaft of the community.

Act Five · The man came back to somewhere else

He returns to Umuofia with a plan: a bigger compound, two more wives, the highest titles bought for his sons. Seven years spent working out how to come back in style. The result is that nobody particularly notices he is back. The village is full of other business — a church, a school, a hospital, the white man's court, and young men who have taken salaried work as mission clerks.

The mission has been run in these years by Mr. Brown, a reasonable man. He forbids his converts to provoke the local gods, spends time with the elders, and has one serious argument with a leader named Akunna, who points out that their own Chukwu is likewise the one high god and the carved figures merely his messengers, in the way the Commissioner has messengers. Brown builds a school and a dispensary and offers a supremely practical reason for using them: if you don't send your children to school, the people who come from elsewhere to rule you will be somebody else's children. The statement is true, which is why it works.

Brown falls ill and goes home. His replacement is the Reverend James Smith, who sees in black and white and regards Brown's methods as appeasement. Under him the fiercest converts come into their own. One of them, Enoch — said to have killed and eaten the sacred python — publicly tears the mask off an egwugwu during the ceremony for the earth goddess. By the arithmetic used here, that is not an insult. That is killing an ancestor in the street.

The spirits come out, burn Enoch's compound, and go on to the church. Their leader is explicit: we will not harm you, you may stay and worship your god your own way, but this building cannot remain. They burn it and kill nobody. It is the last time in the novel that this clan acts as one whole body.

Days later the District Commissioner invites six leaders to "talk." They are handcuffed on arrival, their heads shaved, and they are held, starved and whipped. The price is two hundred bags of cowries; the court messengers ask for two hundred and fifty and keep the difference. The collapse is not epic. It is a few middlemen skimming fifty bags of cowries in the confusion.

The next day the whole village meets in the marketplace. A man stands and says their fathers never saw anything like this, and that these people must be driven out even if it means spilling the blood of their own brothers. At which point five court messengers walk in and order the meeting to disperse.

Okonkwo draws his machete and takes the head off the leader in one stroke.

What follows is the quietest two seconds in the book: the other messengers get away through the crowd, and nobody stops them. Out of the noise he hears people asking why he did it. Payoff ⑥ ↑ The empty village of Abame has just done everyone's arithmetic for them: following him means what happened at that market. Before he raised the machete he was a man of a clan. After, he was a man. He wipes the blade on the sand and walks away.

The Commissioner comes for him. Obierika leads the party round to a tree behind the compound, where Okonkwo is hanging. Suicide is an offence against the earth; the body is unclean, and his own people may not touch or bury it. Strangers have to take him down, after which the land must be cleansed. Payoff ① ↑ The father died in the Evil Forest with nobody to bury him; the son hangs from a tree with nobody permitted to touch him. The man who spent his whole life running from his father lands in his father's exact category. The harder he ran, the more precisely he arrived. Obierika turns on the Englishman and says, in effect: that man was one of the greatest in Umuofia, you drove him to kill himself, and now he will be buried like a dog.

On the last page the point of view moves, without warning, into the Commissioner's head. He is thinking about the book he is writing; the title has been settled for some time — The Pacification of the Primitive Tribes of the Lower Niger. The man who killed a messenger and then hanged himself strikes him as interesting, perhaps a whole chapter; on reflection, not a chapter, but a reasonable paragraph. He also reflects that writing this sort of book takes a firm hand for cutting out superfluous detail.

Where is the fuse? Not in the arrival of the white men. They are the wind, not the spark. The spark is a rule a boy set for himself years earlier at the edge of the Evil Forest: whatever the situation, never appear weak. Everything after that is the same rule being executed in different circumstances — not daring to stay out of Ikemefuna's killing, not daring to do anything less thoroughly than the next man, not daring to hold back when everyone else is silent, and at the end not daring to be led away alive. The colonisers do exactly one thing: they arrange for the final execution of that rule to happen in a year when nobody will follow him.

Three slow-motion scenes

1 · The forest path: he puts you inside the child, then kills him

Almost all of this novel is narrated at middle distance, in the manner of an elder recounting what happened and what people said about it, rarely entering anyone's head. The exception is the few pages of Ikemefuna's walk, where the point of view moves bodily into him: his legs are tiring, he thinks about his mother and his three-year-old sister, he runs the counting song to find out whether she is alive, he is pleased when it lands on his right foot. He notices his palms are sweating.

This is a precise technical action: pity scales with interior visibility, and the visibility is turned up to maximum in the last moments and then shut off. What makes it worse is that he doesn't know — the boy thinks he is walking home, while the reader already knows about the machetes behind him. Suspense is cancelled; only waiting is left. That isn't a thriller, it's a stay of execution.

Then the pacing. The killing itself takes a line and a half, with no blood and no struggle, and the longest sentence in the passage is spent on the reason: fear of being thought weak. Achebe refuses to give the stroke any weight, because it had none in the mind of the man who made it; the weight is issued late — the next full chapter is about a man who cannot eat, sits up at night, and goes looking for someone to talk to. Two seconds of action, a chapter and a half of aftershock.

2 · The night walk: an entire chapter in which nothing goes wrong

The priestess carries Ezinma around the villages in the dark and Ekwefi follows at a distance. No moon, only the voice ahead of her; she loses it several times and finds it again by the calling; she thinks about her nine dead children; at the mouth of the cave she decides that if anything happens to the child inside she will go in, even if the priestess kills her for it.

The technique is stretching time until it runs at the speed of fear: everywhere else the book fast-forwards (three years in a clause), and here it is metered by footsteps. The structural function is the better point — nothing happens that night, and the child comes back untouched. Why keep a long chapter that doesn't advance the plot? Because the reader has to genuinely love this world in order to grieve two hundred pages later when it goes: make the ordinary valuable first, and destruction acquires a price.

It also does one other thing: it is the only time in the book Okonkwo is visible in the dark. He has been up and down that path several times with his machete, says nothing about it, and admits nothing afterwards. This man's love can only occur where there is no audience — a single stroke that makes all his later cruelty legible.

3 · The last page: the right to narrate is taken from you in plain view

The final transition is the strongest thing in the book: the camera leaves the body, leaves Obierika, and moves into the District Commissioner's head. We think along with him — how the book should be written, the title already fixed, this hanged man being too much for a chapter and about right for a paragraph, and the knack of the genre being knowing what detail to cut.

The force comes from three drops. First, the entire book we have just finished is exactly the superfluous detail he intends to cut. Second, he is not evil — he doesn't gloat, he orders no massacre, he is thinking about prose style and even finds the man interesting. Achebe refuses to hand you a figure you can satisfyingly hate and gives you something worse instead: a harmless, professional, administrative failure to see. Third, Obierika's outburst is the last Igbo speech in the novel, and its listener takes in none of it.

There is one more layer in that title. Pacification converts massacres, fines and whippings into the making of peace. The whole of Things Fall Apart can be read as a line-by-line refutation of that one word, and Achebe puts the word on the last page and declines to comment on it.

The core apparatus: how this book is built

1 · The proportions are the argument

Thirteen of twenty-five chapters contain no white men. That isn't a slow start, it is the shape of the case: the most effective refutation of "the colonisers arrived at a blank space" is not an argument but an itemised schedule of what was going on in the blank — the planting calendar, how a bride-price is settled, how a case is tried, how a funeral is run, when fighting is forbidden, who has the standing to halt something. By the time the missionaries appear the reader has lived here for half a book, and every subsequent destruction is the destruction of something whose function is known. Ethnographic material used as dramatic setup.

2 · English made to accommodate Igbo, and proverbs used as load-bearing members

The book is in English, but the Igbo words are largely untranslated: chi, egwugwu, osu, ogbanje, efulefu sit in the sentences and hold their own on context. The speech rhythms are not English either — the indirection and figuration of Igbo carried into English syntax. Achebe turned this into a programme in his 1965 essay "The African Writer and the English Language": English may stay, provided it is altered until it can carry African experience. (That claim started the largest quarrel in African literary history — see the criticism section.)

Proverbs are the structural steel. The book says so itself: proverbs are the palm oil with which words are eaten. This society genuinely thinks in them — negotiations open with a proverb, cases are argued with proverbs, elders discipline with proverbs. So both of the book's heaviest reversals are performed on proverbs: "when a man says yes, his chi says yes" is praise in Part One and is overturned to his face by the narrator in Part Two; "Mother is Supreme" sounds like a pleasantry until misfortune reveals it as a real clause of social insurance. To take apart a world assembled out of proverbs, the cheapest method is to run one of its proverbs backwards.

3 · A Greek shape in Igbo material, and then no tears for you

Okonkwo's structure is standard tragedy: a fatal flaw (fear of weakness, past the point of illness), a reversal (the gun), and a chorus (Obierika, who says all the lucid things). But Achebe cuts the catharsis at the last moment. Classical tragedy ends in mourning — a body is claimed, a speech is made, the audience weeps, order resumes. This one ends with a foreign official estimating a word count. You are permitted to feel the tragedy but not to consume it: the ready-made, dignified grief is confiscated, and what's left is blunter and stays longer.

4 · The title is a borrowing and also a seizure

Yeats's poem is about Europe's own disintegration after the First World War — the centre failing, the falcon out of earshot of the falconer. Achebe lifts the line and fastens it to an Igbo village: the English-language reader walks in standing on familiar vocabulary (you think you know what this title means), and the book then tells you whose collapse it is and whose hands did it. Using the most respectable cabinet in the metropolitan literature to keep the colony's accounts — that was always his method.

What it forces you to think about

Question 1
The Oracle said the boy must die. It did not say you had to do it. Are Obierika's hands clean — and how much difference is there, really, between doing a society's necessary cruelty yourself and leaving it to someone else?

Siding with Obierika is easy, and his case is solid: you don't have to overturn the clan's rule, you only have to decline to be the instrument of it. An old man came to the compound and handed Okonkwo a way out, and Okonkwo trampled it. What he did was not obedience but a performance of obedience — he was not afraid of the god, he was afraid of the other men's eyes. Push "I had no choice" far enough and you usually find a volunteer hiding inside it.

Put the other side at full strength and it stings more. Obierika concedes that he too would not have defied the Oracle; his objection is only to doing it with his own hand. But if the boy dies either way, what does "I would not have gone" preserve — the boy, or Obierika's cleanliness? His clean hands are paid for by somebody else's dirty ones. Every society distributes its necessary cruelties — the tax collector, the executioner, the person who reads out the redundancies — and then looks down on them for having coarse hands. A morality whose entire content is "let someone else do that part" is a division of labour, not a character trait.

The dilemma doesn't need an oracle. Your organisation made a decision you disagreed with and you didn't resign; a different department carries it out; you nod to those people in the lift knowing you are a little cleaner than they are. How much is "a little"? The book gives no answer. It just sets the two men side by side and lets you measure.

Question 2
Mr. Brown or Reverend Smith — which of them does Umuofia more damage?

The intuitive answer is Smith. He arrives and the conflict escalates: a mask torn off, a church burned, elders in handcuffs. Brown does none of that — he debates Akunna as an equal, restrains his own converts, builds a school and a clinic. By any ordinary measure Brown is the good man and Smith is the disaster.

At full strength, the other side says: the man who actually dismantles Umuofia is Brown. Smith produces a visible enemy, and a visible enemy is what lets the clan unite one last time — the day they burn the church is the most fully themselves they ever are in the book. Brown supplies something else: a reason. Don't send your children to school, he says, and the people who come to rule you will be somebody else's children. That is true, and being true is what makes it lethal — it is not a threat, it is a forecast, delivered by one of the people arranging the weather. Under Brown nobody is beaten into anything; they work it out for themselves. The literate take salaried clerkships, the osu cut their hair and acquire names, the woman who threw away four sets of twins keeps her fifth child. A takeover with no victim narrative, leaving nobody to hate afterwards.

So the question becomes: if the outcome is identical, would you rather face the brutal version, so that resistance is at least justified — or the humane one, where fewer people die? And the sharper follow-up: when the options were drawn up by the people taking your world, how much is "they chose it freely" actually worth? This is not only a colonial question. It shows up in every restructuring that says we're not forcing anyone, it's just that the people who adapt fastest get promoted. The gentle takeover is harder to hate and harder to stop, because the first thing it hands you is something that genuinely helps.

Question 3
The village declines to fight. Is that clear-sightedness or cowardice?

Okonkwo takes off the messenger's head and what he hears is people asking why he did it. Umuofia does not go to war. The case for them is nearly unanswerable: Abame is next door, where one dead white man bought the machine-gunning of an entire village on market day. By now the other side has a court, a prison, salaries, guns, and local men working for them. Not fighting isn't spinelessness; it's arithmetic. People who survive have a next generation; a village that has been shot has nothing — and the price Achebe's own generation paid in the Biafran war is the most terrible re-run of that same sum.

But give the other side its full weight. What dispersed in the marketplace that day was not merely an armed revolt; it was this community's last capacity to act as a single body. Umuofia persists afterwards — the people are there, the village is there — but it can never again do anything together. The drive shaft the old man at Mbanta worried about broke on that afternoon. And more should be said: "resistance is futile" is always true, and it is always circulated by the party that benefits from your compliance. A correctly calculated decision does not thereby become an honourable one, and some things, once demonstrated to be surrenderable, cannot be got back.

Most people run this calculation several times a year — saying nothing at the meeting where something plainly unfair is decided, on the grounds that speaking won't help and will cost you. And that reasoning is usually correct. The book issues no verdict; it supplies a gauge. Okonkwo is the only person in it who acts on the old rules to the very end, and Achebe neither makes him a hero nor makes him ridiculous. He simply leaves him standing there with blood on his blade and nobody behind him.

Question 4
When you are defending a culture that is being destroyed, do you also write in its ugliest parts — the discarded twins, the outcast caste, the killing of Ikemefuna?

Achebe put them all in, on hard grounds: a defence that requires concealment isn't a defence, it's publicity. And those ugly parts are load-bearing — without the infants crying in the bush, Nwoye's conversion cannot be understood; the church holds its ground precisely because it catches the people this society threw out. Remove them and the book becomes an elegy, and elegies persuade nobody.

At full strength, the other side makes a point few people care to say aloud: where power is this unequal, writing both sides is not the same as being fair. A great many of this book's first readers were English, and "they kill infants, they keep outcasts" were the two handiest materials in a century of justifying conquest. Put them in the book and a reader can use them to confirm what he already thought, with an extra layer of confidence: even their own people admit it. Honesty has an exchange rate in badly tilted places: the same true sentence is magnanimity from the strong party and gets discounted into a confession from the weak one.

The everyday version is one of the commonest binds there is: whether to name your group's real internal problems in a room that plainly dislikes your group. Say it and you may be the only honest person present — or tomorrow's quotation. Achebe bet on the long run, on an honest book outliving the people who misread it. The bet looks good from here, and the cost is visible too: this novel is still being cited today in two directly opposed ways.

Recurring themes: what Achebe kept writing

1. Who is holding the pen. He often quoted an African proverb to the effect that until the lions have their own historians, the history of the hunt will always glorify the hunter. That is the master plan behind all of it: Things Fall Apart is the first record filed on the lions' behalf, and his 1975 lecture savaging Conrad's Heart of Darkness is the other half of the same operation — supply the missing account, and show how the existing one was manufactured.

2. The seam between a person and their community. His protagonists always die in the same place: the gap between what one man wants and what his people need. Here it is Okonkwo. In Arrow of God (1964) it is Ezeulu, a chief priest who confuses his office with himself and leaves his village unable to harvest. In No Longer at Ease (1960) it is Obi, Okonkwo's grandson, a foreign-educated civil servant who goes down over a bribe. Read together, the three books are one family run over by three different forces in three generations.

3. He kept the books on independence too. Achebe did not stop at what the colonisers did. A Man of the People (1966) is about a post-independence politician and ends in a military coup — shortly after which Nigeria had one; Anthills of the Savannah (1987) is about three friends and a military government; and The Trouble with Nigeria (1983) leaves no wriggle room at all: the problem is a failure of leadership. The man who kept the most exact accounts of colonialism was simultaneously his own country's least forgiving critic, and in him the two were never in tension.

4. Writing has a job description. In "The Novelist as Teacher" (1965) he says it plainly: in a society just out of colonial rule, part of a writer's work is educational — helping readers put a damaged self-estimate back upright. It explains why this novel is shaped the way it is: a book with an assignment, which discharges the assignment as though it had none.

Criticism and controversy

To put it all on the table, several of the objections to this book are real:

  • Where the women are. The gaze is essentially male: the beatings are narrated with striking flatness and almost no comment, and the only woman whose interior really opens is Ekwefi, for one chapter. Critics argue this is not merely period accuracy but a narration that has accepted the same yardstick. There is a defence: the most important deity is Ani, the earth goddess; when the priestess is seized every man in the village gives way; Mother is Supreme is a real institutional clause. Achebe later gave Beatrice a full interior life in Anthills of the Savannah, which many read as a deliberate correction.
  • Whether the characters are thin. Okonkwo is sometimes charged with being a thesis rather than a person, his motivation reduced almost to a formula. The rebuttal is that this narration is communal and proverbial by design and never sought psychological layering — which does not satisfy everyone.
  • The job of "representing Africa." On syllabuses worldwide it became the African novel, so a continent gets compressed into one Igbo village in the 1890s. A number of African writers and scholars have pointed out that this kind of singular elevation is itself a narrowing. That is not the book's fault, but it is the book's situation.
  • Whether writing in English is a problem. The most famous disagreement in African literary history: Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o argued in Decolonising the Mind (1986) that African literature written in European languages is not African literature, and he moved to writing in Gikuyu; Achebe held that English could be remade into a vehicle. Both cases are strong — one says the tool arrives carrying its own worldview, the other says abandoning the lingua franca means withdrawing from the fight over who narrates. It is not settled.
  • The Conrad lecture. In 1975 he called Conrad a thoroughgoing racist (the lecture was published the following year), and the rebuttals have run for decades, chiefly that it collapses Marlow into Conrad and underweights historical context.
  • Whether the pre-colonial world is drawn too intact. Researchers have noted that the novel's Umuofia looks more sealed and more static than the actual Igbo region of the nineteenth century, which had long been reshaped by regional trade networks and, earlier, by the Atlantic slave trade. What that really points out is this: it is a novel, not an ethnography.
  • Controversies about the author. Achebe took Biafra's side during the civil war (1967–70) and travelled on its behalf; his 2012 memoir There Was a Country, and specifically its account of the wartime blockade policy and of Obafemi Awolowo, set off a fierce argument in Nigeria. He twice declined Nigerian national honours, in 2004 and 2011, stating that he was protesting the condition of the country's governance. He won the Man Booker International Prize in 2007 and never received the Nobel.

Ten sentences to take away

1. The sentence this book exists to refute never appears in it: "the colonisers arrived at a blank space." It spends half its length itemising the courts, bride-prices, calendars and jokes that were in the blank.

2. Okonkwo's whole tragedy runs on one rule — never appear weak — and he executes it flawlessly his entire life, including the last time.

3. For the killing of Ikemefuna the novel offers exactly one motive: fear of what the men beside him would think. The heaviest stroke in the book has a motive as light as a draught.

4. The old man who came to warn him off killing a boy is the man at whose funeral he kills one: the heaviest causal link, placed inside the most meaningless accident available.

5. Nwoye is not persuaded, he is answered — the church wins by catching the people, and the questions, that this society threw out.

6. The first time the locusts came, they were dinner, and the whole village went out laughing to gather them.

7. What actually dismantles Umuofia is not the night the church burns; it is one true sentence: don't school your children and someone else's children will come to rule you.

8. Before he raised the machete he was a man of a clan; after, he was a man. The two seconds in which nobody follows him are deadlier than the blade.

9. The father died unburied in the Evil Forest; the son hangs from a tree that his own people may not touch. A man who spent everything running from his father arrived more precisely than anyone.

10. On the last page the book you have just read is classified by an official as superfluous detail — the quietest and most savage counterattack in the history of the novel.
