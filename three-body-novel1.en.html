The Three-Body Problem

Remembrance of Earth's Past · Book One · Liu Cixin · serialized 2006 / book 2008

中文 →

The snow on the Greater Khingan range has frozen and thawed and frozen again. On Radar Peak the great parabolic dish swings toward the sun. There is one person in the control room. When she presses the transmit key there is no ceremony and no witness — a row of indicator lamps comes up in sequence, and a beam amplified a hundred million times by a star leaves the Earth and spreads out toward a patch of sky she knows nothing about.
Eight years later the reply arrives. It says one thing: do not answer.
She answered.

In one sentence

An astrophysicist who watched her father beaten to death in the Cultural Revolution, and who stopped believing humanity could save itself, used a secret military antenna to broadcast Earth's position into space — and when the warning came back, do not answer, she answered on behalf of everyone, alone. Four decades later humanity discovers that its fundamental physics has been locked shut, and that a fleet is four hundred and fifty years out.

Where it sits

Liu Cixin (b. 1963) spent twenty years writing science fiction on the side while working as a computer engineer at a power plant in Shanxi. The Three-Body Problem was his fifth novel, serialized in Science Fiction World in 2006 and published as a book in 2008; The Dark Forest and Death's End complete the Remembrance of Earth's Past trilogy. Ken Liu's English translation won the 2015 Hugo Award for Best Novel — the first for an Asian writer.

Its place in the genre is easy to name: this is the oldest and most classical strand of hard SF, the Arthur C. Clarke line, where the engine of the book is one idea large enough to carry it. Liu has said many times that he is a Clarke disciple. But The Three-Body Problem has something extra: it welds a catastrophe on a cosmic scale onto one specific Chinese person's specific injury. Without the summer of 1967, the Red Coast antenna is only a premise. With it, the whole book acquires weight.

The story

Act one · 1967, a girl watches her father beaten to death

On a struggle-session platform at Tsinghua, the physics professor Ye Zhetai is surrounded by four teenage Red Guards. His crime is teaching relativity and the Big Bang — in the logic of the time, saying the universe had a beginning left room for God, and the Copenhagen interpretation was idealism. His wife, Shao Lin, denounces him first, using the physics vocabulary he taught her. He will not recant, will not say a single soft word. A belt buckle comes down, and he dies on the platform.

Standing in the crowd is his daughter, Ye Wenjie, a graduate student in astrophysics. She does not scream and she does not cry. This scene is the foundation of the entire book: every choice she makes later grows out of it.

She is sent to a logging corps in the Greater Khingan mountains. There a journalist lends her a book — Rachel Carson's Silent Spring, on how pesticide climbs the food chain killing as it goes. Reading it, she has a thought for the first time: what people do to nature and what people do to each other are the same thing.Seed ① Encouraged by the journalist she writes a letter, is betrayed, and becomes a counter-revolutionary.

Just as her case is about to be decided, the military base in those mountains — code name Red Coast — requisitions her. They need someone who understands astrophysics, and a person who is already politically dead and can never leave is exactly the right kind. She walks through the gate, signs, and never comes down the mountain again.

Act two · Red Coast, one person answers for everyone

Officially Red Coast is radar and weapons research. Actually it is one of the most secret projects of the Cold War: transmitting into space, and listening. The reasoning of the period was that if alien civilizations exist, whoever makes contact first gains the ideological and strategic edge. Twenty years go by, the dish turns and turns, nothing comes, and the base keeps a crew of disappointed people half alive.

Ye Wenjie discovers something there: the sun can work as an amplifier. She calculates that radio at certain frequencies, fired into the sun, can be reflected and amplified a hundred million times by an energy mirror inside it — effectively a cosmic megaphone bolted onto the Earth. She writes it up. It is rejected: her superiors think it is nonsense, and dangerous.

So on a day when she has the room to herself, she aims the dish at the sun and hits send. She isn't sure how far it will carry. She isn't even sure what she is hoping for. She only wants to know whether there is anyone else out there.

She also marries on that mountain — Yang Weining, the base's chief engineer. And she sends two men off a cliff: the political commissar Lei Zhicheng and her own husband, killed during maintenance work on the rock face, because they were about to touch her secret. This is the absolution Liu Cixin refuses to grant her: she is not a helpless woman carried along by her era; she has killed people herself.

Eight years later the listening terminal lights up. A Trisolaran who calls himself a monitor, risking a death sentence, sends a warning of three words repeated three times:

"Do not answer! Do not answer! Do not answer!"— warning from Listener 1379 of the Trisolaran world (quoted, Book One)

He explains: the moment your world replies, we can fix your position, and our civilization is looking for a new home. Silence is your only chance.

Ye Wenjie sits at the terminal for a long time. Before dawn she types her answer: come — I will help you take this world. Humanity can no longer solve its own problems; it needs an outside force.Payoff ① ↑

Her reason for pressing that key was already complete back in the logging camp. That book about pesticide had let her see the struggle-session platform and the poisoned birds as one and the same phenomenon — and if that is what the species is, the species is beyond saving. Liu Cixin rests the motive for an interstellar decision on a popular book about agricultural chemicals: the more concrete the motive, the harder it is to argue with.

This keystroke is the pivot of the book. Aliens did not find the Earth. A person on Earth invited them.

Act three · Forty years later, physics does not exist

Cut to the present. The world's best theoretical physicists are killing themselves, among them the young physicist Yang Dong — her note says, in substance, that everything points to one conclusion: physics has never existed.Seed ②

The nanomaterials scientist Wang Miao is summoned to a strange joint operations center where generals and foreign officers sit across from him, along with a foul-mouthed policeman smoking cheap cigarettes, Shi Qiang — Da Shi. They explain nothing. They just want him to make contact with an academic group called the Frontiers of Science.

Then things start happening to Wang himself. A countdown appears on the film he develops; soon it floats in his field of vision, present even with his eyes shut. He is told it will stop if he stops his nanomaterial project. He stops. It stops.Seed ③

Then comes the coldest scene in the book. Ye Wenjie — by now a revered retired professor, and Yang Dong's mother — tells him to go look at the cosmic microwave background.Seed ④ Before dawn, wearing the special glasses, Wang Miao watches the entire universe flicker, like something blinking at him. Thirteen point eight billion years of background radiation, turned into a signal lamp for an audience of one.

Act four · The world inside the game, and sixteen minutes on a canal

Wang Miao logs into a VR game called Three Body.Seed ⑤ Inside it he lives the same thing over and over: a civilization on a planet, tormented by three suns.

That world has only two seasons. In a Stable Era the climate holds and civilization can grow. In a Chaotic Era the three suns move without any pattern — it may mean a cold that lasts generations, a heat that bakes everything to ash, or years of nothing at all. To survive Chaotic Eras the people there evolved dehydration: they drain the water from their bodies, roll up into a hide, and are stacked in a warehouse until the weather is worth returning for.

Generation after generation in the game works the same problem: where will the three suns go next? King Wen tries yin and yang; Mozi builds a mechanical model; the most spectacular attempt is the First Emperor's. Von Neumann designs him a human computer — thirty million soldiers on a plain, each holding black and white flags as one logic gate, signals rippling through the formation, a calculating machine assembled out of people. It produces an answer. Then three suns rise together, the planet is scorched dry, and the civilization is reset for the umpteenth time.

Until one level gives up the secret: the three-body problem has no solution. Three masses under gravity move chaotically; no formula settles it once and for all. That world's destruction was not bad luck, it was mathematics. So the Trisolarans made the only rational decision — abandon home, find a new planet with one well-behaved sun. Four light years away, there is one.

The game is not a game. It is the recruiting filter of the Earth-Trisolaris Organization (ETO): finish it and you are invited in.Payoff ⑤ ↑ That odd game that looked like an author showing off his worldbuilding turns out to be a loyalty test — the reader thought they were being handed background material, and were being pushed through the plot the whole time. The organization's spiritual leader is Ye WenjiePayoff ④ ↑ — which changes the temperature of that scene in act three completely: when she sent Wang Miao to look at the microwave background, she was not helping a younger colleague. She already knew what was there. She was making him see the situation he was standing in.

Operations are run by Mike Evans, son of an American oil magnate, a man who spent his fortune on endangered species and arrived at a belief that all species are equal. He owns an ocean-going ship, Judgment Day, and every communication between Earth and the Trisolaran world passes through it.

The organization has already split. The Adventists expect no salvation; they want the Trisolarans to remove humanity as a species. The Redemptionists worship the Trisolaran world as a god and want to solve the three-body problem for their Lord. The Survivors simply want a berth in the new order for themselves and their children.

Humanity needs the communications archive on that ship, and boarding it means the data is destroyed. Hence Operation Guzheng: at the narrowest stretch of the Panama Canal, dozens of nanofilaments from Wang Miao's own lab — "flying blades," invisible, strong enough to cut anything — are strung across the water. Judgment Day sails in as usual. Sixteen minutes later the ship and everyone aboard have been sliced into forty-odd sheets, as though a zither's strings had been drawn through it. The drives are recovered intact.

Worth pausing here: those filaments are the very project Wang Miao was forced to abandon by the countdown in his eyes.Payoff ③ ↑ The technology the Trisolarans spent so much effort suppressing became the only weapon humanity had for its first counterstrike — the oppression and the response run through the same object. Of all the seeds in this book, that one is harvested with the least effort.

Someone in the briefing room did raise it: there are non-combatants aboard, there are children. The question was raised, and then it was set down.

Act five · Sophons, and insects

The archive gives up the truth. The Trisolaran fleet has already launched — a thousand ships at one percent of light speed, arriving in four hundred and fifty years. And they do not intend to let humanity develop in peace in the meantime. They have sent something ahead: the sophon.

The method: unfold a proton from eleven dimensions into two, at which point it can blanket an entire planet; etch a supercomputer onto that membrane like a circuit; fold it back to microscopic scale. Now something the size of a proton is intelligent, can fly at near light speed, and can talk to its home world instantly via quantum entanglement. Two are sent to Earth, with two jobs:

  • Lock fundamental science — get inside the particle accelerators and hand back a false result on every collision, so that human experimental data contradicts itself forever. Physics stops advancing, and every applied technology will eventually hit the same ceiling. This is why the physicists were killing themselves — Yang Dong had not broken down, she had genuinely watched the data contradict itself.Payoff ② ↑ Liu Cixin throws the conclusion at you first and withholds the mechanism for two hundred pages, and that suspended stretch is exactly when "physics does not exist" is at its most frightening.
  • Total surveillance — anything that happens anywhere on Earth is known in real time on Trisolaris. Humanity has no secrets left. Exactly one thing stays safe: what a person is thinking. (That seed grows into the Wallfacer Project in book two.)

At an ETO gathering, the sophons write across every retina and across the sky: "You are bugs!"Seed ⑥

The last scene of the novel has Da Shi driving Wang Miao and the physicist Ding Yi out to the wheat fields of the North China Plain. It is a locust year; the air and the ground are insects. Look, Da Shi tells them: humans have spent thousands of years on locusts with aircraft, pesticide and genetics, a technology gap wider than the one between Trisolaris and us —

"Bugs have never been truly conquered."— Shi Qiang, closing line (quoted, Book One)Payoff ⑥ ↑

Notice what that sentence is arguing with. "Bug" is a word the sophons threw down as an insult; Da Shi picks it up and redefines it in front of two intellectuals who are close to collapse. The final move of the book is not a plot reversal but the repossession of a metaphor.

The three of them drink among the insects and drive back to the city. On the other timeline, Ye Wenjie returns to the ruins of Red Coast and looks at the sunset: this, she says, is humanity's sunset.

Put the five acts together and you find almost no coincidences in this book: everything hangs on one fuse. An alien civilization did not discover the Earth; a person on Earth had her life broken in 1967 and, eight years later, pressed a key before dawn. Once the coordinates were out, the rest is aftershock — the fleet departs, the sophons arrive first, science is locked, physicists die, Wang Miao is pulled in, the ETO surfaces, and sixteen minutes pass on a canal. Every event on the contemporary timeline is an echo of that keystroke.

Three scenes in slow motion

One · The struggle session: welding a cosmic disaster to one body

The craft here is restraint of viewpoint. Liu Cixin never writes Ye Wenjie's inner state; he writes only what she sees — how old the four girls are, the sentence her mother says, the head her father won't lower, the metal of the buckle. Not one line says she came to hate humanity.

And yet several hundred pages later the reader accepts an insane decision, because this scene made "no longer believing in humanity" into something you can touch. The hard part of science fiction is not making the idea coherent; it is making the reader believe someone would really do it. Liu Cixin earns that credit in full, once, at the start.

One more layer: the Chinese original opens on the contemporary thread and reaches the Cultural Revolution later in flashback, while Ken Liu's translation moves that material to chapter one, which the translator has said is closer to the author's original intent. Same words, different position — and the book's center of gravity shifts from mystery to the making of a person.

Two · Three refusals: a cosmic scale pressed into one finger

This is the famous ten seconds, and its force comes from the direction of the information gap being reversed. Normally the alien is the threat and humanity the victim; here the alien risks execution to warn us, and the threat is the woman in the room.

The rhythm is crueler still. The warning uses the shortest possible sentence, three times — repetition here is not rhetoric but a unit of measurement for urgency: someone willing to die for a civilization not their own has no time to argue, only to shout the same four words. Then Liu Cixin gives us a long silence and lets us sit at the terminal with her. The fate of the species comes down to the travel of one key.

Three · The flickering universe: pushing horror from local to total

The standard recipe for horror is one wrong thing inside the ordinary. Liu Cixin scales that recipe to its theoretical limit: the wrong thing is not the room, not the city, but the entire observable universe.

And he sets the rule before he breaks it. Pages have already been spent making sure the reader knows what the cosmic microwave background is — the embers of the Big Bang, uniform, cold, unchanging on a scale of billions of years. The firmer the rule, the worse its violation. When something can treat thirteen billion years of background radiation as a light switch, humanity does not even qualify as threatened. After this scene, "you are bugs" is not an insult. It is a description.

Does the idea hold up?

The three-body problem: genuinely unsolvable

This is the one central premise in the book that is not invented. Newton settled the motion of two bodies under gravity; with three, no general analytic solution exists — Poincaré proved as much at the end of the nineteenth century, and in doing so opened the door to chaos theory. Chaos does not mean random; it means sensitive to initial conditions: a hair's difference at the start diverges without limit, so no amount of computing power buys more than a bounded stretch of future. The thirty-million-soldier computer that produces an answer and is then refuted is a literary rendering of that mathematical fact.

The solar amplifier: sound in fiction, invented in physics

Ye Wenjie's broadcast depends on an assumption: that a layer inside the sun reflects and amplifies radio at particular frequencies by a factor of a hundred million. This is the novel's invention, not astrophysics. The sun is certainly a huge radio noise source, but coherent frequency-selective amplification of an artificial signal has no basis in reality. Its function in the book is narrative — it gives an antenna in a Chinese mountain valley in the 1970s the power to shout across the galaxy.

The sophon: the book's great invention

Unfolding a proton into two dimensions, etching a computer on it, folding it back, then using quantum entanglement for instantaneous communication — every step is impossible (entanglement cannot transmit information; that is settled physics). As a narrative device it is close to perfect, because it solves three storytelling problems at once:

  • Why doesn't the stronger party simply arrive? Because the crossing takes four hundred and fifty years — which is where the story's time comes from.
  • Why can't humanity catch up technologically? Because fundamental science has been severed at the root. This is the book's sharpest cut: what it negates is not human capability but the belief that humans progress, the deepest article of faith of the modern age.
  • Why is there any hope at all? Because a sophon sees everything and cannot read a mind. The entire Wallfacer Project of book two grows in that crack.

One common confusion is worth clearing up: the dark forest law does not belong to this book. Book one contains only its seed — the monitor's line that answering exposes your position. Why an exposed position means death is not derived into a law of cosmic sociology until book two. What book one is actually about is plainer: the silence has a reason, and we have already shouted.

What it makes you think about

Question one
May one person press an irreversible button on behalf of everyone — if her despair is entirely justified?

Take her side first, at its strongest. Her evidence: a society can beat a physicist to death in broad daylight for telling the truth, can make a wife denounce a husband and students kill a teacher; a species can sing about love while poisoning an entire food chain. She did not fail to try believing in humanity — she tried, and her father died. Her inference is rational: a system that cannot correct itself can only be corrected from outside. Every conscientious objector, whistleblower and revolutionary in history has used the same reasoning.

Now the other side, at its strongest. Her act is the thing she is indicting: one person, on her own conviction, delivering a final verdict for everyone who was never asked. The four girls on that platform were also certain they were saving the world. She charges humanity with holding power it cannot handle, and then takes the largest single grant of power in human history. Worse, it is irreversible: she can still change her mind, but the coordinates are already in flight.

There is a distinction here that is easy to slide past: despair being well-founded and despair licensing action are two different things. The first is a judgment about the world; the second is a grant of authority to yourself. The novel neither defends her nor writes her as a lunatic — it writes her as extremely lucid, which is what makes her hard to sit with.

And the question is becoming concrete. Geoengineering, germline editing, general artificial intelligence all belong to the category of buttons that a few people can press, everyone must live with, and no one can unpress. The real contemporary charge of this book is not the aliens; it is that Red Coast exists, and more than one person is on shift.

Question two
If defeat in four hundred and fifty years is certain, how should today be lived?

Locking fundamental science is an announcement: from now on human technology has a ceiling, and the ceiling is too low to win from. What does a society do once it knows it will not get better?

One answer is: nothing, then — enjoy yourself, since the outcome is fixed and any long effort is waste. That is a purely rational inference. The other answer is Ding Yi's: knowing we cannot win, finish the work this generation can still do, because doing the work is what makes us us. The most quoted line of book two says exactly this — give time to civilization, not civilization to time (from The Dark Forest, not this volume). What matters is not how many years a civilization drags out, but whether those years contain any civilization at all.

Scaled down to a person, the question is not remote at all: is something worth doing well if it is certain to fail? Every one of us loses to time in the end, and it does not affect the case for doing today's work properly. Book one's answer is the insect — an answer with a beautiful counterargument, below.

Question three
"Bugs have never been conquered" — dignity, or consolation?

Da Shi's line is the most bracing exit in the book, and the hard part of it is true: a technology gap is not a verdict. Locusts have outlasted millennia of total human effort through reproduction rate, genetic diversity and sheer cheapness — survival is not always decided by computation and weapons. Applied to us: do not surrender in advance on the grounds that they are far stronger.

The hole in it is just as clear. Insects survive; insect civilization does not — because there isn't one. Species continuity and civilizational continuity are different things: humans could go on breeding in the rubble like cockroaches while losing science, art, memory and the ability to narrate themselves. Is that winning? And the locust's victory rests on not caring about individuals, whereas the entire value of human civilization rests on caring about individuals. A humanity willing to survive by locust strategy may already not be the humanity we wanted to preserve.

One more cut: the insect never knows it is being exterminated; its composure is ignorance. Ours would not be. Composure with full knowledge is courage; composure without it is luck. The reason the line moves us is that it is spoken by a man who knows everything — he is not stating a biological fact, he is handing a cigarette to two people about to fall apart. Take it as a stance and it is right; take it as an argument and it is thin.

Question four
Operation Guzheng: may you cut through the children on that ship for one archive?

This is the ethical pit most easily buried under the spectacle. Militarily the case is airtight: Judgment Day is the only place humanity can obtain Trisolaran intelligence, boarding destroys the data, and a few hundred lives buy four hundred and fifty years of preparation. Any utilitarian calculation returns the same answer.

Notice how Liu Cixin handles it: he lets someone raise the children, and then lets it be set down; the operation itself is written with clinical calm, almost entirely in engineering detail, without a drop of blood. The choice is deliberate — he is demonstrating how fast moral discussion gets downgraded to a line item once survival becomes the highest goal. Every argument in the rest of the trilogy, especially Cheng Xin's two failures of nerve in book three, is laid out from here.

Brought forward to now: any state of emergency declared in the name of civilizational survival automatically acquires a license to override procedure and individual rights. And the dangerous thing about emergency powers is not how harsh they are but how useful they are — once the justification is shown to work, nobody wants to put it down again.

The thing Liu Cixin keeps writing

Line his work up and the same obsession recurs: at a large enough scale, does the morality we are used to still hold?

In "The Village Teacher," the three laws of motion memorized by a few children in a mountain village are what an alien civilization happens to accept as proof that humanity qualifies as civilized. In "Devotion to Truth" (Chao Wen Dao), physicists queue up to die in exchange for hearing the universe's final answer. In "The Wandering Earth," taking the planet along means rewriting ethics, government and a substantial fraction of the population first. His protagonists never choose between good and evil; they choose between two prices.

The other half of him is lyrical. The girl trapped at the center of the Earth in "With Her Eyes," the man who spends a life chasing a glowing sphere in Ball Lightning — Liu Cixin writes vastness like iron and tenderness like a miniature, always landing on something small: a blade of grass, the smell of a flower, one sunset. What stays with most readers at the end of this book is not the fleet; it is Ye Wenjie standing in the ruins of Red Coast, watching the sun go down. This, she says, is humanity's sunset.

Criticism

Without flattery, the book has real weaknesses, and the criticism has a case:

  • Characters are functions, not people. Wang Miao has almost no personality; his job is to see on the reader's behalf. Ding Yi, Chang Weisi and most of the ETO can be summarized in a sentence each. Only Ye Wenjie and Da Shi truly stand up. Readers who want character-driven fiction bounce off here — an endemic problem of the idea-driven strand.
  • The treatment of women. The trilogy's pivotal female characters are either the destroyer (Ye Wenjie) or the one whose compassion dooms the species (Cheng Xin in book three), which critics read as a pattern binding feeling and empathy to catastrophe. The defense is that the male characters are equally vehicles for ideas and no more rounded. The argument is unresolved.
  • The values. Many readers take from the trilogy a Darwinian position: for survival, everything else gives way. In fairness, presenting a logic is not endorsing it — Liu Cixin writes where that logic leads once started, and the characters who follow it furthest are not written as happy. But a book being read as a manifesto by large numbers of readers is also a fact about the book.
  • The author himself. Remarks Liu Cixin made about Chinese domestic politics in several interviews have generated controversy in the West and coloured some readers' view of the trilogy. Stated as fact here, without judgment.
  • The translation. Ken Liu reordered the narrative (moving the Cultural Revolution material to the front) and added footnotes explaining historical context. Most assessments say this makes the book read better in English, but it is a rearranged edition — strictly speaking, Chinese and English readers are not discussing quite the same novel.

Ten sentences to take away

① This is not a story about aliens finding Earth; it is a story about a human being inviting them. All the horror starts inside.

② Ye Wenjie is not insane. She is extremely lucid, and that is what makes her frightening.

③ "Do not answer" is the moral axis of the book: the kindest transmission in the universe is a warning to shut up.

④ What the sophons really kill is not physicists but the modern faith that humans go on progressing.

⑤ The insolubility of the three-body problem is real mathematics; the solar amplifier and the sophon are beautiful inventions. Telling them apart is how you see both the book's ambition and its limits.

⑥ The human computer is the book's best metaphor: thirty million people assembled into a machine to compute a problem that cannot be computed.

⑦ Operation Guzheng teaches one thing: once survival is enthroned as the highest goal, ethical debate becomes a line item at astonishing speed.

⑧ "You are bugs" is a description, not an insult; "bugs have never been conquered" is courage, not an argument. Both are true and neither cancels the other.

⑨ The real dilemmas here are never good versus evil. They are two prices, both payable, and you choose which.

⑩ What stays is not the fleet. It is a sunset over the ruins of Red Coast, and a woman saying: this is humanity's sunset.
