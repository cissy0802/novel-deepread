Novels, Deep-Read · Bohumil Hrabal · No.84

Too Loud a Solitude

Příliš hlučná samota · Bohumil Hrabal · 1976 · a novella

中文 →

Prague, a cellar under a courtyard. There is a square hole in the ceiling, and the people upstairs tip waste paper through it: old newspapers, ledgers, the damp sheets butchers wrapped raw meat in — lift one in summer and the flies go up in a cloud.
An old man coated in paper dust stands in the drift, pulls a book out of the rot, knocks the dust off, finds a particular page and reads for a while. Then he lays it open, very gently, in the dead center of the press trough, the way you might lay flowers in a coffin.
He takes a long pull of beer and presses the green button.

Full spoilers ahead — what Haňťa saved over thirty-five years, whom he lost, what the new machine showed him, and where he was standing when he pressed the button for the last time. The book runs to about a hundred pages and its ending is on the last one: without knowing how he finishes, there is no way to argue about whether his life was a win or a loss.

In one line

A man who has spent thirty-five years in a basement compacting waste paper crushes books by the ton with a hydraulic press while quietly reading the best of them, carrying them home, and turning into a man "educated against his will" — until the city installs a fast, clean, enormous new press that has no use for his cellar, and he discovers that the whole meaning of his life was packed inside the little old machine. So he climbs into it.

Where it sits

Bohumil Hrabal (1914–1997) was a Czech writer with a law doctorate who worked as a railway dispatcher, a steelworker and a stagehand, and who really did spend several years in the 1950s baling paper at a Prague salvage depot. He finished this novella in 1976, when it could not be published in Czechoslovakia; it circulated first as samizdat (typescripts copied and passed hand to hand), then appeared abroad, and was only printed officially at home in 1989. He later said, more or less, that he had lived in order to write this book.

In the map of literature it belongs to the Prague line that runs from Hašek's Švejk and from Kafka: the absurdity is real, but the narrator is neither furious nor despairing — he drinks his beer and tells you something terrible in a voice that is almost cheerful.

The story

Act one · The cellar: a baler's love story

The narrator is Haňťa. The first thing he tells you is that he has been in waste paper for thirty-five years, and that this is his love story. That opening comes back again and again; each of the book's eight chapters begins with the same "thirty-five years," like an old man who has to clear his throat with the same phrase every night before he can start.

The job fits in a sentence. People in the courtyard dump paper through the hole; below, he feeds it into an old hydraulic press, squeezes it into square bales, ties them, ships them out to be pulped into new paper. The red button sends the ram back; the green button drives it down. Thirty-five years of that.

But the machine eats more than newsprint. Every book the city wants rid of comes down the same hole: library discards, private collections cleared out after a death, publishers' unsold stock, whole consignments dumped for reasons nobody explains. Haňťa pans for gold in the drift. He fishes out a book, opens it, reads one sentence and keeps it in his mouth like a sweet, letting it dissolve. Over thirty-five years he has sucked on Kant, Hegel, Nietzsche, Lao-tzu, Jesus. He has been educated, he says, without ever meaning to be — the sentences simply worked their way into him, as if he were a jug that kept being filled until it brimmed over.

Foreshadow ①So his baling has a ritual that belongs to no one else. In the heart of every bale he places a book, open, turned to the page he loves best, so that the page faces the descending steel. Around the outside he wraps a color reproduction of some great painting torn from an art book. To him each bale is a finished work — a little coffin with a book laid out inside. Nobody will ever know. The trucks take them away and somewhere else they turn to pulp. The ritual is entirely useless, and it is what keeps him alive.

Foreshadow ②The cellar holds mice as well as books. The paper drifts are their home; litter after litter is born and grows up inside them. When Haňťa presses, the mice go into the bales with the paper. He sees them, knows exactly where they are, and the paper still has to be pressed. He is not a cruel man. He is one part of a much larger machine, and that machine does not consult the mice.

Foreshadow ③Which is why the line he repeats most often comes from Lao-tzu: "The heavens are not humane" (Tao Te Ching, ch. 5). In Lao-tzu the sense is that heaven and earth play no favorites; they treat all things like the straw dogs burned after a sacrifice. In Haňťa's mouth it turns concrete and cold: the press is not humane, the paper is not humane, the world is not humane — and, he adds, roughly, a man with any sense in his head can't be humane either. He is talking about the mice, and about himself. Every time he presses the green button he is part of that inhumanity.

His boss stands at the top of the cellar steps and bellows. The paper is up to the ceiling again, the courtyard is jammed again, Haňťa is dawdling again, reading again, drunk again. To the boss he is a slow, filthy, useless old soak. The boss is not wrong — he just has no idea that the slowness is where Haňťa keeps his entire life.

Act two · Two tons of books over the bed

After work Haňťa carries the rescued books home, one bundle at a time. In his flat they climb from floor to ceiling: books in the kitchen, books in the toilet. The masterpiece is the bed. Above it he has built a platform of planks, a kind of canopy, and on it rest some two tons of books.

Foreshadow ④Every night he sleeps under those two tons. He knows perfectly well that one day the planks will give and the whole lot will come down and bury him. It doesn't frighten him; he half looks forward to it — crushed by the books he has loved all his life does not sound like the worst way to go. On first reading you take it as an old eccentric's joke, a drunk man's flourish.

There is also beer: a big jug, filled from morning to night, and a slow circuit of pubs after his shift. He always smells of paper and beer and that cellar mixture of mildew and blood. In the pubs people look at him as a down-at-heel old crank, while inside his head two thousand years of philosophy are running at once. That is the widest gap in the book: he knows more than anyone around him, and his place in the world is lower than anyone's.

Sometimes, under the cellar's dim bulb, he has two visitors. One is a young Jesus, surrounded by pretty girls, forever climbing, pushing forward; the other is an ancient Lao-tzu, eyes lowered, forever turning back toward the root. Haňťa sits on the paper and watches them and decides these two are the world's two ways of moving — an upward spiral and a circle that returns to where it began. He himself is more Lao-tzu: his whole life has been the guarding of a smaller and smaller place.

He has heard about what goes on beneath the city, too: in the Prague sewers two great clans of rats wage a war without end, generation after generation, neither able to wipe out the other. Above the sewers are the streets, above the streets the sky, and above the sky — in the words of Kant, whom he loves to quote — "the starry heavens above me." His world is stacked in layers: the starry sky and the moral law at the top, the rats' war at the bottom, and Haňťa in between with his hand on the green button.

Act three · Two women: the ribbons and the girl with no name

Haňťa was not always in the cellar. As a young man he loved a girl called Mančinka. Her story is the funniest and most mortifying passage in the book. Before a dance she visits an outdoor privy, and the ribbons in her hair trail down into the pit without her noticing; back on the floor she spins, the ribbons fly, and the whole room catches the smell. On a later ski trip something just as humiliating happens to her. Both times, at exactly the moment she most wants to be beautiful, she is shamed in public by the filthiest thing there is.

Haňťa tells it without malice, laughing, the way he would tell his own bad luck. In his world beauty and filth were never two separate things: books are dug out of rubbish, masterpieces are wrapped around bales of trash, a girl's loveliest evening comes with a stench. Mančinka eventually leaves him and goes on to another life.

The second woman he never even knew by name. It was during the war: a small, thin Gypsy girl who barely spoke followed him home one day and stayed. Every day she lit the stove, cooked him goulash, scavenged firewood from wherever she could find it, carried it home and waited for him to come back from work. Nothing much happened between them — one person waiting for another, a pot simmering on the stove. It was the closest Haňťa ever came to happiness, and he never asked her name.

Then one day she didn't come back. The Nazis took her, as they took so many Roma, and she died in a camp. Haňťa learned this only long afterward. When he talks about her there is no wailing; a few sentences and he moves on — yet you can feel that those few sentences weigh more than all the philosophy he has ever quoted. The heavens are not humane, and this time they landed on someone whose name he never had time to learn.

Act four · The uncle's garden and a retirement plan

Foreshadow ⑤Haňťa has an uncle, an old railwayman, retired. The uncle bought a decommissioned signal box and moved it into his garden, laid a loop of narrow-gauge track between the fruit trees, got hold of a little locomotive and gives the neighborhood kids rides on weekends. A man retires and simply brings his life's work home to keep playing with.

Haňťa envies him, and makes a plan of his own. In a few years he will retire. When he does, he will buy the press he has worked for thirty-five years, move it into his uncle's garden, and go as slowly as he likes, making every bale a true work of art — a fine book open at its heart, a great painting wrapped around it. It is the only picture of old age he has: not getting free of the machine, but finally being allowed to spend the rest of his life with it at his own pace.

Read in passing, it is a simple, rather sweet wish from an old worker. It is also the only moment in the book when Haňťa looks forward.

Act five · The giant press at Bubny

Then he hears that a huge new press has gone into operation at Bubny, in the north of the city. He takes the tram out to see it.

It is many times the size of his. In a day it does what would take him months. It is run by a Socialist Labor Brigade — young people in gloves and clean overalls who drink milk on their breaks. They work fast and neat: a book comes in, they rip off the covers in a couple of moves and toss the pages into the hopper without a glance at what is printed on them. Nobody pans for books. Nobody lays an open book at the heart of a bale. Nobody thinks anything here needs mourning.

Payoff ③ ↑Haňťa stands and watches tons of books become pulp within the hour. "The heavens are not humane," the line he muttered all through the opening chapters, here takes for the first time a shape he can see with his own eyes: not a cruel man, but a crew of healthy, hard-working, entirely well-meaning young people and a supremely efficient machine. Inhumanity, it turns out, needs no malice — only indifference. He has been part of this machine for thirty-five years himself, but at least he cared; the new age has saved itself even that step. The beauty of the move is that all along you took the Lao-tzu for a drunk's self-mockery, and only here do you realize it was the clearest judgment in the book.

He comes home knowing he is finished. Not because he will lose his job, but because he finally understands that his way of working is not a slower kind of efficiency in the new world; it is something that should not exist at all.

Act six · White paper, and the green button

Sure enough, the notice arrives. His cellar is to be shut down and the old press scrapped; two young members of the brigade will take over. Haňťa is transferred to the basement of a printing works, to bale clean white paper with nothing printed on it.

Anyone else would call that an easier job. For Haňťa it is the cruelest sentence possible: white paper has no words, no books, not a single sentence to keep in your mouth and let dissolve. His one secret fortune for thirty-five years was rescuing live sentences from dying paper; the paper he is given now is empty from the start. He goes. He cannot stand it.

Payoff ⑤ ↑The retirement plan collapses with it. He meant to buy this press, move it to his uncle's garden and live out his days beside it — the one forward-looking thought in act four turns out to have depended entirely on the machine still being his. Take the machine away and he no longer has a "later." Looking back, that sweet little wish was telling you something: Haňťa had never once imagined a self without this press.

He goes back to the old cellar. The new crew hasn't arrived yet, or has gone home for the day. He lines the trough of the press with books, the way you would make a bed. He thinks of Seneca — the Roman philosopher ordered by the emperor to die, who opened his veins in the bath. Haňťa feels he has the same right to choose his own ending.

Payoff ① ↑He climbs into the trough, lies down among the books, holds a book he loves to his chest and opens it. He has made himself into the last bale. In the first chapter he laid an open book at the heart of every bale, like flowers in a coffin, and it looked like a quirk. Only now do you see that thirty-five years of that ritual were rehearsal: the book is the heart, the bale is the coffin, and the last thing laid inside is the man who performed the ritual.

Payoff ② ↑He presses the button. For years he watched the mice go into the bales with the paper, knew they were there, and could not stop; now it is his turn. He is no longer the inhumane hand but the thing being pressed — for once the machine is fair.

Payoff ④ ↑And the two tons of books above his bed at home. In act two he lay under them and said that being crushed by them wouldn't be so bad, and you took it as a drunk's flourish. Here it comes true, only differently: the books don't fall on him; he lies down among them and lets the steel do what those two tons never did.

The novel ends there. The voice keeps talking up to the moment the ram comes down — no funeral, no witnesses, no word on whether he dies. From its first page the book has been one man's monologue, and where the monologue stops, the world stops.

Gathering the causes into one fuse: what set all of this off was not the giant press at Bubny, nor the notice transferring him to white paper. It was a day thirty-five years earlier when Haňťa, for the first time, paused in the rubbish, opened a book that was supposed to be crushed, and read a sentence. From that moment he was both executioner and mourner: he still had to press the green button every day, but he could never again not care. Everything after — the books in the bales, the books above the bed, the beer, the visions, the retirement plan, and finally that last lying-down — is aftershock from that one pause to read a line. The new machine merely called in, all at once, the contradiction he had been carrying for thirty-five years.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow-mo one · Making a bale

Replay it: paper drops through the hole; he digs out a book and finds the page; lays it open in the center of the trough; paper in, press, release, paper in, press; wrap it in a painting; tie it off. He describes every step closely, with the feel of it in his hands.

Why does it hit so hard? Because Hrabal writes a work of destruction as a liturgy. You are watching a man destroy books carefully, tenderly, with an eye for beauty — a paradox in a single gesture — and the author never explains it; he just makes you follow the hands. The more beautiful the motions seem, the worse you feel. The scene is set up in the first chapter and thickens every time it recurs; when it appears for the last time, the protagonist has swapped places with the book. The same motion repeated dozens of times, and only the last repetition lets you understand all the others.

Slow-mo two · The young people at Bubny drink milk

Replay it: the enormous machine, the clean overalls, gloved hands tearing off covers, a bottle of milk each on the break.

The power lies in the choice of point of view and detail. A writer out to denounce would make the brigade coarse and stupid. Hrabal makes them healthy, diligent, milk-drinking — milk against Haňťa's beer, clean against his dirt, fast against his slow. They have done nothing wrong; they simply don't read. Because there is nobody to hate, Haňťa's despair has nowhere to go. The whole scene is seen through an old man's eyes: he isn't looking at a machine, he is watching his own headstone being made.

Slow-mo three · Lying down in the trough

Replay it: books laid in, lying down, the book held, Seneca, the button.

Why is it so strong? Because it is told in the first person, in exactly the same tone as everything before it. No sudden rise in pitch, no farewell speech; Haňťa describes climbing into the press the way he has described baling, drinking, making the rounds of the pubs. The garrulous, cheerful, rambling voice of the whole book carries straight through to the last sentence — and that sameness is what makes the ending frightening. You wait for a scream and get a man calmly packing up his life. And formally the ending is untellable: nobody can narrate what happens after he is crushed, so the narration can only stop at that instant. The monologue and the life end on the same button.

The book's central device

This is not a premise-driven novel; its force lives in the structure of its voice and its images. Three pieces.

One: a voice that won't stop. The whole book is Haňťa's monologue — long sentences, image chained to image, from mice to Kant, from beer to Jesus, barely pausing for breath. Hrabal tied this way of talking to the Czech pub tradition; he coined a word for it, pábení — roughly, rambling yarn-spinning that turns the ordinary into the marvelous — and called its practitioners pábitelé. The effect is that the lowliest man in the world is given the richest inner life, while the reader stays locked inside his head, never seeing how anyone outside sees him.

Two: the high and the low pressed skin to skin. Kant's starry heavens beside the butcher's flies; visions of Jesus and Lao-tzu in a mouse-infested cellar; a girl's dance tied to a privy. This isn't shock for its own sake; it's a worldview. For Haňťa the sublime doesn't live elsewhere — it is what you dig out of the garbage. The fate of a book is the device made flesh: a book can be humanity's highest thought and a bundle of scrap at the same time, and the only difference is whether someone stops to read it.

Three: the press as a layered metaphor. On the surface it's a machine. One level down, it's a method of writing — the book itself is compressed, a hundred pages into which a man's thirty-five years and half of European thought have been squeezed. Further down, many readers take it as the censorship of its time: after 1968, Czechoslovakia pulled books from shelves and destroyed them, writers were barred from print, and Hrabal was among them. To be honest about it: the novel never says this outright. It is a reading, though a persuasive one — a man secretly saving books underground, and a text secretly passed around as typescript, mirror each other closely.

One smaller device deserves a mention: the repeated "thirty-five years" that opens each chapter. It makes every chapter feel like another telling on the same night, and it makes time stand still — for Haňťa, thirty-five years is not a line moving forward but a circle that keeps coming back to its start. It is the "Lao-tzu way of moving" he described himself, until, in the end, the circle closes in the press.

What it makes you think about

One · Was the giant press at Bubny wrong?

What takes Haňťa months, the new machine does in a day. The young crew don't read, but they also don't slack, don't drink, and don't let the paper pile up to the ceiling.

Make the machine's case first, as strongly as possible. A salvage depot was never meant to preserve books; it was meant to recycle paper. Haňťa's ritual — a book in every bale, a painting around it — contributes nothing to that purpose and only slows things down; his dawdling clogs the courtyard and enrages his boss, and the cost falls on others. The pressed paper becomes new paper for new books and new schoolbooks. When you get down to it, a man turned a public job into a private devotion and then called the arrival of efficiency barbarism — and there is a fair amount of vanity in that. Every generation of displaced craftsmen says the new machine has no soul, and it is the new machine that lets more people afford paper.

The other side: efficiency answers "how," and never "what does this mean." Haňťa's thirty-five slow years bought one thing — a person who recognized value in the rubbish. The books the city threw out were, at least, read by someone, remembered by someone, mourned by someone. The new machine deletes that act of recognition entirely. When a society hands everything to the fastest process, what it loses is not one old worker but the capacity to stop and look.

Bring it home: when a job is automated, outsourced or handed to a language model, what usually disappears is not just the position but the small, private standard some person was quietly keeping inside the process. Is there something in your work like putting a book in the bale — nobody asked for it, it isn't measured, but you know why it matters? If it were optimized away tomorrow, would you call that progress or loss?

Two · Saving books while destroying them: guardian or accomplice?

Haňťa saved a few tons of books. The books he pressed with his own hands over thirty-five years come to many, many times that. He pressed the green button every day and never once refused.

Make the case for "accomplice" as strongly as possible. Book-destruction runs smoothly precisely because of people like Haňťa: disapproving in their hearts, compliant with their hands, consoling their consciences with a little private rescue on the side. The two tons over his bed are, in a sense, an indulgence that lets him keep doing the job with a clear mind. If everyone who disapproved refused to press the button, the machine would stop. "Saving a little from the inside" often just helps the system run more smoothly.

The other side: refusal costs everything at once; staying costs a little at a time. If Haňťa refuses, he is fired and replaced by someone who doesn't read at all, and not a single book is saved. By staying, at least those tons of books survive, and those sentences survive in at least one person's head. In an era when an individual can't change the whole, "keeping what you can from within" may be the only real choice most ordinary people have — and a form of resistance harder to see than the public heroic gesture.

For Hrabal this question was not abstract: in the 1970s he made a public compromise in order to be published again (see Controversies below), and some colleagues and readers despised him for it. Haňťa's dilemma is the author's own, in a pair of work overalls. Bring it home: in a company you don't fully believe in, do you leave, or stay and do right within what you can control? And from the day you stay, how do you know you are still saving books and haven't gone over to only pressing them?

Three · Is it worth staking all of life's meaning on one job?

Haňťa has no family, no promotions, no other pursuits. All his meaning is in that cellar and that machine. The day the machine is taken from him, he can find no other reason to stay.

Take Haňťa's side first. Most people do their jobs with half a heart, as the price of living. Haňťa, in a job everyone looked down on, found a whole world — knowledge, beauty, ritual, dignity. That kind of total engagement is something many people never have in their lives. Asking him to "let go," to "find another way to live," is asking him to admit that those thirty-five years were only a job. For him it wasn't a job; it was how he existed. Losing it isn't unemployment — it's losing himself.

The other side is just as hard: staking your whole self on something others can take away at any moment is a dangerous fragility in its own right. The cellar wasn't Haňťa's, nor the machine, nor even the job — they belonged to a system that could reorganize, abolish or optimize them at will. If your meaning can only live on someone else's ground, then someone else holds the power of life and death over it. Haňťa could have carried his tons of books and his head full of sentences anywhere; he decided they only counted beside that machine.

To be clear: the novel does not present Haňťa's end as something to emulate; it shows a man driven into a corner. We discuss it not to admire the choice but to see how, step by step, he closed off every way out. Bring it home: retirement, layoffs, a vanishing industry, children leaving — when the thing that defines you is taken away, what is left of you? The question is best asked before it is taken.

Through-line: what Hrabal kept writing

Hrabal's recurring obsession is how small people survive the steamroller of history by holding on to some unfashionable sense of beauty. His protagonists are almost always powerless: a railway apprentice, a hotel waiter, a paper baler. They ramble, they brag, they're a bit ridiculous, and each guards one thing inside that history refuses to recognize and they themselves refuse to give up.

In Closely Watched Trains (1965), Miloš, a young apprentice at a small station under German occupation, is tormented by adolescent anxieties and ends up dying in an attack on a munitions train — the great and the trivial knotted together with equal absurdity; Jiří Menzel's film of it won the Oscar for best foreign-language film. In I Served the King of England, a short waiter scrambles upward, gets rich, goes to prison, is tossed about by the times, and finally ends up alone mending a road in the border mountains with a few animals for company, where at last he grows still.

Set side by side, Too Loud a Solitude is the most concentrated and the darkest link in that chain: the others at least still move through the world, while Haňťa has given up even that — his world shrinks to a cellar and finally to a trough. The title is the paradox: alone, he is never lonely, his head roaring with sentences; and yet nobody else on earth can hear that roar.

Controversies and criticism

The author's own compromise. The verifiable facts: after 1968 Hrabal's work was banned in Czechoslovakia. In early 1975 he gave an interview to the official cultural weekly Tvorba that was taken as a declaration of loyalty and self-criticism; afterwards he could publish again, though some work appeared only in censored form. Many dissident writers and readers were disappointed in him, and some protested by burning his books. Too Loud a Solitude was written after that — and could not be published: a novel about secretly saving books inside the system, by a writer who had made his peace with the system. Some read it as confession, some as self-defense; both remain readings.

"Name-dropping philosophers." Critics note that the Kant, Hegel, Lao-tzu and Jesus in Haňťa's mouth are mostly names and a quotation or two, never really developed — a kind of showing off. Defenders reply that this is the character's truth: a self-taught old worker who learned from the scrap heap would only ever have fragments, and his way of owning a thought is to keep one sentence in his mouth and turn it over. The fragmentation is not the author's flaw; it is the character's condition. Both views have force, and readers can judge for themselves how far the quotations are actually digested by the novel.

The women. Mančinka's two episodes are essentially bodily humiliations; the Gypsy girl has almost no lines and no name, and exists to cook and to wait. Some critics argue that the women are props in Haňťa's memory, one a punchline, one a silent comfort. You can also argue the reverse: the girl's namelessness is the book's most painful stroke — her erasure is the same event as the books pressed into pulp.

Romanticizing manual labor. Another criticism holds that the novel makes a dirty, exhausting, low-paid job far too poetic, and romanticizes the beer and the cellar. That's worth keeping in mind: the poetry is Haňťa's, not the job's; read the book as a hymn to working-class labor and you have misread it.

The author's death. In February 1997 Hrabal fell to his death from a fifth-floor window of a Prague hospital; the official account is that he slipped while feeding pigeons. Because his writing more than once dwells on falls from high windows and on suicide, speculation has never quite gone away. Only the fact itself is certain; nothing else should be treated as a conclusion.

Ten lines to carry away

② He was educated without ever meaning to be: the sentences found their own way in, like water into a jug.

③ An open book at the heart of every bale — a useless ritual, and a man's entire reason to go on.

④ The heavens are not humane, and neither is the press; his pain is knowing he is the one pressing the button.

⑤ He knows more than anyone and stands lower than anyone — that is what a too loud solitude sounds like.

⑥ He never asked the Gypsy girl her name, and those few lines outweigh every philosopher he quotes.

⑦ The young crew at Bubny did nothing wrong; they just didn't read — inhumanity needs no malice, only indifference.

⑧ The one time he looked ahead, it was to take the machine home with him — proof he never imagined himself without it.
