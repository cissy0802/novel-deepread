Novel Deep-Read · Franz Kafka · No.10

The Trial

Der Process · Franz Kafka · written 1914–1915 · published posthumously 1925

中文 →

Eight in the morning. A bank official waits in his rented room for the landlady to bring his breakfast. What comes through the door instead is a man he has never seen.
The man is wearing a well-cut black outfit. He says: you are under arrest. Others drift in behind him; two of them eat the breakfast. K. asks to see a warrant — there is no warrant. He asks what the charge is — nobody knows the charge; they were only sent to inform him. Having informed him, they tell him he may go to work.
He goes to work. For a full year after this morning he is never held for a single day, never once put in handcuffs, and never told what he did.

This page spoils the entire novel—where the court actually holds its offices, what the lawyer and the painter each propose, the parable of the doorkeeper the priest tells in the cathedral, and where Josef K. dies, at whose hands, and what his last three words are. There is no ending to protect here: the force of the book is that you know from page one it will not turn out well, and every page still lets you believe something can be done. One more thing to say up front—Kafka never finished this book and never meant to publish it; the order we read it in was arranged by his friend. That fact is itself one of the questions below.

In one sentence

A thirty-year-old bank official is arrested on the morning of his birthday, is not told the charge and is not locked up; over the following year he tries everything he can think of—arguing, pulling strings, hiring a lawyer, bribery, a painter who is said to know the judges, sleeping with every woman connected to the court—and every route leads back to the same stifling attic; on the night before his thirty-first birthday two men who look like third-rate actors take him to a quarry outside the city, lay him on a stone and pass a knife back and forth over him, waiting for him to do it himself. He doesn't, so they do it for him. His last words are "Like a dog!"

Coordinates

Franz Kafka (1883–1924), a German-speaking Jew from Prague, spent his working life as a lawyer at the Workers' Accident Insurance Institute for the Kingdom of Bohemia—drafting injury assessments and compensation disputes by day, deciding what a finger was worth, writing fiction at night. He published only short pieces in his lifetime and died of laryngeal tuberculosis at forty.

The Trial (German title Der Process) was written mainly between August 1914 and January 1915, then set aside and never finished. He left it as a set of loose notebooks and gave his papers to his friend Max Brod. His written instruction to Brod was: burn all of it. Brod did not burn it. He edited it and published it in 1925—the title is Brod's, the chapter order is Brod's, and the unfinished fragments Brod cut out.

Its place in literary history needs no decoration: almost every twentieth-century way of writing about an individual facing a power he cannot see traces back to this unfinished book. The adjective "Kafkaesque" comes mostly from here.

The story

Act one · The morning of his thirtieth birthday: a very polite arrest

Josef K. (no surname is ever given) is a Prokurist at a large bank—roughly a senior manager with signing authority who can act for the firm on his own. Thirty, unmarried, he lodges in Frau Grubach's boarding house and visits a barmaid named Elsa once a week. His life is tidy, ascending, unimpeachable.

On his birthday the maid does not bring breakfast. He rings; a stranger walks in, and a second one is sitting in the next room. They call themselves warders, Franz and Willem; they eat his breakfast and suggest he hand over his underclothes for safekeeping, since if the case ends well he'll get them back and if it doesn't he won't need them. Then an inspector arrives and settles on the bedside table in Fräulein Bürstner's room with the air of a neighbour dropping by. Three junior colleagues from the bank are brought in as witnesses—men K. nods to in the corridor every day.

K. asks to see the warrant. There isn't one. He asks the charge. The inspector says they were only instructed to tell him he is arrested and know nothing else; the court is drawn to guilt, it does not come looking without reason. Then he adds the line that sounds most like good news: you are under arrest, but that needn't stop you living as usual and going to the office. seed ②

The wrongest thing about the whole scene is not violence but courtesy. Nobody shouts at him, nobody touches him; the only coercion is that they sit in his room and will not leave. And across the street, at a window, two old people have pressed their faces to the glass to watch, thoroughly enjoying it, with a young man standing beside them, hands in his pockets. seed ①

He goes to work. That evening he waits in the hallway for his neighbour Fräulein Bürstner—the men used her room that morning, and he wants to apologize and also to have someone to talk to. In her room he re-enacts the entire arrest, getting more excited as he goes, finally imitating the inspector by shouting his own name at the door, "Josef K.!", loudly enough to wake the building. Then he lunges, takes hold of her and kisses her face and throat like an animal drinking at a water's edge. She says very little. seed ③

Act two · The court in the attic, and the whip in the storeroom

A few days later a telephone call reaches the bank: on Sunday he is to appear for a preliminary inquiry. The address is in a slum on the far side of town; no hour is stated and no room number. K. sets off on Sunday at a time of his own choosing, walks into a tenement with wet washing on the stairs and children crawling on every landing, and knocks on doors with an invented pretext—is there a joiner here named Lanz? On the fifth floor a woman doing laundry hears the fake name and simply pushes the door open: come in.

Behind it is a large room packed with people, low, airless and thick with smoke, with more of them craning from galleries along both sides. The examining magistrate leafs through a small worn notebook and looks up: you are a house painter? No, says K., I am the chief clerk of a large bank. The room roars with laughter. The magistrate says: you are one hour and five minutes late.

K. decides not to defend himself but to expose the court in front of everyone. He does it well—this procedure is being run against thousands of people, he says, and he is not speaking for himself but for all of them. Some of the audience applaud. Halfway through he notices something: every man in the room has the same badge on his collar—the two factions he thought he was addressing are one body of officials. And just as he reaches his stride, something happens at the back: a student has a washerwoman pinned against a wall, she screams, and the room's attention swings away instantly. His speech becomes background noise. He swears and walks out; behind him the magistrate remarks, unhurried, that he has just thrown away the advantage an interrogation can offer a prisoner.

The following Sunday he goes back on his own—nobody summoned him, he simply went. The room is empty except for the washerwoman; she offers to help him, and mentions that she belongs to the court herself. While they are talking the student comes in, hoists her onto his shoulder and carries her off to the examining magistrate. Her husband, the court usher, smiles bitterly and says this has been going on for years, then takes K. upstairs to see the court offices.

The offices are in the attic, the ceiling too low to stand straight under, with benches along the corridor full of waiting defendants. As K. walks past, they get to their feet one by one and bow, stooping—they have taken him for an official, because only an official walks that upright. seed ④ He tries to speak to one of them; the man shakes too badly to finish a sentence. Then the attic air turns on K. himself: he goes dizzy, his sight darkens, he can barely stand, and two court people have to hold him up and walk him out. The accused breathe perfectly well in here; it is the healthy man who cannot—the first time the book inverts the question of who belongs. Down on the street, one lungful of cold air and he is himself again.

What follows is the coldest scene in the book. Leaving work one evening, K. hears groaning behind the door of a junk room at his own bank. He pulls it open: inside stands a man in dark leather with a bare chest holding a cane, and on the floor are Franz and Willem, being stripped. The reason is that K. denounced them publicly at the inquiry—he had complained that the warders ate his breakfast and wanted his underclothes. An offhand complaint has turned into a whip on two men's backs. K. offers money, saying they are not really guilty; the whipper says calmly that he can take the money but the work still has to be done, and if K. would like to be beaten in their place that can be arranged. Franz cries out, K. is afraid colleagues will hear, slams the door and tells the two porters who come running that there is nothing in there, only a dog barking outside.

The next evening he walks to the same door and, without knowing why, opens it—the same room, the same man in leather, the same two men, the same positions, everything untouched, as though yesterday had never happened. This time he doesn't even step in. He closes the door and instructs the porters to have the storeroom cleared out completely.

Act three · Three experts: the lawyer, the painter, the merchant

Word reaches the countryside and K.'s uncle rushes into town a hundred times more agitated than K. himself; what he keeps saying is not "you're innocent" but "you'll drag the family down." He hauls K. off to an old schoolfriend, the lawyer Huld (in German the name means grace, or clemency). Huld is permanently bedridden in a room too dark to see faces in, and a visitor is sitting by the bed who is introduced as the chief clerk of the court—which is exactly this lawyer's talent: he doesn't go to the court, he entertains the court at his bedside. The girl who nurses him is Leni; she calls K. out of the conversation and shows him a small defect in her right hand—a web of skin joining the middle and ring fingers, which she displays as a charming curiosity. Then she sits on his lap and says the crucial thing: she finds every accused man attractive. When K. returns to the sickroom his uncle is beside himself: you have ruined your own case.

It takes K. months to work out how the lawyer actually operates. Huld is composing a "plea," and has been for a long time, and may scrap it and start again at any point, because—he explains—nobody knows precisely what a first plea should contain, and the court does not guarantee it will be read. What he is really doing is maintaining relationships: drinking with junior officials, chatting, absorbing their moods. He tells K. the same thing repeatedly: what matters is not being right but being connected; the accused should do as little as possible, since everything he does makes it worse. He also describes, in passing, the standing of defence lawyers in this system—their room is in an attic, and there is a hole in the floor that a careless man can put a leg through into the storey below.

Meanwhile the bank's deputy manager is taking over K.'s clients a little at a time. K. now spends whole mornings staring out of the window, drafting his own defence—he intends to set out all thirty years of his life item by item and account for every small thing that might be held against him. He knows this document would take months, that he might not have time to write it before retirement. payoff ② ↑ That first morning's apparently generous "you can go on living as usual" now shows its teeth: there is no prison because none is needed—once a man begins writing a defence of his own life, the life has already been confiscated. The elegance of it is that Kafka never lets you see the moment of confiscation. He gives his protagonist the right to go to the office for a full year, and lets the reader discover that he stopped going long ago.

An old client of the bank takes pity and passes on a remedy: go and see the painter Titorelli. He lives in a garret in a slum on the other side of town, and a gang of small girls dogs the staircase; Titorelli mentions offhandedly that they belong to the court too.

The studio is a few paces across, the single window nailed shut, hot as a steam bath. Titorelli's trade is painting judges—a post inherited from his father with no official appointment whatever, yet he is the man every judge comes to. He shows K. an unfinished canvas: a judge high on a throne with a figure rising behind him. Who is that, asks K. Justice and the goddess of Victory combined, says Titorelli—except that Justice ought to hold scales and wear a blindfold, and in this picture her heels are lifting and the blindfold has slipped. K. looks a while and says: like that she can't stand still; that's more like the goddess of the Hunt. The painter doesn't argue.

Then comes the passage that reads most like a rulebook. Can a man actually get out of this, K. asks. Titorelli counts three routes, and there are only three: definite acquittal, apparent acquittal, and indefinite postponement. He is candid: he has never in his life seen the first, it exists only in stories old men tell; the second he can help with—he collects signatures from a circle of judges as guarantors and the man walks free on the spot, but the file goes on travelling up through the system, and on the day it reaches the wrong judge you are re-arrested in the street and it all begins again, with no limit on how many times; the third is keeping the case pinned at its lowest stage forever, which requires constant small manoeuvres and no lapse of attention, and in exchange you are never convicted. K. asks: doesn't the third leave a man no life at all? The painter says: but you won't be sentenced.

K. does not choose. He is close to fainting in the heat and only wants out. Titorelli takes the chance to pull a few pictures from under the bed and sell them: heathland scenes, every one identical—the same two spindly trees, the same sunset, the same grass. K. buys three and tucks them under his arm. seed ⑤ As he leaves, the painter points him to the studio's back door—which opens directly into the offices of the court.

The last expert is not an expert but a specimen. The lawyer keeps a client in his house, the grain merchant Block: five years into his case, his business long since mortgaged away, secretly employing five petty lawyers besides Huld, and now living in the maid's room so as to be available at any hour. K. watches him summoned: the lawyer, from his bed, dresses him down in front of K. and then makes him kneel; Leni says, laughing, this is the lawyer's dog. Block kneels and kisses the edge of the bed. payoff ④ ↑ The stooping defendants who bowed to K. in the attic corridor were, at the time, just a blur of pitiable people—this scene fills in everything between: an ordinary businessman took five years to become this, and every step was reasonable, and every step was for the good of the case. Kafka's cruelty is that he never writes the moment of degradation; he writes the daily maintenance of relationships. K. dismisses the lawyer on the spot. What he is dismissing is less Huld than the sight of himself in five years.

Act four · The cathedral: the door and the doorkeeper

The bank sends K. to show an Italian client around the city's cathedral—an assignment arranged by the deputy manager, and K. sits up the night before brushing up his Italian. He arrives on time; the Italian does not. It is a wet afternoon and the cathedral is nearly dark, lit by a few scattered lamps. As he is about to leave, a voice calls his name across the empty nave—a priest, high up in the pulpit.

The priest says he is the prison chaplain, and that it was he who had K. brought here. Your case is going badly, he says; you are thought to be guilty. K. protests: but I'm innocent, and how can anyone be guilty as such—we are all human beings. The priest says: that is how guilty men talk.

Then, to stop K. misunderstanding the court, the priest tells him a story written in the introductory texts to the Law. This is Before the Law—the only part of this book Kafka published in his lifetime (issued separately in 1915, later collected in A Country Doctor):

Before the Law stands a doorkeeper. A man from the country comes and asks to be let in. The doorkeeper says not now. Later, then? Possibly, but not now. The door stands open, and the man bends to look inside; the doorkeeper laughs and says, if you want it that badly, try—but note that I am the lowest doorkeeper, and inside there is hall after hall, each with a keeper more terrible than the last; the third of them I cannot bear to look at myself. The man had not expected this complication, and decides to wait. He waits for years, and gives away everything he brought as bribes; the doorkeeper takes it all, saying each time that he accepts it only so the man won't feel he left anything untried. He grows old, his eyes fail, and at the end he is petitioning even the fleas in the doorkeeper's fur collar. Dying, he remembers a question he has never asked, and beckons the doorkeeper down: everyone strives for the Law, so how is it that in all these years no one but me has come? The doorkeeper roars into his failing ear:

"No one else could gain admission here, because this entrance was meant only for you. I am now going to shut it."Before the Law · closing lines (all quotations here translated by the author of this page)

K. says at once: then the doorkeeper deceived him. The priest disagrees, and the two of them fall to arguing exegesis on the spot—how the commentators read it, whether the doorkeeper was himself deceived, whether he had any authority at all, whether he too was waiting, whether the man was free the whole time, whether the ending is regret or mockery. Every reading is overturned by the next. K. grows exhausted and says that on this account lying becomes the order of the world. The priest's reply is the hardest sentence in the book to digest:

"You don't have to consider everything true, you only have to consider it necessary."Chapter nine · the prison chaplain

Standing in the dark, K. says the most human thing he says anywhere: that he is tired, that he has worn himself out thinking about this from every direction. He asks the priest: you're not angry with me? The priest comes down the steps and takes his hand, and then delivers the deadliest line of all:

"The court wants nothing from you. It receives you when you come and it lets you go when you leave."Chapter nine · the prison chaplain

Act five · The eve of his thirty-first birthday: the quarry

On the evening before his thirty-first birthday K. has dressed in black and is trying the fingers of a new pair of gloves—he has known for a while that someone would come. At nine, two gentlemen enter in frock coats and top hats, pale and fleshy, saying nothing. K. studies them a moment and asks: which theatre do you play at? No answer. He thinks: they have sent a pair of second-rate goods to deal with me—they intend to dispose of me by the cheapest available method.

The three of them go into the street linked so tightly they move as one body. At a junction a woman's back appears on some steps ahead, very like Fräulein Bürstner. payoff ③ ↑ K. does not establish whether it is her, but he suddenly becomes the active one, turning to follow her direction, with the two gentlemen falling in behind him. Everything he did that year was a response; this last stretch of road is the only one he leads. The re-enactment in her room in act one looked at the time like nothing but a loss of composure—a frightened man performing his own disaster in a stranger's bedroom; here that night is redeemed as the last autonomous act of his life, and what it points at is a person whose face he never even sees.

They leave the city and reach a quarry. The two men take off his coat and shirt, lay him down with his head against a loose block, arrange him carefully, and still cannot get it to look right. Then one of them opens his coat and draws out a long, double-edged butcher's knife, and the two of them pass it back and forth above his body. K. sees it perfectly clearly: they are waiting for him to take the knife and put it into himself. He does not take it. He thinks that leaving the whole job to the authorities and assuming none of it himself might be a kind of failure—he cannot rise to it completely.

At that moment a window flies open in the top storey of a house beside the quarry, and a figure leans far out with both arms stretched. payoff ① ↑ In his last seconds K. asks a string of questions that get no answer: who was that? A friend? A good man? Someone who felt for him? Someone who wanted to help? Only one person? Everyone? Was there still help to be had?—On the first morning, the two old people across the street had leaned at their window in exactly this way. The first action in this book and the last action are the same one: someone is watching. Kafka never writes a sentence about society's indifference; he simply sets the same window twice, a year apart—the onlookers never become rescuers, and the dying man, in his final moment, mistakes an onlooker for a friend.

One gentleman grips his throat, the other drives the knife deep into his heart and turns it twice. As his eyes close he can still see the two faces cheek to cheek above him, watching the conclusion. He says:

"Like a dog!"Chapter ten · the final lines

The narrator adds half a sentence: as if the shame were to outlive him. payoff ⑤ ↑ Now look again at the pictures from under Titorelli's bed. The three "different" paintings K. paid for and carried home under his arm are the machine's self-portrait: laid out, three routes; laid flat, one picture. And on the day he bought them he left through the studio's back door, which opened into the offices of the court.

Put the five acts together—where is the fuse? Not the arrest; nothing was compelled that day, they gave notice and left, without even a charge. The fuse is the first Sunday: the summons named no hour and no door, nobody escorted him, he could perfectly well have stayed home. He went, because he wanted to set the record straight. Every door for the next year is one he opens himself: he goes back to the attic a second time, he pulls open the storeroom door a second time, he seeks out the lawyer and the painter, he stays in the cathedral to hear the story out. The priest's last line is both the verdict and the only set of instructions: it receives you when you come and lets you go when you leave. The problem is whether a man who has been told he is guilty can leave. That is the real mechanism of this novel: it builds the cell as a room you may exit at any time, and then demonstrates that nobody exits.

Three scenes in slow motion

1 · The first sentence: handing "innocent" to an unreliable witness

The opening is among the most dissected in literature: someone must have been telling lies about Josef K., for without having done anything wrong he was arrested one morning. The whole technique sits in that "for"—it is an inference, running from "did nothing wrong" to "someone must have lied." And who makes the inference? Not the narrator. The German is free indirect style: the grammar is third person, the logic is K.'s own. One sentence tricks the reader into a position: you think you have been handed a guarantee from the author, and what you actually hold is the defendant's opinion of himself.

He keeps this up to the end: not one sentence in the novel steps outside K.'s consciousness. So everything about the court—its size, its ranks, its authority, whether it exists at all—is reported by people who claim to belong to it, with no third-party confirmation anywhere. A reader who reaches the last page wanting to say "this was a miscarriage of justice" finds that the only evidence is a line spoken by the accused.

2 · The storeroom: running the same shot twice

The real blow in the flogging scene is not the cane, it's the next day. K. opens the door again and nothing has moved: same posture, same leather, the same sentence beginning. In a realist novel the next day must bring change—that is the proof that time exists; Kafka cancels the change, which is to announce that in this world time leads nowhere. Punishment is not an event that occurred but a condition that persists, stored behind that door: open it and it's there, close it and it's still there.

The location is calculated too. This storeroom is not in the courthouse but in the bank—the place where K. has most power, one door away; there is no "outside the system" to escape toward. And his two reactions are the whole man: the first time he shuts the door and says there's nothing in there, only a dog barking outside; the second time he orders the room cleared out. His way of solving a problem is to empty the room the problem is in. A year later he is emptied out to a quarry beyond the city, with the same efficiency.

3 · The cathedral: handing over a key, then changing the lock

Before the Law runs to a little over a page, and the moment it ends the reader feels a clean satisfaction: at last somebody has said what this book means—the Law stands open and you cannot enter, because there is always someone telling you not now. Then Kafka does something vicious: he has the priest and K. take the story apart on the spot.

Did the doorkeeper deceive the man? But he told the truth throughout. Was he kind? He pocketed every bribe. Could he have admitted him? He doesn't know either; he is only the lowest. The door was meant for one man and is closed by that man's death—so who was trapped, the one outside or the one inside? Every interpretation is knocked over by the next sentence. The function of this exegesis is not to explain the parable but to demonstrate the condition of interpreting: the meaning is certainly there, and you certainly cannot reach it. The argument is itself a door of the Law.

Then there is the priest's line, that you need not hold everything true, only necessary. It is disturbing because it is not sophistry: this is exactly the attitude we take toward most of the rules that govern our lives—never mind whether they are true, do they run, can they be avoided. K. says that this makes lying the order of the world. He is right. He also cannot refute it, and he is right about that too.

The device at the heart of the book: a court with no centre

If the engine of a novel like The Three-Body Problem is a physical premise, the engine here is an institutional premise, and Kafka works it out more rigorously than most science-fiction writers do. Assemble everything the book says about the court and the rules come out roughly as follows.

One: it has no visible centre. The offices are in the attics of slum tenements, the portraits of judges are invented by a painter (who admits the judge in question is a short man who sits on a kitchen chair with a horse blanket under him), and the law books, opened, turn out to be pornographic pictures and a dirty novel. Every court person K. meets—warder, usher, washerwoman, painter, lawyer, priest—is of the lowest rank and says there are higher ones, and the higher ones never appear. The effect: you cannot appeal, because there is no superior to appeal to; and you cannot revolt, because there is no opponent to revolt against.

Two: its existence is sustained entirely by everyone treating it as existing. No police escort, no cells; the only physical act in the book is hiring two men who look like third-rate actors. Yet everyone cooperates automatically: the uncle fears disgrace, colleagues fear contamination, the landlady's manner changes, a merchant moves into his lawyer's house. This machine needs no violence, because it is already installed in how people judge one another.

Three: the three exits form a closed system. Take Titorelli's options as a logic problem. Definite acquittal is an unobservable term—it exists in legend, and can be neither proved nor disproved. Apparent acquittal is a bounded reprieve with unbounded recurrence. Postponement extends the state of "not yet convicted" indefinitely, at the price of lifelong attendance. An unfalsifiable exit plus two looping paths: this is not a model of a judicial system, it is a model of guilt itself. You cannot prove your innocence, because innocence has no deliverable evidence; you can only spend the rest of your life denying, or accept a pardon that may be revoked at any moment. This deserves to be marked honestly: as a description of law it is not accurate and was never trying to be—what it describes accurately is something else, the interior operation of an accused person.

Four: the absence of a charge is the source of all the power. If the book named his offence, readers would immediately start weighing whether it warranted the sentence, and the whole thing would become a case. Withhold the charge and the trial moves out of the courtroom and into the reader's head, where the only evidence is whatever you volunteer.

What it forces you to think about

Question one
Is Josef K. wronged—does this book ever say he is innocent?

The standard reading is that an innocent man is ground up by a machine he cannot see; after the Second World War this became the default answer. But start with the opposite side, which is stronger than it looks.

The novel never states in any reliable way that K. is innocent—the line about his having done nothing wrong is his own thought. And here is the man the book spends a year showing us: on the night of his arrest he barges into a neighbour's room, grabs a woman just home from work and kisses her throat; an offhand denunciation gets two subordinates flogged, and when his money is refused he shuts the door and says it's a dog barking outside; he is condescending to his landlady, his uncle, and Block alike; he tries to sleep with every woman who might be useful. Worst of all: in an entire year he never once genuinely examines himself. He asks over and over what right they have, and never once asks whether he has wronged anyone in his thirty years—the defence he drafts is meant to clear his life item by item, not to see it. If this book is a trial, what stands accused is a way of living: respectable, ascending, spotless, owing nobody anything, because it never came close to anybody.

Now the innocence side at full strength, without appealing to sympathy. A system that requires you to discover your own charge is the standard architecture of a forced confession. "He refuses to reflect, therefore something is wrong with him"—that sentence form is the most dangerous thing in the book: it treats not-confessing as evidence and writes inner candour into the procedure. Every self-criticism and confession-for-leniency in history has rested on the same premise: there must be something, and if you can't think of it you aren't being sincere enough. The rule of law demands that the prosecution prove its case, that charges be specified and time-limited, not because everyone is innocent but precisely because "anything can be found on anyone" is factually true, which is why the entrance to inquiry has to be nailed shut by rules. K.'s arrogance is real and so is his coldness; but a man having a poor character and whether he should have his throat cut in a quarry with no charge named are two things you cannot draw a line between.

Back to your own life: the everyday form of this is an email with the wrong tone, a performance review nobody will explain, an accusation that has been shared a few thousand times. What hurts is not being accused; it's the moment you start going through your own files. Anyone going through the files will find something—everyone would. And the moment you start looking, you have conceded that the other side had grounds to open a case.

Question two
Which of Titorelli's three routes would you take—and note that you have already taken one.

Start with indefinite postponement, argued better than the painter argues it. Its merit is verifiable: nobody who takes this route gets convicted. All you have to do is keep the case alive and motionless—show up regularly, be pleasant, always be preparing materials, never push for a decision. The cost is a slice of your time and morale every week, and in exchange you keep your life and never have to face a final answer. Most hard problems in the world are not solved, they are managed until death: chronic illness is controlled by numbers rather than cured, the knot in a marriage is handled by avoiding the subject rather than opening it, a company's bad debt is provisioned annually rather than written off. A large part of the actual skill of adulthood is turning insoluble things into things you can carry.

Now the other side, which needs only one witness: Block. He took precisely this route. Five years, and he did every step right—engaged extra lawyers, learned to read officials' moods, moved as close to the case as he could get, made himself available at all hours. He was not convicted. He merely stopped being a person. He kneels by a bed and is called the lawyer's dog in front of a visitor. That is the bill for postponement: the case does not kill you, it slowly becomes you, and when you look back your time, your money, your posture and your way of speaking have all grown into the shape of this one thing. It doesn't want your life, it wants your lifetime—deducted in instalments small enough that you never make a decision about any of them.

Apparent acquittal corresponds to settling, to admitting some harmless fault, to signing whatever makes it go away: effective immediately, at the price of a file that keeps circulating, so that you live from then on with "this could restart" as background noise. As for definite acquittal—being fully heard, fully believed, the thing ceasing to exist—the book's verdict is cold: nobody has seen a case. Most accusations are not withdrawn, only forgotten, and forgetting is not yours to schedule.

Note K.'s own choice: he takes none of the three; he wants a fourth—to bring the court down. This is the most heroic thing about him and the reason he dies fastest. The everyday version: faced with trouble you can neither win nor escape, do you manage it or insist on a reckoning? Manage it and you survive, but you may become Block; insist and you keep hold of who you are, with no guarantee of how long. The book gives no answer; it only itemizes both bills.

Question three
"You only have to consider it necessary"—is accepting that clear-sightedness or surrender?

Take the priest's side first. What he offers is a fairly mature survival principle: do not mistake "this is unreasonable" for "this does not exist." Academic promotion boards, visa rules, a platform's recommendation algorithm, the way the wind changed in your industry—you can demonstrate that much of it is neither fair nor intelligent, and the demonstration buys you nothing. The first lesson of adulthood is to process "true" and "necessary" separately: truth is what you may think about, necessity is what you have to live in. K. spends a year demanding an account, which is not courage but effort spent in the one direction guaranteed to yield nothing. The priest is not urging him to confess; he is telling him: stop asking whether it is right and look at how it runs. There is even some tenderness in it—he is teaching a man how to suffer less.

Now K.'s side at full strength. His objection—that this makes lying the order of the world—sounds like quibbling but identifies a real problem: "necessary" is not a natural property, it is manufactured. A wall is necessary because enough people get up every morning and go on building it. Once everyone decides to handle the world by necessity alone and stops asking whether it is true, the world loses any mechanism for correcting anything—not because it has become solid, but because nobody is checking. And there is a hidden cost in the priest's advice: a person who acts by necessity alone for long enough loses the ability to tell true from false, the way an unused muscle wastes.

Where Kafka himself stands, the book gives clues and no conclusion: he gives the priest the most powerful line in the novel, and then has the priest admit he belongs to the court. The one who says the true thing is on the machine's side—that is the deepest layer of unease here.

Back to yourself: the last time you told yourself "that's just how it is," had you seen the hardness of the world clearly, or spared yourself a fight you didn't want? In the moment the two are nearly indistinguishable; the difference shows up years later, in whether you have become someone who no longer distinguishes them.

Motifs: the thing Kafka keeps writing

Lay his work side by side and the same structure recurs: a person receives a verdict, the grounds for it always lie one level beyond what he can know, and he can neither appeal nor decline it.

In The Judgment (1912, written in a single night) a young man is talking with his father when the father suddenly pronounces: I sentence you to death by drowning. The son runs out of the house, grips the railing of a bridge and lets go; no explanation is offered, the verdict simply is one. The Metamorphosis (1915) writes the same event onto a body: Gregor wakes as an insect, for no reason, and his family's patience begins its countdown by the day. In the Penal Colony (written October 1914, during a pause in the writing of this novel) turns it into a machine—an apparatus that engraves the sentence into the condemned man's back with needles, and by the rules of that place the condemned is not told his sentence in advance: "he will read it on his body." Those three plus The Trial are one idea at four different hardnesses.

The other obsession is doors. Here K. is pulled into a system he cannot get out of; The Castle (1922, also unfinished) reverses the direction: another K. is desperate to get in, the village is right there, the castle's telephone never connects, the appointment always lacks one more formality. One man is admitted against his will, one can never be admitted at all, and both books stop halfway—not a coincidence, but two directions of the same machine.

The sources are no mystery. His day job at the accident insurance institute was a lifetime of paperwork about what an injured man is owed and why he isn't owed it; his relationship with his father became a letter of some thirty pages (written to him in 1919, entrusted to his mother, never delivered, published in 1952); and in July 1914 his fiancée Felice Bauer, accompanied by her sister and a friend, confronted him in a Berlin hotel and the engagement was broken off—in his diary he called that meeting "the tribunal in the hotel." Weeks later he began writing The Trial. This need not be treated as a cipher for the book, but it does establish one thing: the situation of sitting among strangers while others announce what kind of person you are, he wrote from inside.

Criticism and controversy

  • How much of the book we read is Brod's? Kafka left written instructions that all unpublished manuscripts be burned; Brod did not burn them. The manuscript was a set of loose notebooks with no authorial ruling on the order of chapters; the title, sequence and cuts are Brod's, and he framed the whole thing as religious allegory. The German critical edition of around 1990 (led by Malcolm Pasley) rearranged it and separated out the unfinished fragments, and reads noticeably differently. When we say "Kafka's Trial" we mean a text completed by two people, one of whom explicitly asked for it not to exist. The defence of Brod is not weak either: Kafka handed the papers to precisely the man who had already told him to his face that he would never do it—and someone who truly wants to burn things has ample opportunity. There is no neutral gallery here: you and I, reading this, are beneficiaries of that disobedience. As a postscript: after Brod's death the manuscripts were fought over in the Israeli courts for decades, and were awarded to the National Library only in 2016—a legal process about Kafka that ran for half a century.
  • The women are almost entirely functions. Fräulein Bürstner, the washerwoman and Leni are all connected to the court and all sexually available to K. within minutes; Leni says outright that accused men look good to her. One criticism is that this makes women into corridors to power, a thoroughly male-authored arrangement. The defence: the book never leaves K.'s consciousness, so what we see is not these women but these women as seen by a man who assumes he can have them—and the text supplies counter-evidence (Leni is managing him, and he is helpless when the washerwoman is carried off). The defence holds, without reducing the problem to zero.
  • Be careful with "it predicted totalitarianism." Kafka died in 1924, before the Gestapo and before the Moscow trials. What he wrote about was Austro-Hungarian bureaucracy, insurance paperwork and his own family; readers recognize prophecy because later history grew into that shape. Calling a novel prophetic is in fact a compliment to its readers. One related fact belongs here plainly, without commentary: all three of Kafka's sisters were murdered in the Nazi genocide; the youngest, Ottla, volunteered to accompany a transport of children from Theresienstadt to Auschwitz and was killed in 1943.
  • "Kafkaesque" has been worn out. Today the word is nearly a synonym for an annoying service counter. But there is not one inefficient bureaucrat in this novel—they are highly efficient, and nobody can state the purpose. Demoting the word to a complaint about queueing erases the harder half of the book: the court is also inside the person.
  • What the title loses in English. The German Process carries both "trial" and "proceedings, process"—and there is, precisely, no trial in this book, only a proceeding that never ends and never concludes. The Trial renders the action and drops the endlessness. There is no correct answer in that trade; a reader only needs to know which half went missing.
  • Whether the middle is a flaw cannot be settled. Readers who dislike it call the middle stretch repetitive and the characters flat (attic, heat, a woman, another man who says he is of the lowest rank); whether that repetition is the theme executing itself or the residue of an abandoned draft has no referee—because the author gave up on it.

Ten sentences to take away

1 · There is no trial in this book, only a proceeding that never closes—which is exactly what the German title says.

2 · "You're under arrest, but you can go on as usual" is not leniency, it is the method of execution: no prison, because none is needed.

3 · The "for" in the first sentence is not the narrator's, it's the defendant's—the only evidence of innocence in the book comes from the accused.

4 · The court sits in an attic where the accused breathe easily and the healthy man faints.

5 · Titorelli sells him three identical heaths: three routes laid out, one picture laid flat.

6 · Block kneels by the bed and is called the lawyer's dog; a year later K. dies saying "like a dog"—the one causal line in the novel you can state plainly.

7 · The door was meant for one man and closed by his death: what he was waiting for was not permission but the question he never asked.

8 · "You only have to consider it necessary"—that the man saying it belongs to the court is worse than the sentence itself.

9 · The fuse is not the arrest but the first Sunday: no hour on the summons, no escort, and he could have stayed home.

10 · The first action of the book and the last are the same one: someone is watching from a window. A year apart, and the onlookers never became rescuers.
