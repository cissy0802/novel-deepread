Novels, Deep-Read · Laurence Sterne · No.103

Tristram Shandy

The Life and Opinions of Tristram Shandy, Gentleman · Laurence Sterne · 1759–1767 · nine volumes

中文 →

On the first Sunday night of every month, Mr. Shandy does two things: he winds the big clock on the back-stairs landing, and then he goes to bed with his wife.
He has done it for years, without fail, and the two chores have grown far too close together.
So one night in March, at precisely the wrong moment, Mrs. Shandy asks: my dear, you haven't forgotten to wind up the clock?
A child is conceived inside that question. He will later say that every misfortune of his life began with it.

This piece contains complete spoilers: how Tristram was conceived, how his nose was flattened, how he got the wrong name, and what a falling window did to him at five; where exactly Uncle Toby was wounded and why the Widow Wadman needs to know; and why a nine-volume "autobiography" ends with its hero barely on stage. Without the last line there is no way to talk about what this book is doing to the very act of telling a story.

In one sentence

A gentleman named Tristram Shandy sets out to tell his life and opinions from the very beginning, so he starts on the night he was conceived, and then wanders off to his father's crackpot theories, his uncle's old wound, a man-midwife's forceps, a parson's death. Nine volumes later he has barely passed the age of five; what the book really narrates is a man trying to get his life down on paper while life interrupts him every second.

Coordinates

Laurence Sterne (1713–1768) was born in Clonmel, Ireland, the son of a poor infantry officer, and grew up trailing the regiment from posting to posting. He then spent more than twenty years as an Anglican parson in rural Yorkshire, and until he was forty-six he was almost nobody. At the end of 1759 he had the first two volumes of Tristram Shandy printed in York; they reached London in January 1760 and made him the most talked-about man in town almost overnight. The volumes kept coming until the ninth, in 1767. He died of consumption the following year.

It is routinely placed at the source of the experimental novel. The English novel was only a few decades old; Defoe, Richardson and Fielding had just established that a story runs from beginning to end, and Sterne was already taking that rule apart. The Russian Formalist critic Viktor Shklovsky made a deliberately provocative claim in 1921, roughly that Tristram Shandy is the most typical novel in world literature. What he meant: the thing every other novel hides, the telling itself, is here laid out on the table.

The story

Act One · A night interrupted by a question

The narrator, Tristram Shandy, is a middle-aged man coughing blood in his study as he writes. A person's temper and luck, he says, are largely fixed at the moment of conception, so that is where his story has to start.

The trouble that night lay with his father. Walter Shandy is a retired Turkey merchant living at Shandy Hall in Yorkshire, and the great passion of his life is not money or women but theory: he has an elaborate, self-consistent, absurd doctrine for everything. He is also a man of method who assigns the household's small duties to fixed days, which is why the clock and the conjugal duty both fall on the first Sunday night of the month.

Tristram calls in a philosopher for support. In his Essay Concerning Human Understanding, John Locke describes the "association of ideas": two unrelated thoughts that keep occurring together get welded in the mind, so that one drags the other in after it, and much human unreason grows from such welds. That is his mother exactly. The clock makes her think of the other thing; the other thing makes her think of the clock. Setup ①So at that moment out comes the question about the clock. Walter explodes: since the creation of the world, has any woman ever interrupted a man with such a silly question?

By Walter's physiology, the "animal spirits" have to be gathered in that instant to escort a tiny pre-formed person, the homunculus, on its journey; the question scattered them, and the homunculus arrived already battered and ill-starred. Tristram reconstructs the date by counting back: he was born on the fifth of November, 1718.

Yet before he can be born, the book swerves. He must explain why his mother did not give birth in London: her marriage settlement allowed her to lie in there, but a false alarm the previous year had dragged Walter to London for nothing, and he invoked another clause to make her have the next child in the country. So he must explain the midwife, an old village woman whose licence was paid for by the parish parson, Yorick. So he must explain Yorick.

Setup ②Yorick is a country parson with a clear heart and an unguarded tongue who rides a horse thin enough to count its ribs, and who is said to descend from the Danish jester whose skull Hamlet holds. He makes jokes without checking who is listening; the people he offends keep score, and in the end he dies worn down by their slanders. His gravestone carries only Hamlet's words, "Alas, poor YORICK!" (vol. 1, ch. 12). The next page is printed solid black. A novel that has barely begun stops to hold a funeral for a minor character.

Act Two · Upstairs, a birth; downstairs, a conversation

Back to that November night in 1718: Mrs. Shandy is in labour upstairs with the old midwife beside her, and Walter and his brother Toby sit smoking in the parlour below. There is a commotion overhead. What can they be doing, brother? asks Walter. Toby takes the pipe from his mouth, knocks its head two or three times on his left thumbnail, and begins: Setup ③"I think—"

And there the sentence stops. To understand what Uncle Toby thinks, Tristram explains, you first need to know Uncle Toby.

Toby Shandy is a retired captain who fought in William III's wars on the Continent. At the siege of Namur in 1695, a cannonball knocked a stone off a parapet and the stone struck him in the groin. Setup ④He spent four years recovering in bed in London, and every visitor wanted to know: where did you receive your wound? Each telling was torture, not because the wound hurt but because he could never explain it. Was it the scarp or the counterscarp, the hornwork or the ravelin? He tied himself in knots. For Toby, "where" always meant a point on a map.

His servant, Corporal Trim, has an idea. Why explain at all? Go down to the country and build the works of Namur on a bowling green, and then you can simply point. Toby is reborn. He takes a small house near Shandy Hall with a bowling green, and he and Trim dig trenches, ramparts and drawbridges with their own spades. England is at war with France again and Marlborough is winning everywhere; the two men read the gazettes daily, and whatever town falls in Flanders is besieged again on the lawn, on schedule. Tristram's word for this kind of obsession, the kind you can't get off once you've mounted it, is a "hobby-horse": his father rides theory, his uncle rides fortification, and neither understands a word the other says.

Toby is the gentlest person in the book. Once, a big fly buzzed around his nose for an entire dinner; at last he caught it, rose, went to the window and opened his hand to let it go. The world, he said, was surely wide enough to hold both of them. Tristram says he saw this at ten years old, and that the lesson sank deeper into him than all the books he read afterwards.

Payoff ③ ↑Only after all of this does Tristram walk us back into the parlour, where Uncle Toby still has the pipe in his hand and is still in the middle of his sentence. "I think," he says, "it would not be amiss, brother, if we rung the bell." Between the opening of the sentence and its close lies half a man's life. The reader has waited chapters for the second half, and it turns out to be the most ordinary remark imaginable; but during the wait, Toby has turned from a name into someone you know. The method of the whole book is demonstrated in a single sentence.

Now there is a knock at the door. Walter, who doesn't trust the old midwife, has insisted on a man-midwife equipped with instruments waiting downstairs; Mrs. Shandy has insisted on the old woman alone. The servant Obadiah, galloping on a coach-horse to fetch Dr. Slop, meets him head-on at a corner as the squat little doctor trots up on his pony, and Slop goes headfirst into the mud. He arrives at Shandy Hall plastered with it, having left his bag of instruments at home.

Obadiah rides back for the bag, and to stop the instruments rattling he ties its strings in a series of hard knots. Slop can't undo them, saws at them with a penknife, cuts his thumb, and starts cursing Obadiah. Walter hands him the excommunication formula of the medieval bishop Ernulphus to read aloud, a curse that works its way down from the hair of the head to the nails of the toes over several pages. Afterwards Toby says his heart would not let him curse the devil himself so bitterly.

At last Slop produces his pride, a new pair of forceps, and offers to demonstrate them to the brothers. Setup ⑤He uses Toby's hands as the baby's head, squeezes, and tears the skin off Toby's knuckles. Nobody takes it to heart. They have just heard Trim read aloud a sermon that fell out of one of Toby's military books, an argument that conscience is an unreliable judge, written by Yorick; it is in fact a real sermon Sterne himself preached at York Minster, dropped into the novel whole. Everyone is still discussing it, and no one stops to wonder whose head a pair of forceps that can skin a grown man's knuckles is about to close on.

Act Three · The nose and the name

There is trouble upstairs, and Slop goes up. When the news comes down, the child is born and alive, but the forceps have crushed his nose flat against his face. Payoff ⑤ ↑The skin off Toby's knuckles was a rehearsal. At the time it was slapstick, a clumsy doctor misusing his props; now the same instrument and the same clumsy force have landed on a newborn's face. Sterne hides the warning inside the joke, and only after you have finished laughing do you see that it was telling you what would happen. The maid Susannah pulls a strip of whalebone from her stays, wraps it in cotton, and props up a makeshift bridge for the baby's nose.

Why this is a catastrophe requires another of Walter's theories: noses. The Shandys have suffered for noses before. Tristram's great-grandfather had almost none, and his great-grandmother extracted an extra jointure in the marriage settlement on that account. Walter has read every treatise on noses ancient and modern and believes the length of a nose decides the fortunes of a family. The fourth volume even opens with a mock-Latin tale about a stranger with a stupendous nose who rides a mule into Strasbourg and sets the entire city arguing over whether the nose is real.

When Walter hears, he goes upstairs without a word, throws himself face down across his bed with one hand trailing to the floor, and lies there for an hour and a half. Toby sits beside him in silence. Afterwards the brothers go downstairs together, and that one flight of stairs takes Tristram several chapters; he even asks himself whether it isn't shameful to spend two chapters on two men coming down a staircase.

Walter rallies, because he holds one last card: the name. He has a theory that names exert a magical influence on character. The best of all names is Trismegistus, after Hermes Trismegistus, the legendary "thrice-great" Egyptian sage, and the very worst is Tristram, a name he holds in unconquerable contempt; no one called Tristram, he is sure, could ever amount to anything. The nose is gone; the name must be saved. The boy will be Trismegistus.

But the infant suddenly goes into a fit and turns black in the face and seems about to die, so he has to be baptised at once. In the middle of the night Walter can't find his breeches, so he sends Susannah running ahead to tell the curate the name. Susannah can't hold onto anything that long. "Tris-something," she says. Tris? says the curate. Then it must be Tristram, which happens to be his own name. So the one name that should never have befallen this child is stamped on him by a flustered maid and a curate glad to leave his mark. By the time Walter arrives, the water has been poured.

He won't accept it and consults a gathering of divines and lawyers about changing the name. The learned gentlemen argue it to a conclusion that leaves Walter speechless: by the reasoning of church law, a mother is not, strictly speaking, related to her own child. The name stays.

Act Four · A hat, a window, a tear

Then worse news: Tristram's elder brother Bobby, away at school, has died. Walter does not weep. He launches into the consolations of the ancients and grows more moved by his own eloquence the longer he goes on.

At the same moment, down in the kitchen, the servants have heard too. Corporal Trim stands among the cook and the scullion and the coachman and says: are we not here now, and in a moment... and he lets his hat drop straight to the floor... gone! Susannah bursts into tears. Upstairs, classical rhetoric; downstairs, a hat. Sterne doesn't say which is better. He just sets the two scenes side by side.

Walter's remedy for his remaining son is to write him a complete system of education with his own hand, the Tristra-paedia. Three years and more go by and it isn't half done. The child grows faster than the book: by the time it reaches how a thing should be taught, the boy is past the age for learning it. Meanwhile, Tristram is left more or less to himself.

Hence the window. When Tristram is five, he needs to pee in the night, the chamber pot is missing from under the bed, and Susannah lifts him onto the window seat to go out of the window. Setup ⑥Down comes the sash. It strikes the one place on a five-year-old boy where nothing should ever strike: an accidental circumcision. Why did the sash fall? Because the lead weights that should have held it up were gone. Trim had melted every piece of lead in the house to cast cannon for Toby's lawn war. Payoff ⑥ ↑The household had been donating to that lawn for a while, in fact. Back in the third volume, Trim had already turned a pair of old jack-boots that belonged to the great-grandfather into two mortars, much to Walter's dismay. At the time it was just another joke about the brothers' quirks. Here, the pretend war on the grass finally wounds someone in the house, and it is the youngest member of the family. A hobby-horse is not just a harmless pastime; it has to be fed. Toby is consumed by guilt; Walter goes to his books to research circumcision among the ancient peoples.

Toby's finest moment comes in an old story set into the middle of the book. In about 1706 he hears that a poor lieutenant named Le Fever is lying ill at the village inn with his young son. Toby sends Trim to inquire and that night makes up his mind: tomorrow he will bring the man home and get him the best physician. He swears, in the name of God, that the man shall not die.

Here Sterne wrote the book's most famous passage. The accusing spirit flies up to heaven's chancery with Toby's oath and blushes as he hands it in; the recording angel, writing it down, drops a tear on the word and blots it out forever. Le Fever dies anyway. Toby buries him and takes in his son.

Act Five · Riding away from Death

In the seventh volume the book abruptly turns around. Tristram stops telling family history and starts telling his own present. Death has come knocking, so he decides to run: across the Channel and south through France. The volume is an anti-travel book: other travellers describe the sights, Tristram describes broken-down carriages and post-house bills.

Strangest of all is the time. Passing through Auxerre, he says he is there twice at once: now, alone and on the run, and many years ago, travelling through the same town with his father and uncle. Two times lie on one page. Finally, in a field in Languedoc, a country girl named Nannette pulls him into the grape-pickers' dance, and as he dances he leaves Death behind, at least for this volume.

Somewhere along these roads he does the arithmetic. He has been writing for a whole year and has got no further than the first day of his life, so every day he lives adds another day to write, and the faster he writes, the further behind he falls. A man who wants to write his whole life can never finish, because the writing is also part of the life he has to write.

Act Six · The widow's eye and a bull

In the last two volumes he finally keeps the promise made at the very beginning: the story of Uncle Toby's amours. The clock has to be turned back to 1713. The Peace of Utrecht has been signed, Europe stops fighting, and so Toby's lawn war has to stop as well; he grieves as if for a death in the family. Right then, the Widow Wadman next door opens her campaign.

She has been watching Toby for a long time, and her siege has an engineer's precision. Once, in Toby's sentry-box, she says something has got into her eye and asks him to look. Toby leans in and searches her eye conscientiously, minutely, for a long time, and finds nothing. He looks into a woman's eye that long and sees only an eye. But it is enough: he falls in love. Trim meanwhile courts the widow's maid; he keeps trying to tell Toby the story of the King of Bohemia and his seven castles, and is interrupted every single time, so it never gets told.

Toby puts on his old regimentals and makes a formal proposal. Mrs. Wadman does not refuse. She only asks, with tender concern, the question she has been turning over for a long time: and where, Captain, did you receive your wound?

Payoff ④ ↑Toby answers with gratitude: Madam, you shall see the very place. And he sends Trim home for a map of Namur. The little word from Act Two is taken off the map by a woman and put back on a body. The reader has laughed along for hundreds of pages, taking it for an old soldier's fixation; only now does it come clear that the word always had two meanings, that Toby only ever heard one of them, and that the other was the question he never let himself touch. In the end it is Trim who spells it out: what the widow wants to know is not Namur but whether that part of him still works. Gentle Toby is wounded, and the courtship comes quietly to nothing.

In the very last chapter, as the family sits talking, Obadiah bursts in with a grievance. His cow was put to Walter's bull and didn't take, and he suspects the bull is no good. Walter defends his bull's honour at length. Mrs. Shandy finally can't help asking: Lord, what is all this story about?

Payoff ② ↑Payoff ① ↑"A COCK and a BULL," says Yorick (vol. 9, last chapter), English for a tall tale, a load of nonsense, and he adds that it is one of the best of its kind he ever heard. The end. Yorick died in the first volume, with a black page after his gravestone; but because the book's time is scrambled, in 1713 he is still alive, and so a man long buried gets the last word of the whole book. And the book that opened on Mrs. Shandy's interruption closes on another; it starts with an act of generation gone wrong and ends with a bull that can't do its job. Both ends are a botched act of breeding, and both are a woman asking an untimely question. The first time she asked about a clock; the last time, she asks exactly what every reader of the book has been wanting to ask.

The fuse

The fuse isn't the forceps, the name or the sash. It is the clock on the first page: a man scheduled the one thing in life that should never be scheduled, and from then on everything in this family is a tug-of-war between plans and accidents. The birth, the nose, the name, the education Walter prepares for his son are each punctured by the smallest possible mishap. And Tristram the narrator inherits his father's disease: he too means to tell his life according to plan, and every sentence gets hijacked by the next thought. The shape of the book is the shape of this family's fate.

Three slow-motion scenes

Slow motion one · The fly

The scene is only a few lines long: a dinner table, a fly, a hand closing slowly and opening again, a window. Its force comes from where it sits. It isn't part of the plot but of a digression introducing Toby's character, like a photograph slipped between the pages. Sterne doesn't tell us Toby is kind; he has Toby talk to a fly. And another layer: the narrator almost never appears in the book as someone present, he is always telling us about other people; only here does he say that he, at ten, saw it and remembered. The book's rarest "I" is spent on its gentlest moment.

Slow motion two · The hat on the floor

The chapters on Bobby's death are cross-cut: upstairs Walter is reciting Socrates, downstairs Trim is talking about death in the kitchen. Sterne places two eulogies together, gives both exactly the same information (a young man has died), and draws opposite responses. Walter uses a thousand words to push the grief away; Trim uses one gesture to bring it close. The hat lands with such force because it completes a sentence without words: the first half is speech, "are we not here now," and the second half he doesn't say; he lets the hat say it. The narrator even adds that everything depended on the way the hat fell; dropped any other way, it would have been nothing. It's a lesson in craft: emotion isn't in the adjectives, it's in the right object at the right moment.

Slow motion three · The recording angel's tear

In the Le Fever chapter, Toby swears an oath in his urgency, which to a devout reader is blasphemy. Sterne doesn't defend him; he pulls the camera straight up to heaven. One angel delivers the words, another records them, and then a tear falls and washes the word away. The passage is just one action in slow motion, delivering, blushing, writing, weeping, and yet it hands down a theological verdict: good intentions can erase the letter of a sin. It's a textbook move of eighteenth-century sentimental fiction, writing designed to draw sympathy and tears; but set in a book full of dirty double meanings, you can't tell whether Sterne is wringing your heart or winking at you while he does it. That not-being-able-to-tell is his voice.

The core device: a narrative machine that runs backwards

In a sentence: this book makes storytelling itself the biggest character in the story. Taken apart, the machine has four parts.

Part one: the digression is the point. Tristram says outright that digressions are the sunshine of reading, its very soul; his book is digressive and progressive at the same time. That's not just a quip. The digression wedged into Toby's "I think—" is exactly what gives the banal second half its weight. A digression is a way of building charge. This part holds up.

Part two: writing time can't catch up with lived time. A year of writing to reach the first day of life means the book can never be finished. That's a genuine logical structure (see Crossovers below), but the novel uses it to convey a mood: a man coughing blood finds that the harder he tries to pin his life to the page, the faster it slips off the edge. In the ninth volume he suddenly turns to someone called Jenny and says, more or less, that time wastes too fast, and every letter he traces tells him how quickly life follows his pen. At that moment the device becomes a confession.

Part three: the reader is pulled into the book. He keeps addressing his readers directly as "Sir" and "Madam," and one Madam, caught reading carelessly, is sent back to read the previous chapter again. When he reaches the beauty of the Widow Wadman, he leaves a blank page and invites you to draw her yourself. There is the black page, the marbled page (which he calls the motley emblem of his work), a whole chapter torn out, curly lines charting the plots of the earlier volumes. None of this is decoration. Each one reminds you that you are holding a book, something a man is in the middle of writing.

Part four: everyone is trapped in their own associations. This is Sterne's borrowing from Locke: once ideas are welded together, a person thinks along their own track. Walter turns any subject into theory; Toby hears any subject as fortification. Talk philosophy to him and he answers with a siege. So this family is always in conversation and never actually communicating. Locke treated faulty association as the source of prejudice; Sterne sees the other side: since nobody can understand anybody, the one thing left for people to exchange is goodwill. That is how Toby and Walter love each other.

To be honest: the black page and the blank page are a delight the first time and merely markers the second, and a good deal of Sterne's "experiment" is secondhand goods borrowed from Rabelais, Burton and Cervantes. Calling it "the first postmodern novel" is a later generation's retroactive claim, not Sterne's self-understanding; at the time he thought of himself above all as a humorist and a sentimentalist. But parts one, two and four are the real thing. They name something every realist novel works to avoid: life has no plot of its own; the plot is what the teller forces into it.

What it forces you to think about

One · A man turned the war that wounded him into a backyard toy. Should someone tear up the lawn?

Toby was struck by a stone at Namur and spent half his remaining life rebuilding Namur on a lawn. He is happy, mild, unable to harm a fly. But his game needs lead, and a window comes down on his nephew. Imagine you're Mrs. Shandy: do you let him keep playing?

The strongest case for tearing it up: Toby's game isn't just a game, it's a way of not facing something. He traded a wound in his body for a dot on a map, and so he never had to ask himself what the wound meant, until a widow asked it for him, when it was far too late to do anything. And hobby-horses have to be fed: jack-boots, sash weights, a child's accident. A good man's obsession still runs up a bill.

The strongest case for leaving it: what makes you so sure facing a thing is always better than going around it? Toby spent four years in bed, and the pain came not from the wound but from his endless failure to explain it. The lawn gave him back his speech, his laughter, a world where he was in command. When someone gets through an injury by way of a harmless obsession, maybe the best thing others can do is not cure them but ride alongside for a while. That is what Trim does, and he is the best friend in the book.

Sterne plainly loves Toby, but he let the sash fall and didn't whitewash the hobby-horse. Bring it home: the relative who pours a whole retirement into model trains, or a sport, or local history; or the thing you couldn't get through the week without. The question isn't whether it's good. It's what it lets you avoid, and who is paying for it.

Two · If every plan can be smashed by a falling window, why plan at all?

Walter designed his son's birth, nose, name and education, and every one of them lost to the smallest accident. If you were a new parent reading this book, what would you conclude?

The strongest case for giving up on plans: Walter's failure isn't bad luck, it's a wrong direction. He treats his child as an object for theory; during the three years of the unfinished Tristra-paedia, nobody is watching the boy, and nobody even notices the chamber pot is gone. The more thorough the plan, the more absent the planner. What would have protected that child was someone standing by the window, not someone arguing in the study.

The strongest case for planning anyway: accidents only look absurd because someone made a plan. Without Walter's insistence there would have been no doctor waiting downstairs. It was a hard birth that night; the forceps ruined the nose, but no one can say what would have happened without the man who held them. That accidents happen doesn't prove that preparation is useless, only that it is never enough. Walter is ridiculous, but he is the only person in that house who seriously thought about how to raise a child well.

Sterne laughs at Walter without despising him: that hour and a half face down on the bed is not written for laughs. Bring it home: the classes you've signed your child up for, the five-year plan you drew up for yourself. Are they taking care of a person, or of your fear of losing control?

Three · Should you live your life or record it?

Tristram finds that the more he writes, the further behind he falls; he runs off to France half to escape Death and half to find something new to write about. If you have ever held up a phone to film a child's first steps, you are in his position.

The strongest case for recording it: life that goes untold disappears faster than you'd think. Almost everything Tristram writes happened before he was born. Without this narrator coughing blood, these people wouldn't have left so much as a black page behind. Recording isn't the opposite of living; it is the only way to let the dead have the last word.

The strongest case for living it: the book itself is the counter-evidence. Tristram spends nine volumes on other people; his own life, whom he loved, what he did, who Jenny is, is almost a blank. His best moment is the dance with Nannette in Languedoc, when he isn't writing, and the book gives it only a few paragraphs. Writing can't catch up with life, so a man who does nothing but write ends up with nothing but writing.

Sterne himself wrote furiously until he died, yet he gave his narrator's finest moment to a dance. Bring it home: the last time you didn't reach for your phone and just watched, do you remember it? More clearly than the things you filmed, or less?

Motifs: what Sterne kept writing about

Sterne wrote only two novels, but his obsessions are plain. The body is forever interrupting the mind. Walter's theories are interrupted by a nose and a window; a clergyman's sermon and a dirty pun share a page. Sterne himself was that contradiction: a parson who wrote a book full of innuendo and then published his own sermons under the title The Sermons of Mr. Yorick (1760). Yorick was his mask.

The second obsession is death at arm's length. He coughed blood from a young age, and the seventh volume is simply a race against Death. A Sentimental Journey (1768), published just before he died, sends Yorick himself to France, where he weeps over a dead donkey and a caged starling that keeps repeating that it can't get out. The laughter-through-tears of Tristram Shandy turns there into more tears than laughter; and that book stops in mid-sentence, on a hand reaching out and not yet closing on anything. He spent his whole career writing interruptions, and didn't even let himself finish the last one.

Controversy and criticism

"Nothing odd will do long." The most famous put-down came from Samuel Johnson, who in 1776 told Boswell that nothing odd will last, and that Tristram Shandy had not. History overturned the verdict, but the problem it names is real: part of the book's first sensation was novelty, and by the later volumes reviewers were already complaining of repetition and too many smutty jokes.

Plagiarism. In 1798 the physician John Ferriar published a study that set out, passage by passage, the sentences and paragraphs Sterne had lifted from Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy, from Rabelais and others. The most embarrassing case: a passage in the novel denouncing writers who copy from each other is itself copied from Burton. Defenders call it deliberate pastiche; critics call it being caught red-handed. The borrowing itself is beyond dispute.

A clergyman's dirty book. Many contemporaries thought it unseemly for a man of the cloth. Later readers more often ask whether his "sentiment" was a performance: the author who weeps over a fly was coldly estranged from his wife and, in his last years, carried on an amorous correspondence with a married woman, Eliza Draper. That belongs to the biography and needn't be used to try the book, but the sentimental label has always travelled with a charge of hypocrisy.

Where are the women? Mrs. Shandy is the most silent person in the book. She almost never offers an opinion, agrees with whatever her husband says, and her compliance is played for laughs. The Widow Wadman is the only character who knows exactly what she wants and goes after it, yet her desire is written as the punchline of a riddle. Feminist critics argue that much of the book's humour rests on treating women's bodies as a joke to be decoded. Others read it the other way: the last question, "what is all this story about?", belongs to Mrs. Shandy, and in a room full of men on hobby-horses, she is the only one who sees that the whole thing is a cock and bull.

Crossovers

  • Mathematics / philosophy · Russell's "Tristram Shandy paradox": In The Principles of Mathematics (1903), Bertrand Russell uses a Tristram who takes two years to chronicle two days: if he lived forever, every day of his life would still get written, because an infinite whole can be as large as one of its parts. Alike in "never catching up, yet finishing"; unlike in that Russell's Tristram is immortal and Sterne's is coughing blood.
  • Psychology · Pavlov's classical conditioning: Pair a bell with food often enough and the dog salivates at the bell alone. Mrs. Shandy's clock-and-bed reflex has the same shape: a neutral signal, through repeated pairing, takes over another response. Unlike in that Pavlov measured glands, while Sterne shows a person betrayed by her own associations at her most private moment.
  • Psychology · Freud's fort–da game: In Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920), Freud describes his toddler grandson throwing a spool away and reeling it back, which he read as the child actively replaying his mother's departure in order to master it. Toby rebuilding the Namur that wounded him has the same shape: the blow he suffered passively becomes a game he commands. Unlike in that Freud went on to "repetition compulsion," while Toby's repetition is cheerful and shared.
  • Psychology · The Zeigarnik effect: In the 1920s Bluma Zeigarnik reported that interrupted tasks are remembered better than completed ones. Toby's "I think—," suspended for chapters, hooks the reader the same way. Unlike in that later replications have been inconsistent, and Sterne's interruptions are deliberate: he doesn't want you to remember, he wants you to wait.

Ten lines to take with you

① A child was conceived inside the question "have you not forgot to wind up the clock?"

② The father had one theory for noses, one for names, one for education; all three lost to a pair of forceps, a maid and a window.

③ Uncle Toby says "I think—" and the rest arrives chapters later: let's ring the bell.

④ He caught a fly, opened the window and let it go: the world is wide enough for both of us.

⑤ Upstairs a man mourns his son with Socrates; downstairs another just lets a hat fall to the floor.

⑥ The angel wrote the oath down, and a tear washed it away.

⑦ A year of writing to reach the first day of his life: the longer he lives, the more he has to write.

⑧ The widow asked where he was wounded, and he sent for a map.

⑨ A parson buried in the first volume gets the last word of the ninth.

⑩ "What is all this story about?" The one who asks is the only person in the book not riding a hobby-horse.
