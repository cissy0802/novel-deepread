Novels, Deep-Read · Liu Cixin · No.57

Fleeing with the Earth

The Wandering Earth (2000) · Micro Era (2001) · Devourer (2002) · Liu Cixin

中文 →

Five thousand people are standing on a frozen sea. The batteries have just been pulled from their sealed suits.
On the shore, a few hundred thousand others are singing "O Sole Mio." They have reasons to be cheerful: the Earth has been running for four hundred years, the sun is still hanging there behind them, and it shows not the slightest sign of blowing up. So the five thousand on the ice are frauds — the people who drove a hundred generations underground on a lie — and today the bill comes due.
Halfway through the song, the sky lights up.
It is not a sunrise.

This page contains complete spoilers — including the one-second reversal that ends The Wandering Earth, the button the last macro-human presses in Micro Era, and the terms humanity is offered in Devourer. This site's conviction is that everything worth discussing lies on the far side of the ending; without the ending you can only discuss atmosphere. If you want the suspense, go read the originals first. This page will wait.

In one breath

Three stories work the same problem three times: the sun is finished, humanity has to survive, so what are you willing to trade? The first trades away "human." Twelve thousand engines push the planet out of the solar system, and four hundred years later the survivors execute the scientists who told them the truth, on the ice. The second trades away "big." People shrink themselves to a billionth of their size, and the species loses the capacity for grief. The third gets nothing at all. Earth is eaten by a ring fifty thousand kilometres across, and the terms humanity is offered are: live to sixty, be kept comfortable, then go to the table.

Coordinates

Liu Cixin (b. 1963) wrote all three while working as an engineer at the Niangziguan power plant in Shanxi, six to eight years before The Three-Body Problem appeared. The Wandering Earth ran in Science Fiction World in 2000 and took that year's top Galaxy Award; Micro Era was written in 1999 and published in 2001; Devourer appeared in November 2002 and won a Galaxy Award readers' nomination. All three come from his "push one idea to its limit" period.

Read together, they turn out to be the laboratory for The Three-Body Problem. The hardest propositions in that trilogy — that survival is a civilization's first need, that morality gets repriced at cosmic scale, that a species may rebuild itself into something else in order to live — are each field-tested here, and tested with less mercy. A novel has to leave the reader a door. A novella doesn't.

The story

Act one · A child who has never seen night (The Wandering Earth, part one)

The first sentence lays the whole world out:

"I never saw the night. I never saw the stars. I never saw spring, autumn or winter."— The Wandering Earth, opening line (my rendering)

The narrator is born at the end of the Braking Era, just as the Earth's rotation stops. The reason he has never seen night or stars is that the plasma columns of the Earth Engines burn permanently overhead.

The cause is an astronomical finding: the sun will undergo a helium flash within a few centuries — once the hydrogen in its core is spent, the helium ignites in a very short window, the star swells violently, swallows the inner planets and then collapses into a white dwarf. Earth has no future beyond a few hundred years.

Humanity argued at length over how to run. The Spaceship faction wanted arks: light, fast, gone early. The Earth faction won with an argument that becomes the bedrock of the whole story — any ship is a closed ecosystem, and humanity has never kept a closed ecosystem self-sustaining for a century; only Earth, a natural system with air and oceans and soil that has run for billions of years, can be trusted. If we leave, we take it with us.

So: twelve thousand Earth Engines built across the plains of Asia and the Americas, burning heavy elements — which is to say, burning rock. In primary school, the narrator's class of thirty is taken on a round-the-world trip by Teacher Xiaoxing (a school subject, not a holiday) to see North China Engine No. 794 below the Taihang Mountains at Shijiazhuang: eleven thousand metres tall, more than two thousand metres higher than Everest. They also go up to the surface, where the waves have frozen solid in the act of breaking, standing in rows like walls, and they watch the sunrise between them. seed ①

The Braking Era takes forty-two years to stop the planet spinning. The braking is itself a catastrophe: the oceans keep their momentum and are thrown onto the continents. The survivors move into cities five hundred metres underground, and from then on the surface is simply a place you need a sealed suit to visit.

Then comes the lightest and coldest scene in the book. The narrator's father tells his mother he has fallen in love with Teacher Xiaoxing and wants to move in with her. She asks a question or two, tells him to go if that's what he wants, and turns back to watch the fireworks outside the window. Two months later he comes home the way a man comes home from an errand, and she never asks about it. seed ②

Liu isn't writing the catastrophe here; he is writing the shape it leaves inside one family. Love, fidelity and jealousy all require a "later" in order to exist, and this world has no later. Humanity has not turned wicked. It has simply let an unused organ atrophy.

Act two · Fifteen orbits (The Wandering Earth, part two)

The Escape Era runs on gravity assists: Earth must circle the sun fifteen times, going to full thrust at each perihelion, borrowing the star's own gravity to bank speed until, after fifteen passes, it can break free.

The price is fifteen trips past the execution block. At perihelion the surface is roasted beyond approach; at aphelion everything freezes. The worst disaster comes from inside the planet — the iron-nickel balance of the core is wrecked by all this abuse and magma floods the underground cities. The narrator's mother dies in one of them.

His father dies above. He serves in the space force, and when the Earth crosses the asteroid belt a meteoroid destroys his ship.

The narrator's reaction to both deaths is very close to no reaction at all. This is not a cold narrator — this world has switched off the mourning function, because every person lives every day at the edge of death and nobody can afford extended grief.

He marries, too: at an Olympic bobsleigh event he meets a Japanese athlete, Yamabe Kayoko, and it is love at first sight — the only kind of love this era has time for.

Later the Earth takes its final slingshot around Jupiter, which swells until it fills half the sky, and breaks free of the sun into the First Wandering Era.

And it is in the Escape Era that a story starts circulating in the underground cities: the sun looks perfectly fine, nobody alive has seen evidence of a helium flash, and perhaps the Coalition Government has been lying from the start. seed ③ Nobody takes it seriously. It is just the grumbling of hard years.

Act three · The sun did not explode (The Wandering Earth, part three)

Four centuries pass. The Earth clears the solar system, and the sun, looking back, is just another bright star. Humanity lines up four hundred years of photographs of it — and they are indistinguishable from the ones taken at the start.

By now the grumbling has grown into a party. The Homecoming faction's reasoning is hard to dismiss: if the sun is not going to explode, then four hundred years of global martial law, a hundred generations of forced labour underground, and an entire crust fed into the engines constitute the largest fraud in human history — and the Coalition Government has ruled on the strength of it for four hundred years. They want to steer the planet back.

The revolt comes. Kayoko joins the rebels and is killed.

The rebels take the Earth Engine control centre. The government's last five thousand surrender rather than fight beside the machinery, which is the planet's only means of moving. Sentencing is quick: drive them onto the frozen sea, strip the batteries from their suits, and let a surface at a hundred degrees below zero carry out the execution. payoff ① ↑ It is the same sea where Teacher Xiaoxing took thirty children to watch the sunrise. The same frozen waves: a school outing in act one, an execution ground at the end — Liu never changes the set, he just walks four hundred years across the same slab of ice.

A few hundred thousand people on the shore start singing "O Sole Mio."

Then the helium flash. payoff ③ ↑ The rumour that travelled from the Escape Era to this morning is refuted within a second of finally taking power — and the thing that refutes it is not a court but the star it said would never explode. The five thousand on the ice and the crowd on the shore are lit by the same light, and the song stops mid-phrase.

The executed win the entire argument within minutes of dying, and the victory does them no good whatsoever. The Earth keeps going, carrying the people who have just killed the ones who saved them.

It is worth turning the camera back on the narrator. payoff ② ↑ He did not weep for his father, or his mother, or Kayoko, and he is not weeping now for the five thousand on the ice; he is standing in the crowd that is singing. His mother's weightless little "go if you want," four hundred years earlier, finally shows its true weight: it was never a character trait, it was a diagnosis. What the Wandering Earth project actually re-engineered was never the planet's orbit but the people riding on it — turn a species' emotional range down notch by notch and it can endure a hundred generations of darkness, and once it is turned down, it is also capable of what happens on that ice.

Act four · The Ark comes home (Micro Era)

Micro Era swaps in a different catastrophe: the sun will undergo a brief energy flare, shedding roughly five per cent of its mass, and the Earth's surface will be burned down to bare rock. Humanity sends a dozen-odd ships to nearby stars to look for a new home. The people aboard are called Pioneers.

The Ark passes sixty stars and finds nothing usable. Flying at close to light speed, it logs twenty-three years aboard while twenty-five thousand pass on Earth. What the Pioneer comes home to is exactly the planet the forecast promised: no ocean, no cloud, just cooked stone.

He is certain he is the last human being in the universe, and he has already worked out how to end himself. He also knows what is in the hold — the embryonic cells of Macro Era humanity, and a seed bank. seed ④ They were meant for the new home. There is no new home.

Then he notices something glittering on the ground. Humanity did not die. Before the flare, people used genetic engineering to reduce themselves to a billionth of their former scale — a nation fits in a room, a city is a small bright frost on the rock. Resource consumption falls to almost nothing, so the scorched planet supports them not just adequately but lavishly. This is the Micro Era, and he is the last macro-human.

The micro world is bright, cheerful and permanently mid-festival. They treat the giant who came down from the sky as a god and throw a celebration for him. He tells them about the Macro Era — the oceans, the forests, the weight of the people who lived twenty-five thousand years ago — and they listen happily without understanding a word of the heaviness. In a world that no longer knows scarcity, grief has no job to do, so it was not inherited.

Which leaves a genuine button in his hand. The embryos in the hold could bring the Macro Era back: back to claim a share of the planet, back with all of old humanity's weight and ambition, competing for ground with these children too small to see. payoff ④ ↑ He vaporises every embryo. Then he opens the other half of the hold and scatters the seed bank's plant seeds across the bare rock, as a gift to the Micro Era.

The continuation of humanity is completed by the last old human strangling old humanity with his own hands. He has no enemy, no audience, and nobody who will ever know what he gave up; he plants the seeds, and his line ends.

Act five · A ring comes down out of the sky (Devourer)

In Devourer, even the warning is intercepted. Earth's space force stops an interstellar crystal and reads the message inside: the Devourer Empire's generation ship is arriving in a hundred years to drain this planet of its resources.

The Devourer is a ring: fifty thousand kilometres across, with a band ten thousand kilometres wide. It has been travelling between stars for an unknown length of time, eating as it goes.

The envoy arrives first. He is a dinosaur, named Dà Yá — "Big Tooth." The news he brings is harder to digest than the ship: the Devourer Empire is descended from the dinosaurs who left Earth tens of millions of years ago. They built a civilization in the Cretaceous, consumed this planet's ecology down to nothing, and left. Now they are back to eat the old place. seed ⑤

Big Tooth doesn't threaten anyone. He is rather courteous. The terms are: humanity need not go extinct — the Empire will keep humans as livestock on the ring, raise them to sixty, and guarantee they are comfortable and cheerful along the way, because contented livestock taste better. Human negotiators argue for something, anything, to be preserved of human civilization, and he dismantles their hopes one by one with a story about the Kingdom of White Mountain. In a ledger that has been running for tens of millions of years, the dignity of a civilization is not a line item with a price.

Humanity declares war. A century of preparation goes into one move: turn the Moon into a weapon, calculate its limiting speed, and put it head-on into the Devourer. It very nearly works. The Devourer is forced into an emergency over-limit acceleration, pays enormously for it, and passes the Moon at a distance of four hundred kilometres — on a fifty-thousand-kilometre scale, that is close enough to shave.

Then the Earth is eaten. The hundred-odd survivors of the command centre go into hibernation to wait for the drained planet to be spat back out.

Two hundred and thirty years later they wake and return to Earth — a desolate yellow world. Big Tooth comes too, saying he also hibernated, in order to see them once more. The Earth soldiers board his ship for the Devourer Empire; the Marshal goes last, and before he climbs aboard he looks down at the ground.

He sees ants. payoff ⑤ ↑ That one glance re-totals the whole book: Earth's civilized species get smaller with each turn — dinosaurs, then humans, then presumably them. Big Tooth is not an invader, he is a child who came home to eat his parents' house; the Marshal is not a loser, he is the one handing the baton on. Liu later pulled this single stroke out and expanded it into Of Ants and Dinosaurs: dinosaurs and ants build a joint civilization in the Cretaceous and then destroy it together. The ants at the end of Devourer crawled out of that history.

Three stories, one fuse

None of the three catastrophes is the story; the catastrophe is only the starting gun. The story is entirely in the question that follows it: what are you prepared to trade in order to live? The Wandering Earth trades "human" — four centuries of martial law, a hundred generations underground, love compressed into a weightless "go if you want" — for a life in transit, and the humans who come out the other end do what they do on that ice. Micro Era trades "big" — the body, grief, the entire memory of the old species — for lasting peace on a cinder, and the bill is paid by the last macro-human, who refuses to let his own line come back. Devourer gets nothing, because the offer on the table is "you may live, but as meat." Three times the same question: is the thing that survived still the thing that went in? Three times the same answer — no, but it is still here. Liu never comforts you with the idea that you can have both.

Three scenes in slow motion

1 · "Go if you want" — a catastrophe inside a shrug

Replay it. The father says he is in love with Teacher Xiaoxing and wants to move in with her; the mother asks a couple of questions, tells him to go, and turns to the window to watch the fireworks. Two months later he is back, and she never raises it.

The power of the passage is entirely in its refusal to be powerful. Any other writer would grant the mother a reaction: a tear, a sharp question, a smashed cup — even a cold laugh would do, because a cold laugh means she still cares. Liu gives her nothing. The point of view sits with the son, who finds nothing strange in it either, and who narrates the episode in the tone people use for weather. Which makes the reader the only person in the room who thinks something is wrong, and that dislocation works better than any denunciation: you are grieving on this family's behalf, and this family has lost the equipment for grief.

2 · The second of the flash — the reader is a rebel too

The narrative gamble here is enormous. Liu spends a long stretch making "the sun is not going to explode" solid: four centuries of identical photographs, no evidence an ordinary person could check, and martial law that really has run for four hundred years. Without noticing, the reader has moved over to the rebels' side — the execution seems excessive, but the anger seems earned.

Then one line capsizes the ship.

The technique is an extreme use of timing: the truth is not investigated by a character or exposed by an informant, it simply arrives, a few minutes late. Those few minutes are the whole cruelty of the ending — a little earlier and five thousand people live; a little later and the verdict is simply history. Liu stages no repentance scene. He lets the universe speak immediately after humanity has brought the gavel down, and holds the camera on the faces of people still halfway through a song. This is not contempt for mobs. It is the claim that human beings are constitutionally unable to wait for evidence.

3 · Pressing the button — the largest decision, with no audience

The moment the Pioneer vaporises the embryos has every dramatic ingredient: the last backup of an entire civilization, one hand, an irreversible act. Liu writes it almost without sound.

What matters is what he deleted: no opposition, no debate, no witness. The micro-humans do not know the button exists; there is no second macro-human to consult; the decision will not enter any history. A decision nobody opposes is not power, it is conscience — and that purity is exactly what Liu is after.

The action that follows holds the whole story up: he turns around and sows seeds. First destroy your own continuation, then make a present of someone else's — two gestures side by side, with no explanation offered. Here, unusually, Liu puts the value of a civilization in one person's hands rather than in a law.

The ideas, taken seriously

1 · Earth Engines: where the arithmetic fails

The chain is clear: fusion of heavy elements (burning crustal rock) for thrust, twelve thousand engines firing together, first to stop the spin, then fifteen perihelion burns to throw the planet clear.

What holds up is the premise. The Spaceship-versus-Earth argument is a real one: humanity has never kept a closed ecosystem self-sustaining for long, which is a documented engineering failure, and "bring the planet" is not absurd in that light — it solves the hardest part by the stupidest possible means.

What fails is the energy budget. Accelerating a planet to solar escape and across four light years in a few thousand years demands an astronomical amount of kinetic energy, and the fuel can only come from Earth's own rock — you are burning the very thing you are trying to move, and the exhaust has to be thrown away as reaction mass, so the planet is dieting the entire way. Stopping the rotation brings its own cascade through oceans, crust and magnetic field, and that magma flooding the underground cities is the author's own honest acknowledgement of the bill.

One more thing has to be said plainly: the real sun is not going to helium-flash within centuries. On the stellar timetable the sun has roughly five billion years left on the main sequence before the red giant phase, and the helium flash comes near the end of that phase — nor is it the sudden event the story describes. This is a premise, not a forecast. Liu needs one thing from it: a death sentence that is completely credible and completely unverifiable, so that the revolt four hundred years later has room to happen.

2 · Shrinking a person a billionfold: which link is literature

On the resource side the accounting is elegant: consumption scales with volume, capability scales with intelligence. Cut the volume to a billionth and food, shelter and energy round to zero, so a planet burnt to bare rock supports an entire civilization without strain. That step is airtight, and it is the story's real idea.

The body is where it breaks. Organisms do not scale proportionally — the square-cube law says volume falls with the cube of length while surface area falls with the square, so the smaller you get the more surface effects dominate. At micrometre scale, surface tension matters far more than gravity (a drop of water is a mountain you cannot climb); more fundamentally, human-level cognition needs neurons in the tens of billions and vastly more connections, and there is no known way to pack that into such a body. This is a premise, not biology.

It hardly damages the story, because what Micro Era is really testing is not size but whether a civilization keeps the ability to hurt once scarcity is gone. The micro-humans are permanently mid-festival precisely because their world holds nothing left to fear. Liu gives that the shape of a utopia, then stands the last old human beside it and lets him fail to decide whether to be glad.

3 · A civilization that eats planets: bad economics, deliberately

The easiest objection to the Devourer Empire is that the energy cost of interstellar travel is far greater than the resources you can loot from one planet. Anything that can build a ring fifty thousand kilometres wide and force an over-limit burn to dodge a moon already commands enough energy to synthesise whatever it needs at home. Why fly for centuries to dismantle a ball of rock? Every "cosmic predator" premise has this problem, and Devourer does not dodge it.

The story's answer is ecological rather than economic: the Empire is a species that ate its own home and was forced onto the road, and its behaviour is a habit tens of millions of years old rather than an optimum. That works as literature — a civilization is allowed to be stupid, allowed to be captured by its own path dependence. But claiming the energy budget closes would be dishonest: this is the skeleton of a parable, not a feasibility study.

The genuinely hard link is the livestock clause. The idea that contented livestock taste better takes the entire logic by which humans have handled animals for millennia and flips it over unchanged. It needs no scientific support, because the evidence is on our own dinner tables.

What it forces you to think about

One · If your descendants could be spared grief forever, would you sign?

Put it in a concrete setting. Tomorrow a consent form is on your table. Sign, and your children and their descendants get a new neural architecture: still intelligent, still creative, still capable of love, but never again capable of deep grief — not at a death, not at the collapse of a hope. They will go on being happy, like the micro-humans. Decline, and they inherit your version: they will suffer, they will despair, they will lie awake at four in the morning.

The strongest case for signing is not "happiness is better" but "nobody chose the pain." Nobody consented to be born with this nervous system. If grief can be removed while intelligence and love remain, the person who refuses to sign is making the crueller decision on someone else's behalf — what you are preserving is not their depth but your taste for depth, and they carry the cost. Every technology that has ever reduced suffering was opposed by someone arguing that suffering makes us profound, and none of those objections looks good in hindsight.

The strongest case against signing is not "grief has value" but "grief is the instrument." Grief is what tells you which things mattered. Remove it and you still love, but your love loses its units — if everything is precious, nothing is. The micro-humans throw a festival for the Pioneer, and they would throw one for anything else; that is not happiness, that is a thermostat. Worse, the form is one-way: the people on the far side of it will have no way to judge what they lost, because judging it requires the faculty that was deleted.

You can see where Liu stands from the staging: he lets the micro-humans flourish, lets the last macro-human protect them and strangle his own line to do it — and keeps the point of view, from first page to last, on the one who can still be sad. He awards the victory to the micro-humans and the story to the macro-human.

This is not far from us. Drugs that regulate mood, feeds that filter out bad news, a path laid out for a child so they can never fail — each is one box on that consent form, except that we tick them one at a time and so are never asked to think it through all at once.

Two · When a truth can't be verified for four hundred years, may the majority disbelieve it?

The revolt in The Wandering Earth is not a mob tantrum, and its reasoning deserves to be laid out in full: four hundred years have passed; a hundred generations have been born and died underground; not one living person has seen evidence of a helium flash; all the authorities can offer is models and photographs, and the photographs show a perfectly healthy sun — while the Coalition Government has maintained global martial law on the strength of that prediction. "An unfalsifiable apocalypse plus a government that has ruled on it for four centuries" is the textbook recipe for tyranny. What the rebels want is modest: show us something we can see.

The strongest case on the other side is not "scientists know better." It is that some truths have verification cycles longer than a human life, which is not a defect of science but the timescale of the universe. A society that rules "if I can't see it in my lifetime it doesn't count" can never handle a cross-generational problem — stellar evolution, nuclear waste, debt, demography, all the same. Putting such judgements to a vote systematically excludes the people who will live with the consequences.

Neither side cheats, and that is what makes the ending terrifying. The rebels are not villains; they want verifiability. The government are not liars; they are guarding a correct prediction. What fails is time: human trust structures cannot survive four hundred years, and the star does not care.

Bring it home: climate policy, pension systems, basic research funding, any public decision that charges you now and pays out in decades — all of it walks the same wire. The difference is that our cycles are much shorter, so our "helium flash" usually arrives while we are still alive to see it. Call that luck.

Three · Kept alive, or extinct with your dignity intact?

Take Big Tooth's terms seriously instead of as villain dialogue. Humans are not slaughtered; they are kept on the ring, live to sixty, and are materially provided for and emotionally content throughout. Which means an ordinary person's life under the Devourer Empire might be materially better than their life on Earth — only the ending is fixed, and they will never learn they are cattle, because knowing would make them unhappy and unhappiness spoils the product.

The strongest case for accepting: "dignity" is a luxury only the living get to discuss, and extinction compensates nobody. An unwinnable war kills everyone now so that a few can feel better about how they died; sixty contented years are a real thing for most individuals, and who authorised a handful of would-be martyrs to refuse it on their behalf? Staying alive is the precondition for every future reversal.

The strongest case for fighting is not "give me liberty or give me death." It is that the moment you accept the terms, you stop being a party to the contract and become its object. Livestock are not asked whether to renew. The contentment is administered, the lifespan is set, the breeding is managed — this is not a worse life, it is a different standing, and once inside it there is no lever for getting out, because any capacity for resistance will be culled as a defect of stock. And the Moon very nearly worked, which means fighting was not pure martyrdom: it was this species acting as a subject one last time.

Liu's own answer hides in the ending: humanity fights, loses, the planet is eaten — and then the Marshal and Big Tooth grow old, hibernate and board a ship together. He hands no medal to heroism and does not say surrender would have been better. He just has the Marshal look down at the ants before he climbs in.

The everyday version of this question is commoner than it sounds: whether to take a treatment with a dismal cure rate, whether to quit a stable job that treats you as a component, whether to stay in a safe relationship that does not see you as a person. Every time it asks the same thing — do you want more time, or do you want to remain the one making the decision?

The obsession he keeps returning to

Put the three back among his other books and the same obsession surfaces everywhere: on a civilization's balance sheet, humanity is an expense, not an asset. Nearly all his important fiction presses the same spot — when the pressure to survive gets high enough, the things we prize (family, honesty, freedom, the capacity for grief, the standing of being a person at all) get struck off one by one, and what strikes them off is usually not the enemy but our own reasoning.

The Three-Body trilogy is the long-form version: Cheng Xin chooses humanity twice and the species pays twice, while Thomas Wade, who would strike every human consideration off the page, comes close to saving everyone. Ball Lightning is the private version: a man stakes a life on one physical phenomenon, obtains it, and forfeits the right to be an ordinary person. Of Ants and Dinosaurs is the expansion of that last glance in Devourer: two species build a civilization in partnership and destroy it because they need each other.

These three sit in a clear place in the body of work: they were written before he learned to leave the reader a door. The Wandering Earth offers no compensation at all, and even the tenderness of Micro Era is a complete farewell.

Criticism, honestly

  • The film is not the book, and points almost the opposite way. Guo Fan's 2019 The Wandering Earth is a landmark in Chinese-language science fiction cinema, but its plot was built from scratch: Liu Peiqiang, Liu Qi and the crisis of igniting Jupiter are all inventions of the film. Meanwhile the novella's actual core — the revolt four centuries on, the five thousand on the ice, the irony of the flash — goes entirely unused. Reading the film's warmth back into the original is the single most common misunderstanding of this story.
  • The charge that it holds ordinary people in contempt. The story gives us a Coalition Government that is right about everything and a populace that executes its benefactors on the ice, which more than a few critics read as a romance of technocratic rule. The defence is that the rebels' reasoning is presented fully and fairly, and that the author does not write them as idiots; he merely lets the universe speak at the worst possible moment. The argument is real, though, and one reading does not dissolve it.
  • Flat characters, functional women. This is the oldest and most accurate criticism of Liu. The people in these three stories are largely carriers for the premise: the mother, Teacher Xiaoxing and Kayoko get a gesture or two and no interiority; the human side of Devourer is little more than a set of ranks. Scale and ideas are his strengths; character is his weakness, and it shows most in short fiction.
  • The "law of the jungle" objection. Devourer renders the universe as a food chain and the rise and fall of civilizations as an ecological ledger, and some readers hold that even presenting this amounts to flattering the logic of the strong. Others note the story's sympathy stays with the eaten, and that Big Tooth is himself a satire of strength — he wins, but he is only a child who came back to gnaw on his parents' house.
  • Controversy over the author's own public statements. A long 2019 profile of Liu Cixin in The New Yorker included responses on several Chinese political questions that drew wide argument in the English-speaking world, and the question of whether author and work can be separated has followed his international reputation since. That is stated here as a fact.

Ten sentences to take away

1. All three stories ask one question — what will you trade in order to live — and Liu never pretends you can keep both sides of it.

2. What the Wandering Earth project actually moves is not the planet's orbit but the people riding on it; the orbit can be calculated, the people are what really got spent.

3. The mother's weightless "go if you want" is the largest disaster site in the book: love and jealousy both require a "later," and this world has none.

4. The few minutes at the end are the whole cruelty — the truth turns up by itself, slightly late, and does not accept apologies.

5. The rebels are not a mob; they want evidence they can see. What fails is not their intelligence but a species' trust, which cannot span four hundred years.

6. The frozen sea where schoolchildren watched a sunrise becomes an execution ground four centuries later; Liu never changes the set, he just walks time across the same ice.

7. The hardest stroke in Micro Era has no witnesses: humanity's continuation is completed by the last old human ending old humanity himself.

8. The micro-humans' cheer is not happiness but a thermostat — delete grief and love survives, minus its units.

9. The notion that contented livestock taste better needs no scientific backing; the evidence is on our own tables, simply turned over.

10. The ants the Marshal sees before boarding are the conclusion of all three: civilization gets handed on, but not necessarily to you.
