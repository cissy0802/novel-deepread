Novels, Deep-Read · Liu Cixin · No.90

War and Ashes

Full-Spectrum Barrage Jamming · Glory and Dreams · Fire in the Earth · Liu Cixin · c. 2000

中文 →

Beijing, the main Olympic stadium. The marathon winner has already crossed the line; the clock has stopped. A hundred thousand people stay in their seats.
They sit there quietly, watching the tunnel, waiting for a girl who is still somewhere out on the city streets — a girl who cannot speak, whose country is being taken apart piece by piece, whose decent running shoes were bought in a Beijing sports shop after she arrived.
When she finally comes through the tunnel, the whole stadium starts shouting her name.

Full spoilers ahead — including where Misha finally steers his space station and how a war that blinds both sides comes to a halt; whether Xini finishes her forty-two kilometres and what the Republic of West Asia buys with an Olympic Games; and how the vow Liu Xin made at his father's deathbed burned down a city, then turned up, a hundred and twenty years later, as one breezy line in a schoolboy's diary. All three stories keep their force for the last few pages. There's no discussing them without the endings.

In one line

Three stories, three people backed into a corner. An astrophysicist whose country is losing an electronic war it cannot win, a hundred million kilometres from home with nothing but a space station. A mute runner whose country has been reduced to seventy-odd half-starved athletes in a war fought as an Olympics, with nothing but her legs. A miner's son whose father spent a lifetime breathing coal dust and finally suffocated, with nothing but a technology no one has tried at scale. What each of them ends up spending is their own body. All three ask the same thing: when the stronger side writes every rule, what is the last thing the weaker side still owns — and is it worth spending this way?

Coordinates

Liu Cixin (b. 1963) grew up in the coal country around Yangquan in Shanxi, and wrote these stories while still a computer engineer at the Niangziguan power plant. "Fire in the Earth" appeared in the February 2000 issue of Science Fiction World; "Full-Spectrum Barrage Jamming" won the Galaxy Award in 2001; "Glory and Dreams" comes from the same years and is set at the 2008 Beijing Olympics, which had not yet happened. The first two are in English in the 2020 collection To Hold Up the Sky.

They belong together because none of them is an "idea story" in the cosmic sense. There's no grand law of the universe here. They're about industry, nations and war: things on the ground.

The story

Act one · Full-Spectrum Barrage Jamming: a war you can't see

The near future. NATO armies are pushing into Russia from the west, and the Russians are falling back. Tanks and manpower aren't the problem; the Russians aren't outnumbered. The problem is invisible: the electromagnetic spectrum. NATO's electronic-warfare systems are far ahead. They can intercept, suppress and spoof Russian communications and radar. A Russian command post that speaks is overheard; a radar that switches on draws an anti-radiation missile. The front-line army is a giant with its eyes covered and its ears stopped.

The western front is commanded by Marshal Levchenko. His son Misha is no soldier. He's an astrophysicist, and right now he's aboard the Eternal Blizzard, a science station a hundred million kilometres from Earth, orbiting the Sun near the orbit of Mercury, with the single job of watching the star. Father and son are separated by more than distance: one fights a losing war on the ground, the other studies a star that has nothing to do with it.

Misha's girlfriend, Karina, is a major in the Russian army and the creator of a jamming system called Flood. Its logic is crude to the point of despair: if you can't beat the enemy on any single channel, stop trying and drown every channel at once — pour out powerful noise across the whole spectrum so that nobody can hear anybody and no radar sees clearly. In electronic warfare, spot jamming targets one frequency and barrage jamming smothers a wide band. Karina means to smother all of them.Setup ① The price is in the name: a flood doesn't sort friend from foe, and Russia's own radios and radars go blind too.

The bet is that this drags the war back to an age without electronics, where what counts is men, steel, endurance of cold and contempt for death — exactly what Russia has historically done best.

Act two · Full-Spectrum Barrage Jamming: into the Sun

Flood works, and immediately becomes a target. A jammer has to transmit, and transmitting gives away its position. NATO strikes come one after another and the jamming vehicles are destroyed one by one. Karina dies at her post; she doesn't let the equipment fall into enemy hands. The sea of noise begins to recede, and NATO's eyes and ears come slowly back.

The news reaches the station. From the Eternal Blizzard Misha sends back his assessment: if the jamming can't be kept up for another three or four days, the war is lost. Then he proposes something only an astrophysicist would think of: fly the station into the Sun. His calculation is that the impact will set off a violent disturbance on the solar surface, and that the charged particles and radiation it throws toward Earth will drown radio communication across the planet. The man-made flood is ebbing, so borrow one from the sky.

He flies the station in.Payoff ① ↑ Only here does Karina's idea of drowning every channel show its real scale. In the first half it's a weapon on wheels, the most a major can do on a battlefield. In the second half her lover blows the same idea up to the size of a star. What Misha does is keep Karina's flood going, with the Sun as the jammer. The same logic appears twice: the first time it ends with a woman's death, the second time a man's death carries it on. The title doesn't name a weapons system after all. It names a relay that two lovers run with their two lives.

The Sun erupts, and Earth's spectrum is drowned for about a week. For that week both sides are blind, fighting a mid-twentieth-century war with no satellites and no precision guidance, only men and steel. The Russian army counterattacks on numbers and will. The story stops as the NATO coalition pulls back in haste. It doesn't show what comes after victory.

Another version of the story circulates online with the setting moved to China and the characters renamed. Readers who've compared them report that in that version the pilot who flies into the Sun is called Zhuang Yu, the same name as the researcher in "Sun of China", though not the same man.

Act three · Glory and Dreams: a team instead of an army

In the second story, the international community has found what looks like a far more civilised way to abolish war. It's called the Peace Window: when two countries come into conflict they no longer fight; they settle it with an Olympic Games. Gold medals stand in for shells and the medal table for the casualty count. The first country to be tested is the Republic of West Asia, blockaded and sanctioned for years, its people living with poverty and hunger, and its opponent is a great Western power. The model is easy to recognise: Iraq under the international sanctions of the 1990s.

The stakes are anything but civilised. The result will decide whether West Asia gets dismembered by the great power, and every extra gold claws something back. But what does West Asia have left? Krell, head of its national sports bureau, combs a ruined country for its former athletes and gathers seventy-odd of them. They haven't trained properly or eaten properly in years.

Xini is the team's marathon runner, and she is mute.Setup ② On a field where results do the talking she's the quietest person on the team, and not only because she can't speak: nobody expects anything from her. She can't outrun world champions trained in the best conditions on earth. She only gets proper running shoes when she reaches Beijing and finds a sports shop.

Everyone on the team is in a different kind of trouble. The gymnast Laili has worked as a prostitute to survive; in competition she goes for a high-difficulty routine to win the points and breaks her neck.Setup ③ Sali, the team's best hope for gold, demands a large cash bonus, is refused, and withdraws. Liu doesn't defend anyone: when a country bets everything on one Olympics, some people stake their lives and some name their price. Hunger made both kinds.

Act four · Glory and Dreams: run to the end

The marathon is among the last events. Xini falls behind from the start, and the gap keeps growing. Her rival, a champion named Emma, runs a professional's race and crosses the line.

By any normal logic the race is over. But the hundred thousand people in the stadium don't leave. They sit quietly and wait for the West Asian girl who is far behind. When she finally appears in the tunnel, they start shouting for her to run all the way to the end.Payoff ② ↑ When we were told she was mute, it read as one more detail to make her pitiable. Here it turns over. Someone who has never been able to make a sound has her name shouted for her, over her last few hundred metres, by a hundred thousand strangers. She never says a word in the whole story, and the loudest thing in it is her name.

She crosses the line, falls in front of the Olympic flag and the flag of West Asia, and does not get up.Payoff ③ ↑ Laili's broken neck looked like a side plot at the time, one athlete's tragedy. When Xini falls we see it was the first instance of the same thing: in a contest that replaces war, the athletes' bodies are the shells. The Peace Window spares the casualties of the battlefield without sparing casualties. It moves them from soldiers to athletes, from under artillery fire to under the floodlights.

The ending is cold. The Games don't change West Asia's fate. Xini's death wins no gold and no peace. What it wins is that moment with a hundred thousand people in the stands, and that's all the "glory" in the title comes to.

Act five · Fire in the Earth: a father's last breath

The third story opens with a suffocation. Liu Xin's father is an old miner with stage-three silicosis. The coal and rock dust he has breathed for decades has hardened into nodules, and his lungs are slowly turning to stone. In the end it isn't the disease that kills him so much as running out of breath, one gasp at a time. Liu spends a whole passage on his open mouth and his failure to reach the air, and the reader's own chest tightens.

After the father dies, Liu Xin's mother wants him to quit high school and take his father's place at the mine; in that town it's the obvious way for a family to survive. What takes root in Liu Xin is something else: nobody should have to go down the pit again.Setup ④ Years later he comes home with a technology: underground coal gasification. You don't dig the coal out. You burn the seam in place, under control, turn it into combustible gas and draw that up through boreholes. No shafts, no coalface, no dust, so no silicosis.

The mining town he returns to has gone dull. The people he knew as a boy wear faces blunted by years underground, and that only makes him more impatient.

Act six · Fire in the Earth: a city that burned for eighteen years

He runs the trial at the very mine where his father worked all his life. Director Li of the mining bureau goes along reluctantly, and Aguli, a fire-control expert who knows coal fires, warns him again and again: the seams underground are connected, and a fire that gets out of the controlled zone can never be brought back. Liu Xin won't listen. He wants proof, and wants the trial big enough to convince everyone, so he keeps expanding it.Setup ⑤

The fire escapes along a narrow seam and breaks into the main seam below. You can't see a fire underground. The first thing people notice is that the ground is warm. Grass and trees wither patch by patch, and the earth under the city becomes a slowly heating pan. The city is abandoned and its people scattered. The fire burns for eighteen years.

In the end Liu Xin puts on the miner's work clothes his father left behind and walks down into the burning mine.Payoff ⑤ ↑ This is where the opening suffocation and the ending meet. The pit choked the father slowly. The son swore no one would go down it again, and ends up in his father's clothes as the last man to go down. The fate he spent his life trying to overturn plays out again in full on his own body, faster and hotter. Aguli's and Director Li's warnings come true here as well. But the story doesn't take their side, and the next page shows why.

The final section is titled "A Hundred and Twenty Years Later (A Middle-School Student's Diary)". A child writes up his day: a trip to the coal museum; the teacher saying that over a century ago this place was destroyed by an underground fire that burned for eighteen years; a visit to a modern mine, where gasified coal has long since become humanity's most ordinary energy source now that the oil has run out; and finally a go at how people used to dig coal in the old days, which strikes him as clumsy and miserable.Payoff ④ ↑ The vow Liu Xin made at his father's bedside is kept only here: nobody does go down the pit any more. Going underground has become a museum attraction and silicosis a word in a history lesson. The price was a city burning for eighteen years, the man who made the vow dying in the fire, and later generations not needing to remember his name. Cruellest of all is the tone. A child writes lightly about "people in the old days", and we've just read how one of those people suffocated, breath by breath.

One fuse

Put the three side by side and they share one fuse: in any set of rules written by the strong, or by history, there is a gap that only a human body can fill. The rules of electronic war were written by the technologically stronger side, and Misha fills the gap with a space station and himself. The rules of the Peace Window were written by a great power, and Xini fills it with her last few hundred metres and herself. The rules of the coal industry were written by a century of poverty, and Liu Xin fills it with a city and himself. Each title, "full-spectrum", "glory", "fire", names the moment the gap gets filled, not the gap. Liu states no law of the cosmos here. He says one thing: the bill is always paid in bodies.

Three slow-motion shots

Shot one: a message from a hundred million kilometres

The finest thing in "Full-Spectrum Barrage Jamming" isn't the flash as the station hits the Sun. It's the time lag before it. A hundred million kilometres separate the Eternal Blizzard from Earth, which radio waves take more than five minutes to cross. Every sentence Misha sends reaches his father as the Misha of a few minutes ago, and the people on Earth can only learn of his decisions after the fact.

Liu takes a war whose battlefield is the spectrum and lands it on a man cut off by physics itself. The soldiers at the front can't hear one another because of the jamming; the marshal and his son can't hear one another because of the speed of light. The whole story is about communication, and its last exchange is bound to go one way only. No one on Earth has time to talk him out of it or to say goodbye. The camera stays inside the station, and the reader, like everyone on the ground, can only wait for a decision made minutes ago to arrive.

Shot two: a hundred thousand people waiting

At the climax of "Glory and Dreams" the winner arrives first. That's the strangest choice in the story. Sports coverage would follow the leader over the line and cut. Liu does the opposite: after the winner finishes, the camera holds, and nothing happens. A hundred thousand people sit and watch an empty tunnel.

That blank is the heart of the story. As sport, the race is over. As a story, it's just beginning. From this moment Xini isn't running a race; the score no longer needs her. Making the crowd wait lifts an athlete out of the competition and hands her back to herself. Only then come the shouts, and only then does she fall. The rhythm presses down to total silence and then releases into a hundred thousand voices. A story about a mute girl does its work through the drop in sound.

Shot three: from suffocation to a diary

"Fire in the Earth" opens and closes with two different kinds of breathing. At the start, the father's suffocation is written very slowly, very close and very concretely, every failed breath, and the reader is held at the bedside. At the end, a schoolboy's diary is fast, distant and carefree: a hundred and twenty years and a city that burned for eighteen of them, dealt with in a sentence.

The force comes from the sudden jump in distance. Until then the narration stays right up against Liu Xin, his haste, his stubbornness, his guilt. In the last section the camera is thrown a hundred and twenty years away and looks back through the eyes of a child who feels nothing about any of it. We're made to stand in two places at once, knowing how much the man in the past suffered and seeing how little the people after him care. The cost of progress is invisible to those who benefit from it. Liu never says so; the tone of a diary says it for him.

The ideas, taken seriously

Full-spectrum barrage jamming: the concept is real; "full-spectrum" is fiction. Barrage jamming is a real electronic-warfare technique: broadband high-power noise that smothers a whole band at once. The cost is that the power gets spread thin, so the wider the band, the less power lands on each frequency. That's why truly covering everything is close to impossible. From long wave to millimetre wave spans several orders of magnitude, and no transmitter can out-shout an opponent at every frequency. More to the point, field telephones, fibre optics, signallers and runners don't care about radio jamming at all.

A station crashing into the Sun triggers a storm: this link doesn't hold; the story needs it. The Sun's mass is about 2×10³⁰ kg, and a space station falling in is a grain of sand dropped in the ocean. Sungrazing comets plunge into or skim the Sun every year, and none has ever set off a flare. Flares and coronal mass ejections draw their energy from tangled magnetic fields on the solar surface suddenly reconnecting, not from anything hitting it. But the consequence is real. Big solar eruptions do disrupt communications on Earth. The Carrington Event of 1859 made telegraph lines spark, and a geomagnetic storm in 1989 blacked out much of Quebec. During large flares, shortwave radio drops out across the daylit hemisphere. Liu borrows a real effect and invents its cause.

The Peace Window: not a science-fiction device but a political thought experiment. Its premise is that a war can be settled by a different contest, and history has something like it: many cultures settled wars by single combat between champions, and David and Goliath is the template. It breaks down in practice. A contest only replaces a war if the loser truly accepts the result, and a great power willing to accept results never needed a war in the first place. That's where the story's edge is. The Peace Window makes war's form civilised while keeping its imbalance intact. A country that can't feed itself competing at sport with a country that has the best training facilities on earth is not so different from the two of them comparing tanks.

Underground coal gasification: the hardest science of the three, almost all of it real. UCG exists. The Soviet Union began experimenting with it in the 1930s, and a plant at Angren in Uzbekistan ran on and off for decades from the 1960s. The principle is just as the story says: drill boreholes, feed air or oxygen into the seam, let the coal burn incompletely underground, and draw off a mix of carbon monoxide, hydrogen and methane. Underground coal fires are real too, and they really can burn for decades. In Centralia, Pennsylvania, a mine fire that started in 1962 is still burning, and the town has been almost entirely abandoned. India's Jharia field and China's Xinjiang have long-burning coal fires of their own. The fiction is one thing only: a gasification trial running away and setting a whole city alight. In practice UCG's main risks are groundwater contamination and subsidence; a company in Queensland, Australia, was convicted in 2018 over environmental harm from a gasification project. Liu chose the more visual disaster, but the kind of disaster is grounded.

What it makes you think about

Question one · When you can't win, is it legitimate to drag everyone into the dark?

Put concretely: you're in a contest you're bound to lose, and within the rules your opponent beats you at everything. Now you have a move that doesn't win. It overturns the whole board and sends everyone, you included, back to a primitive state where you have the edge. Do you make it?

The case for overturning it: Rules are never neutral. The rules of electronic war favour the technologically strong, which is why they like to fight there. Any "fair competition" designed by the strong is fair only in form. Play by someone else's rules and you lose. Changing the battlefield is the weak side's only wisdom, and its oldest: guerrilla war, strikes and wars of attrition all work on it. And Flood and the Sun harm no civilian. They only switch off everyone's eyes and put both sides back at the same starting line.

The case against: But dragging everyone into the dark is a harm in itself, and the people hurt worst are often not the opponent but bystanders who have nothing to do with the war. A solar storm that kills radio worldwide for a week means hospitals, flights, ships, power grids and distant distress calls all failing. None of that appears in the story, because none of it is on the battlefield. Whoever overturns the board only ever counts their own losses. Misha switches off the whole world's eyes, not just the enemy's. And once "if you can't win, wreck it" is shown to work, every future loser will do it.

Where does Liu stand? He's plainly moved by the tragic grandeur of it. The story makes Misha a hero and doesn't show anyone else on Earth during that week. But it doesn't show the victory either: it stops at the enemy's retreat and won't tell you what a win bought this way looks like. You've probably met the structure. Someone who can never win within the rules decides that nobody gets to win, in a team, a lawsuit or a price war. You've seen that person, or been them.

Question two · Running yourself to death for "glory": heroism, or being used?

Inside the story: Xini can no longer win. The champion has finished, the gold isn't West Asia's, and no step she takes changes the score. Should she stop? If you were her coach or her family, would you shout at her to finish or to stop?

The case for heroism: A country blockaded, starved and lined up for dismemberment has no voice in the world. Those last few hundred metres are the one time it is seen by a hundred thousand people and heard by the world. Glory isn't the score. It's a people treated as a test case proving, at the last moment, that it isn't one. Nobody forced her to run; she chose to. Deny that, and you're saying a mute girl isn't entitled to make a decision for her own country.

The case for exploitation: But what was her choice made inside? A situation where dignity can only be bought with the body; a Peace Window that uses athletes as ammunition; a hundred thousand people shouting at her to keep going. That shout is compassion and also pressure. It comes from kindness, and it also decides for her. A glory that can only be proved by a young person's death is itself an exploitation of the young, whether the exploiter is the enemy, the homeland or the weeping crowd. Set Laili's broken neck and Sali's asking price beside Xini, and Liu may not have meant to celebrate only one of the three.

Where does the author stand? The story's feeling is plainly with Xini; it's called "Glory and Dreams". But it reports the outcome coldly: the Games changed nothing. You've surely seen the pattern: the athlete who plays injured, the employee who works himself into hospital for the department's honour, the student who wrecks her health to make the family proud. Onlookers always shout "keep going"; the cost of keeping going falls on one person.

Question three · Is the person driven by pain the one who should lead change — or the one who shouldn't?

Put concretely: a reform's keenest, most urgent champion is often the person the old system hurt most, like Liu Xin, whose father suffocated from silicosis. His pain lets him see an evil that others have stopped noticing, and it also deafens him to any advice to slow down. Do you hand him the wheel?

The case for handing it over: The old system lasted a century because of the people it didn't hurt: the bureau chief, the expert, the families used to the pit. All of them have good reasons to wait a bit longer, and every year of waiting means another batch of men dead of silicosis. Caution is never neutral; the people underground pay for it. Without someone burning like Liu Xin, gasification would stay forever at "needs further study". The diary at the end proves he was heading in the right direction.

The case against: But his pain is exactly what made him wrong. Aguli and Director Li aren't stalling. They're stating a concrete fact that only people who know the trade know: the seams are connected. Liu Xin can't hear it, not because they're wrong but because he carries a dead father, and every suggestion to slow down sounds to him like betraying his father. His haste burns a city, and for eighteen years people are uprooted, mining families too. The people hurt most have the strongest motive and the largest blind spot.

Where does Liu stand? He makes Liu Xin both culprit and prophet: the first five sections put him on trial and the last one rehabilitates him, and Liu won't choose between the two. The question turns up around you constantly: someone burned by a previous regime designing the new one, a parent who was once wronged planning their child's life. The pain gives them a direction and takes away their brakes.

Motifs: what this writer keeps writing

Put these three back into Liu's work as a whole and two obsessions come into focus.

The first is the individual burned up for the whole. Misha, Xini and Liu Xin all end up patching a gap in a system with their own bodies. The shape keeps coming back in his later work: Lin Yun in Ball Lightning, willing to do anything for a weapon; the people in the Three-Body trilogy who make cold decisions for humanity's future. His fiction seldom doubts that sacrificing the individual for the whole is justified. What it cares about is whether the sacrifice is grand enough and exact enough. That's where he is most moving and where he is most often challenged.

The second is the poetry of industry. Someone who grew up in Shanxi coal country and worked in a power station writes about coal, electricity and steel with an intimacy few science-fiction writers have. The mining town in "Fire in the Earth", the window-cleaners hanging off skyscrapers in "Sun of China", the antenna at Red Coast Base in The Three-Body Problem all share the same eye: technology doesn't grow out of laboratories; it grows out of generations of people doing hard labour. "Fire in the Earth" is his most personal take on it. Silicosis, the father's death, was the most common way to die in the coal country Liu knew.

Controversy and criticism

  • Nationalism and the aesthetics of war. "Full-Spectrum Barrage Jamming" is often criticised as military wish-fulfilment: long stretches of electronic-warfare jargon, characters as chess pieces, a heroic sacrifice to tie it all off. One English-language reviewer called it the low point of its collection, and another pointed to a number of errors in the astrophysics and microelectronics. After 2014 and 2022, a story casting Russia as the tragic hero and NATO as the invader sits uneasily with many English-speaking readers. The readers' world changed, not the text, but the unease is worth recording.
  • Two versions. Some readers say the China-set version ends with a post-victory ancestral ceremony; others say that passage was a fan continuation and both originals stop at the coalition's retreat. It can't easily be verified, so it is reported here as it stands.
  • Is an anti-hegemony fable too easy? "Glory and Dreams" gives the Western power almost no interior: no convincing character on the other side, only an abstract might. Critics argue that this simplifies the politics. It indicts sanctions and dismemberment without letting the reader see the conflict from any other angle.
  • Women's bodies. All the women in the three stories, Karina, Laili and Xini, die, and each dies by handing her body over to something larger: a weapon, a routine, a race. Laili's years as a prostitute are mentioned in passing, as background. The long-standing criticism of Liu's female characters is especially concentrated here.
  • Techno-optimism. The closing diary of "Fire in the Earth" turns a city's destruction into a prologue to progress. One scholar notes that the story doesn't promise fossil fuels will suffice for the future, but its faith that technology will solve the problems technology creates goes unquestioned. In reality UCG's environmental risks are messier than the story admits.
  • The author's own statements. Remarks Liu has made in some interviews on political questions in China have drawn controversy in the West and shaped how some readers approach works like these, which deal with nations and war. This records the verifiable fact without passing judgment.

Crossovers

  • Game theory · Schelling on commitment: In The Strategy of Conflict (1960), Thomas Schelling showed that destroying some of your own options can win you leverage, like throwing the steering wheel out of the window in a game of chicken. Flood has the same shape: blind yourself first to shift the fight onto ground that suits you. The difference is that Schelling's commitment is a signal aimed at the opponent, while Flood's blindness is real.
  • Cultural history · Huizinga's Homo Ludens: Johan Huizinga (1938) argued that contest and war share a root as rule-bound "play" with winners and losers. The Peace Window pushes war back into contest. The difference is that Huizinga's play requires free entry and rules both sides honour, and West Asia never had a choice.
  • Sociology · Beck's risk society: Ulrich Beck (1986) argued that modern societies' gravest risks are produced by modernisation itself. Trying to abolish the dangers of the pit, Liu Xin creates a fire that consumes a city: the risk grows out of the solution. The difference is that Beck's risks are diffuse and hard to pin on anyone, while this fire has a name.
  • Ecology · shifting baseline syndrome: The fisheries scientist Daniel Pauly (1995) observed that each generation takes the conditions of its childhood as normal and can't see what was lost before. The schoolboy treating silicosis as a curiosity of antiquity is the same drift. The difference is that Pauly worried about forgetting losses, while the diary forgets a suffering, which may be exactly what Liu Xin wanted.
