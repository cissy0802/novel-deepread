Novels, Deep-Read · Leo Tolstoy · No.6

War and Peace

Voyna i mir · Leo Tolstoy · serialised from 1865 · completed 1869

中文 →

Winter, 1805, on the high ground outside the village of Austerlitz. A twenty-seven-year-old prince picks a dropped standard out of the churned snow and runs forward with it. The staff is heavier than he expected and the cloth drags behind him, and as he runs he can hear the battalion coming up behind — the moment he has waited his whole life for has finally arrived.
Then something strikes him on the head.
He falls on his back. The battlefield is gone. There is only sky: high, without end, grey-white, the clouds moving across it very slowly.
His first thought, lying there, is that he has lived this long without once looking at it.

The premise of this site is that everything worth discussing lives past the ending; without it you can only discuss atmosphere. If you want to keep the suspense, read the book first — this page will wait.

In one sentence

Four Russian noble families are put through the seven years of the Napoleonic wars: an illegitimate son suddenly inherits the largest fortune in Russia; a prince who cannot stand his own life goes to war twice looking for meaning and comes back twice with opposite answers; a girl of thirteen grows into the most alive person in the book and then nearly destroys herself. The war takes three of them. The survivors sit down at a dinner table in 1820 and start discussing whether to overthrow the government. And while telling all this, Tolstoy spends several hundred further pages arguing one thing: neither Napoleon nor Kutuzov ever commanded anything, and history is pushed forward inch by inch by millions of people who have no idea they are taking part in it.

Where it sits

Leo Tolstoy (1828–1910), a Russian count by inheritance, served as an artillery officer in the Crimean War and was at the siege of Sevastopol. That is the root of the fact that he never writes a battle from above — only what one man can hear and smell.

The book's origins tell you what it is. He first set out to write about a Decembrist — one of the noble officers whose 1825 rising failed — coming home from exile in 1856. Then he decided he had to explain why the man went to the square in 1825, then that he had to first show how the war of 1812 formed that generation, and he ended up all the way back in 1805. The book grew backwards, digging from consequence toward cause; its whole structure is the shape of its author's own regress. It began serialisation in 1865 under the title 1805 and was completed in 1869 as War and Peace.

It is the ceiling of nineteenth-century realist fiction, and also a novel that refuses to be a novel: in 1868 Tolstoy published a short piece declaring that it is not a novel, still less a poem, still less a historical chronicle. That is not modesty. He is refusing every ready-made contract of genre, because he wants to do two things at once that normally cannot be done together: render a group of lives down to the level of skin, and overturn the entire European method of writing history. The two halves are never sewn together — which is both the book's wound and its ambition.

The story

Act one · 1805: a soirée where Napoleon is discussed in French

The book opens at a Petersburg lady-in-waiting's evening party. A room full of Russian aristocrats converse in French, and the subject is the Corsican currently taking Europe apart — they denounce the enemy in the enemy's language and nobody finds it funny. Two people in the room don't fit. Pierre Bezukhov: large, short-sighted, the illegitimate son of the richest old count in Russia, with no surname and no standing, who genuinely admires Napoleon and says so out loud, killing the mood. And Prince Andrei Bolkonsky: handsome, clever, exhausted, indifferent to everything including his pregnant young wife Lise. He decides to go to the war, and his reason is plain: he cannot stand this life. Almost every character's first movement in this book is an attempt to get out of their own life.

Cut to the Rostovs in Moscow: extravagant, hopeless with money, gloriously alive. At the name-day party thirteen-year-old Natasha bursts into the drawing room clutching a doll, laughing and unstoppable. In the same city old Count Bezukhov is dying, and the relatives are physically wrestling over the portfolio with the will in it; Pierre does nothing at all, is pushed from room to room, and overnight becomes a count and the largest landowner in Russia. Then comes the coldest comedy in the book: Prince Kuragin marries his daughter Hélène to him. Pierre knows he does not love her, but with a roomful of people waiting he cannot say no; and when he finally produces the words je vous aime, they are squeezed out of him by the gravity of the drawing room. The book's first lesson about marriage is that nobody is really deciding at the moment they appear to decide.

The fourth family is the Bolkonskys, at their estate Bald Hills: an old prince, a general retired since Catherine's reign, who runs the day like clockwork and abuses anyone a minute late; and his daughter Marya, plain, heavy-footed, with astonishing eyes. Before Andrei leaves for the war she hangs a small blackened icon on a chain round his neck and begs him never to take it off. He laughs at her superstition, and agrees. Seed ①

Act two · The sky over Austerlitz

Of the 1805 campaign Tolstoy gives almost no overview. What you get is this: at the holding action at Schöngrabern a small unimpressive artillery captain named Tushin, boots not properly on, with no order given and no support sent and his superiors having simply forgotten him, holds off the French advance for half a day — and is then reprimanded at headquarters for losing his guns. The people who actually do the work on a battlefield tend not to appear in the report afterwards — that is the first demonstration of this book's theory of history. In the same action the young hussar Nikolai Rostov (Natasha's brother) is hit in the arm, his horse shot from under him, and lying on the ground he produces a thought so stupid it is heartbreaking: why would they want to kill me? Everybody likes me so much.

Then Austerlitz. The allied armies attack according to a plan nobody understood, get lost in fog, and are punched through the middle by Napoleon. Andrei has waited a long time for this: he wants a moment of his own — the standard, the charge, everyone turning to look at him. He picks up the flag, runs a few paces, and is struck on the head.

He lies on the high ground and sees the sky. Tolstoy does not write his pain; he writes only that the man has finally looked at the thing that has been over his head all his life. After a while riders stop beside him and a short one looks down and says something in French, to the effect of what a fine death. It is Napoleon — the idol he has worshipped half his life, now the size of a fly. French soldiers pull the little icon off his neck; Napoleon gives orders for it to be returned. Payoff ① ↑Here is why that is beautiful: when it was hung there it was the most negligible prop in the book, a devout sister's private consolation pressed on an unbelieving brother; and the man who takes it off him is the most powerful man in Europe, and the man who hands it back is the same man. Two kinds of greatness collide, and the loser is Napoleon.

Andrei does not die. He makes his way back to Bald Hills and walks in on the night his wife is in labour. The baby lives; Lise dies. Her dead face keeps an expression that Andrei reads as a question: what have you done to me? He went to war to escape her, and she has kept him for good by means of a question that can no longer be answered.

Act three · The peace years: an oak tree and a window

Pierre is going down. He discovers Hélène's affair with the handsome gambler-officer Dolokhov and calls him out; next morning a short-sighted fat man who has to ask how the trigger works puts his opponent on the ground and is untouched himself. He separates from Hélène, joins the Freemasons (a secret society organised around initiation rites, degrees and moral self-improvement), and resolves to free his serfs and build schools and hospitals. What follows is the least forgiving passage in the book: his steward walks him round the estates and shows him everything he wants to see — the schools are empty, the hospitals are for show, the remitted labour dues are collected again under another name. He fails not because of a villain but because goodwill passed down through five layers of people comes out the other end deformed.

Andrei, meanwhile. In the spring of 1809 he drives out to the Rostovs' country estate on business. There is an old oak by the road: everything else has come into leaf and it stands there in bare broken bark, stubbornly refusing. He looks at it and thinks: yes, exactly — spring, love, happiness, all of it is a trick played on children; I am thirty-one, my life is over. Seed ②

That night he stays at the Rostovs'. A window is open above him and two girls are talking. One voice cannot contain itself — the moon is too good, nobody could sleep on a night like this, and if only one could crouch down, take hold of one's knees and simply fly. The other is sleepy and keeps telling her to go to bed. Seed ③Andrei, below, feels something turn over in him for no reason he can name.

On the way back he looks for the oak and nearly fails to recognise it: covered in new leaf, shining in the evening light. Payoff ② ↑This is not coincidence; it is Tolstoy setting the same object on the same road twice with a single night in between. He refuses to let Andrei change by way of "working it out." He lets a tree grow its leaves while the man is away, so that on the return it is the man's turn to be startled. Turning points don't happen when a person makes a decision; they happen when he is hit by something that has nothing to do with him.

At the New Year ball of 1810, Pierre gives him a push and tells him to go and ask a girl nobody has invited, who is close to tears. He takes hold of her and the body is so light it might fly — it is the voice from the upstairs window. Payoff ③ ↑Tolstoy does not point it out; he lets Andrei recognise it himself. You hear someone's voice first and see her face a year later. They become engaged. The old prince sets a condition: they must wait a year, and Andrei must spend it abroad. That year is the most expensive item on the book's account.

Act four · Cardboard at the opera

A year with no fiancé and nothing to do runs long. Natasha is taken to the opera, and that evening she abruptly stops being able to read the convention: what is left on stage is painted board and a stout man with his mouth open making noises, and the hall is applauding. On precisely the night when every convention collapses into cardboard, Anatole Kuragin (Hélène's brother) is sitting in a box — handsome, hollow, neither malicious nor anything else, and the way he looks at her does not pretend. In a world that has just lost all its conventions, he is the only thing that looks real.

What follows happens very fast: Hélène sets it up, Anatole writes, Natasha writes to her fiancé's sister breaking the engagement and agrees to elope. Sonya — the poor cousin living with the Rostovs — finds out and tells, and the carriage is stopped. And Anatole has been married for some time already, in Poland; it is Pierre who tells her to her face. Natasha swallows arsenic (not much; she does not die) and is ill for a long time.

Pierre comes to see her. Weeping, she asks whether everything is finished for her. He stands up and delivers a stammering, ungrammatical speech: if I were not myself, if I were the handsomest, cleverest, best man on earth, and if I were free, I would go down on my knees this minute and ask for your hand. Then he runs out. It is the most moving declaration in the book precisely because the man making it has no standing to make it and no intention of gaining anything by it. In the sleigh he looks up and sees the great comet of 1811. All Petersburg is saying it portends war and the end of the world; he looks at it and feels only warmth — the thing corresponds to his own heart, which has just come back into flower. Seed ④

Act five · 1812: Borodino, and a city that burns itself

In June of the following year Napoleon's army crosses the Niemen. Andrei re-enlists, refuses a staff position and takes a regiment — he wants to be near people. He also has private business: he has sworn to find Anatole and call him out. Seed ⑤He knows the impulse is not creditable, and he cannot give it up.

Bald Hills is on the line of the advance. The old prince has a stroke, and on his deathbed says a few tender things to the daughter he has been cruel to for a lifetime, and dies — the love Marya waited her whole life for lasted about a minute. She is then trapped on the estate: the peasants, worked on from outside, will not let her take the grain or the carts. It is the one moment in the book when the gentry are made to see that the ground under them is rented. Nikolai, requisitioning nearby, rides into the yard, disperses the crowd with a few shouted sentences, and looks up at the woman on the steps with the shining eyes — two people recognising each other in entirely the wrong circumstances.

26 August, Borodino. Tolstoy hands the decisive battle of the war to the person least qualified to understand it: Pierre, a civilian in a white hat on a horse who knows nothing and has come to "have a look." He climbs onto the Raevsky redoubt, where the soldiers find the fat gentleman entertaining and call him "our master"; then the shell fragments start coming down, and the men who were joking with him a moment ago are one by one not there, and he is knocked over by a blast and runs back with another man's blood on his face. He never works out who won. Nobody in the book works out who won, including the two commanders on their hills looking at smoke through telescopes.

Andrei's regiment is held in reserve, forbidden to move all day, standing while the artillery calls out its names one at a time. A shell lands smoking at his feet; his adjutant drops flat, he does not. Afterwards he is carried into the field hospital — and from the stretcher he sees, on the next table, a leg being sawn off and a beautiful man crying like a child. It is Anatole. Payoff ⑤ ↑The man he has hunted for six months meaning to kill with his own hands is being amputated a metre away. Andrei thinks of the ball, of Natasha, of his own creditable hatred — and weeps, for the other man. Tolstoy arranges neither revenge nor forgiveness; he simply puts your enemy next to you and has him sawn as well. You can only stand and hate someone while there is distance in between. Remove the distance and hatred has nowhere to put its feet.

Kutuzov holds his council of war at Fili outside Moscow, listens to everyone's opinions and closes the meeting with one sentence: abandon Moscow. Tolstoy insists this is not a decision but an admission. The Rostovs load their carts at the last moment, heaped with carpets, china, picture frames; Natasha sees the wounded being carried into the yard and makes a scene until her parents agree to unload every cart and fill them with men. A family that has been extravagant its whole life is extravagant one last time, and it saves several dozen lives. On one of those carts lies a man she does not yet know is there: Andrei.

Moscow empties, and then burns. Tolstoy's explanation is blunt to the point of provocation: a city built of wood, its inhabitants all gone, occupied by an army with no discipline — it would be strange if it didn't burn. There are no arsonists, only physics. And the comet that all Petersburg had read as an omen of the end portended nothing at all: Payoff ④ ↑the catastrophe arrived on schedule and had no connection to the object in the sky, which on the night it appeared illuminated exactly one thing — a fat man's heart coming back to life. People always find a sufficiently grand cause for a catastrophe after the fact, when the real causes are small and scattered and not one of them is fit to be put in the sky.

Pierre stays behind. Playing a Masonic numbers game he makes his own name come to 666, the same as "l'Empereur Napoléon," and concludes he is fated to end the man. Seed ⑥He walks through the burning city with a dagger. The result: he never sees Napoleon; he carries a screaming little girl out of a burning house, then fights a French soldier who is robbing a woman, and is arrested as a suspected incendiary. Payoff ⑥ ↑It is the book's most economical refutation of the great-man theory: even a man who believes he has been named by heaven finds that all he can actually do inside real history is carry one child out of a fire. And Tolstoy's judgement is unambiguous — carrying out the child mattered more than the assassination would have.

What follows the arrest is the coldest scene in the book: he is marched out to watch "incendiaries" being executed. The soldiers shoot them in pairs, mechanically, and the men doing the shooting are more frightened than the men being shot. Pierre is sixth in line, and it stops before they reach him — he was never on the list, nobody ever intended to kill him, he was brought out to watch. What collapses in that moment is his cosmology: he had always believed the world was ordered, and order has just processed human beings in front of him like a machine, with nobody in charge of it. In the prisoners' shed he meets Platon Karataev — a round peasant soldier, conscripted in place of a brother with many children after being caught taking someone else's timber, who speaks entirely in proverbs and carries a needle and thread to sew for people. He does not argue and he does not console; he simply treats everything as being the way it was always going to be. And Pierre, among the lice and the frostbitten feet, finds the most complete happiness of his life. He laughs out loud: they have shut me up? Shut whom up? Me? My immortal soul?

Act six · Who comes back and who doesn't

Andrei is dying at Yaroslavl, and Natasha finds him. There is no explanation between them and no ritual of forgiveness — only her kneeling there and his hand on her head. He sinks day by day, not weakening so much as receding: he dreams he is holding a door shut against something pressing in, and knows that the thing is death. The door opens. He wakes, and from then on has no further interest in the living, including her. Tolstoy refuses to grant a well-made deathbed farewell: real dying is not tenderness, it is gradually not needing anyone. In those weeks Marya and Natasha turn from two women who could not stand each other into sisters for life.

That same autumn the Rostovs' youngest son Petya rides with a partisan detachment. He is sixteen, too excited to sleep, and gives away his raisins to a captured French drummer boy. Next morning in the charge he is first into the yard, first hit, and dead on the spot. He dies without a line of dialogue and without meaning anything — Tolstoy insists on this, because he had seen it. It is that same raid that scatters the escort and frees Pierre; and a few days before it, Karataev, unable to walk further, sat down under a birch and looked at him, and he avoided the eyes and walked past, and there was a shot behind him, and he did not turn round. Everything this book has to say about survival is in that refusal to turn round.

The rest is settling accounts. Hélène dies that year — society calls it a sudden illness, and the novel's tone is caustic and vague, implying something to do with a drug she took to get out of her situation. Less than a tenth of Napoleon's army gets home, destroyed not by battle but by distance, winter and its own supply line. Kutuzov weeps when he learns the French have crossed the border, is relieved of command shortly afterwards and dies soon after — used and discarded, which is the last stroke Tolstoy adds to his own theory of history. In 1813 Pierre and Natasha marry; not long after, Nikolai marries Marya.

The epilogue jumps to Bald Hills in 1820. Natasha is twenty-eight, heavier, no longer singing, absorbed all day in four children and nappies, interested in nothing but her husband's movements — jealous, complaining, happy. Pierre is back from Petersburg full of a secret society taking shape, and the reader knows what it is: the Decembrist rising five years off, which is to say the thing Tolstoy originally sat down to write before retreating all the way to 1805. The book has come round a very large circle to its own starting point. In the corner sits Andrei's son Nikolenka, fifteen, listening in silence and thinking about his father, about glory, about one day doing something that would have satisfied him — and you have just spent a thousand pages learning where that thought leads. The last several dozen pages contain no characters at all: only Tolstoy, in one long essay on free will and necessity.

Which brings up something counter-intuitive: this book refuses to have a fuse. Other novels can point to one act and say that everything afterwards is its aftershock. War and Peace was built so that you cannot find that act. Who started this war? Tolstoy calls each candidate up in turn, inspects him, and takes him apart, until all you are left holding is several million people each busy with something small. And he plants the same structure in the private lives as evidence: Pierre never decided to marry Hélène, Natasha never decided to elope, Andrei never decided to fall in love with her — in every case a person only discovers afterwards that they were already on the road. The book dismantles the concept of the fuse itself, and then asks you to go on being a responsible person in a world that doesn't have one.

Two scenes in slow motion

One · The opera: take the world apart into cardboard, then let someone fall through

Natasha's pages at the opera are the most modern-feeling writing in the book. Technically they do something very few writers dare: they render a scene that ought to be lyrical as a cold physical description — no music, no drama, only painted boards, bare arms, open mouths, sounds produced on the beat, and a room full of people applauding it.

This is the device the Formalist critics later named defamiliarisation (Russian ostranenie: stripping a familiar thing of its name and its conventions and describing only what it actually is, forcing the reader to look again) — and the concept was in fact derived from Tolstoy's own text. The sky at Austerlitz is the same machine: Napoleon is put into a scale that doesn't suit him, and the verdict comes not from the author but from the framing.

The point is that the scene isn't only satire; it is the engine of Natasha's fall. A girl raised to believe in a set of conventions — the engagement, propriety, the year of waiting — sees for the first time that the back of every convention is painted board. If the world is fake, then whoever looks least fake wins, and Anatole's entire talent is that his appetite doesn't pretend. Tolstoy doesn't make her fall because she is bad; he makes her fall because for one moment she saw too clearly, which is far harder to argue with than any sermon.

Two · Borodino: put the man who understands least at the centre

This battle has generated an enormous literature of dispatches, maps and reconstructions. Tolstoy's solution: no map at all. He ties the reader to a short-sighted fat man in a white hat and lets him wander the position seeing only fragments — morning light, white smoke, someone handing him water, someone calling him master, a man who was talking a second ago suddenly not there.

And at the other end of the same scene he gives Napoleon exactly the same treatment: on the hill, the telescope shows only smoke; an order goes out, and by the time the aide reaches the line the situation is no longer the one the order was written for. The two viewpoints are deliberately made equal in information — the commander of all Europe and a civilian who understands nothing comprehend this day to precisely the same degree. It is the quietest proof of the book's theory of history: the argument isn't in the essays, it's in the framing.

The core device: a machine for dismantling great men

In literary fiction the "worldbuilding" isn't in the world, it's in the structure. This machine has three levels.

Level one: the great-man theory is an optical illusion. The great man theory — that history is driven by the will of a few men of genius — was common sense in the nineteenth century. Tolstoy's refutation is not moral but mechanical: between an order and an outcome lie dozens of relays, tens of thousands of executors, hundreds of thousands of people who don't execute it. If an order happens to coincide with what several hundred thousand people were going to do anyway, it is "carried out," and the historian credits the result to whoever signed it. He has a cruel image for this: a child sitting inside a carriage holding the straps and convinced he is driving.

Level two: so what does move history? Differentials. His positive answer borrows from mathematics: to understand a movement you must cut it into infinitesimally small units — not "the Russian army retreated," but whether each individual soldier's next step was forward or back — and then sum those infinitesimal wills (he uses the word integration outright). That has real weight in intellectual history: it changes the subject of the sentence from who to how many.

Level three: Kutuzov's "gift" is knowing he is powerless. The Kutuzov of the novel makes no resolutions, invents no tactics and dozes through meetings; his entire ability is patience and time. He knows the French rot a little more for every extra day in Moscow, and that any active measure would only slow down what is going to happen anyway. It is the book's most contrarian definition of leadership: the highest competence is not intervention but accurate judgement about what must not be interfered with.

Which parts hold and which are literary necessity is worth separating:

  • What holds: the demolition of "one man's will decides everything" is now more or less standard historiography — institutions, supply, climate, disease and communication lag have all been shown repeatedly to govern campaigns. His account of battlefield confusion is corroborated by countless first-hand testimonies.
  • What overshoots: he pushes it to "commanders have no effect at all," which is stronger than the evidence allows. Decisions at the level of a battle plainly do matter — Austerlitz really was won by Napoleon. To keep the argument elegant he turns Kutuzov into a nearly saintly quietist and Napoleon into a comic narcissist. He objects to explaining history through personalities, and refutes the personality theory of history by means of characterisation — which is itself a hole.
  • What is philosophy rather than proof: the free-will argument in the second epilogue — the more causes of an act we know, the less free it looks; the sensation of freedom is real, the freedom is not — is a position, not a conclusion. An analogy is not an argument.

Worth naming plainly: the most effective part of this machine is not the essays but the framing rule of the novel itself. There is not one shot of a battlefield from above; every large event happens at the edge of some particular person's field of vision. You need not agree with Tolstoy's philosophy of history — by the end of the book it has worked on you anyway, because for a thousand pages you were never allowed to stand anywhere high.

What it forces you to think about

Question one
If history really has no subject, what is left of doing your work seriously today?

Take Tolstoy's side to its strongest first. He is not recommending passivity; he is dismantling a very expensive illusion — that if you find the right man and stand in the right place, you can turn the world. That illusion produced Napoleon, and it produces every person who believes he is bending the course of events while actually spending several hundred thousand lives. His alternative is not nihilism but returning responsibility to its real scale: you cannot affect the war, but you certainly affected the five or six people near you. Pierre could not kill Napoleon, but he carried out a child. If history is made of infinitesimal units, then your unit is real — it is only small.

Now the other side at full strength. The theory has a very convenient side effect: it absolves everyone in high office. If Napoleon was only a leaf on the current, he need not answer for several million deaths. But particular people at particular forks really did choose, and the choices really did have consequences — and Tolstoy himself, writing Anna or Ivan Ilyich, never accepts "he couldn't help it" as a defence. A moral standard that is generous applied to history and severe applied to individuals is at minimum inconsistent.

Bring it down to the everyday: inside a large company, an industry, a social upheaval, "one person can't change anything" is both the most accurate observation available and the most usable excuse. There is only one crude way to tell them apart: watch what you do after you say it.

Question two
Karataev's acceptance: the highest wisdom, or a sedative handed to the unlucky?

Pierre has tried everything — a colossal inheritance, a society marriage, Freemasonry, philanthropy, assassination — and failed at all of it; and he finally becomes happy among lice, foul soup and the daily possibility of being shot. His teacher is an illiterate peasant whose entire doctrine is: things are the way they are, get on with your sewing. There is something very hard in this: a large share of the pain a situation causes comes from the thought that it should not be so, rather than from the situation. What Pierre learns is not submission but how to close the permanent gap between the world he wants and the world that is occurring — and after that, circumstance never breaks him again.

The other side is just as hard, and not easy to dodge. Karataev has no self — Tolstoy says so explicitly: taken separately he has no meaning, only as part of a whole. That is precisely the psychological apparatus that kept serfdom running smoothly: don't ask why, your suffering is round and proper. And who wrote this philosophy down? A count who owned a great deal of land and several hundred serfs. He has aestheticised into wisdom the powerlessness of people he owned. The settlement is what stings most: Karataev, unable to walk, is shot where he sits, while Pierre takes the insight home to his millions of roubles — the same course, with one student paying in his life and the other in a few months' bad food.

This argument happens somewhere every day: "don't be so attached," "accept what you can't change." The statements are true in themselves, but who is saying them and to whom changes what they are. The same piece of wisdom is spiritual practice when addressed inward and management when addressed downward.

Question three
The Natasha of the epilogue: a happy landing, or a person written out of existence?

Take Tolstoy's side first, better than his defenders do. Almost every novel stops at the wedding, because life after the wedding doesn't photograph well: putting on weight, no longer singing, watching your husband's every movement. Tolstoy declines to stop, and declines to add a syllable of regret. His claim is that the girl at the window who wanted to fly and this woman who smells of milk and loses her temper when her husband is late are the same energy, redirected. That readers feel let down only proves they are still inside the aesthetic prejudice that a girl is more worth writing than a mother — which is the prejudice he is attacking.

Now the other side at full strength. The problem is not her choice but the narrator's vocabulary. Tolstoy describes her in the language used of a female animal — strong, full, fertile — and has a character define Sonya, who gave everything for this family, as a "sterile flower." On that scale a woman's value is measured in fertility: the ones who bear are complete and the ones who don't are empty. Meanwhile what Pierre gets in the epilogue is Petersburg, secret societies, ideas; what Natasha gets is four children and jealousy. Both have "settled," but only one of them still has a world that is getting larger. You can argue that he is faithfully recording the nineteenth century — but he is not recording, he is admiring; the tone of those pages is deeply satisfied.

Brought forward: settling down is not right or wrong in itself; the question is how the bill is split. When two people settle at the same moment and one world keeps expanding while the other contracts to a single room, that is not two people making choices — that is one person's choice costing more. Tolstoy writes that account with total clarity. He simply doesn't appear to know that he has.

The subject Tolstoy keeps returning to

Line his books up and the same obsession keeps coming back: people are almost incapable of honesty while alive, and become capable of it only when close to death.

Andrei has to lie on the ground twice — Austerlitz and Borodino — to see what he cannot see upright. In The Death of Ivan Ilyich, a judge who has been respectable his entire life discovers in his last days that the whole thing was wrong, and that the only person good to him is an illiterate servant. In Anna Karenina, Levin gets closer to what he wants in a day of mowing and at his brother's deathbed than in a shelf of philosophy. His protagonists never choose between good and evil; they choose between the false and the real, and the false side is always more comfortable, more respectable and better organised.

Attached to this is his fixation on peasants: Karataev, the servant at Ivan Ilyich's bedside, the mowers in Levin's field — all illiterate, none of them discussing anything, all of them written as carriers of the truth. It is his most affecting string and his least defensible position: handing the answer to a class of people who are not permitted to speak is itself a way of keeping them from speaking. That he gave up his copyrights late in life, wanted to divide his land, and finally left home at eighty-two to die at a small railway station reads less like a conversion than like a man finally trying to live by the logic he had already written down.

Criticism and controversy

  • The two halves don't join. The commonest and strongest criticism is that the novel is a masterpiece and the essays are ballast. Turgenev admired the book but would not accept its philosophy of history; Flaubert, reading it in French, wrote to Turgenev that the early parts were Shakespearean and then complained that the author starts repeating himself and philosophising. Henry James later applied "large loose baggy monsters" to novels of this kind, and the label has stuck. In fairness: those essays really are heavy, and without them the book is only a very good family chronicle.
  • Disputes about the history. Veterans of 1812 and professional historians published rebuttals of his details at the time. His Kutuzov is raised into a nearly saintly passive sage and his Napoleon flattened into a comic narcissist, and both go beyond what the sources support.
  • The women. The near-biological description of Natasha in the epilogue, the definition of Sonya as a barren flower, and the fact that the worst-behaved women in the book all use sexual attraction as their weapon — this configuration drew protests in the nineteenth century and reads worse now. The defence is that Tolstoy is equally merciless about male vanity. That is true, and it does not settle the matter.
  • Where are the serfs? The book is about a nation's survival, and the overwhelming majority of that nation — the serfs — have almost no interior life in it. The one peasant of any weight is Karataev, and he is a symbol rather than a person; in the disturbance at Bogucharovo the peasants are a mob stirred up by drink and rumour who disperse when shouted at. A book arguing that history is driven by ordinary people gives no face to the most ordinary people in it.
  • The author's own repudiation. Late in life, in What Is Art?, Tolstoy classed his own long novels, War and Peace and Anna Karenina among them, as bad art. This was not modesty but the necessary conclusion of his later religious position.
  • Editions and translation. A great deal of the dialogue originally carries its French (which is content, not decoration: who speaks which language in which room is a social coordinate). For the 1873 edition Tolstoy translated the French into Russian and pulled out most of the historical argument, then restored it later; there is also a so-called "original version" assembled from early manuscripts. English translations diverge as well — some keep the French with notes, some render everything into English. Strictly speaking, the War and Peace in two different editions is not quite the same book.

Ten lines that stay

① The ambition isn't to narrate a war; it's to prove nobody commanded it — and the evidence offered is a thousand pages of ordinary life.

② Andrei went to war to escape his own life, and ended up on his back seeing the sky for the first time: every turning point here falls outside somebody's plan.

③ An oak refuses spring and is then covered in leaf — the book's best refutation of "working things out." People aren't reasoned awake, they're knocked awake.

④ Natasha doesn't fall because she is bad; she falls because for one evening she saw the world as painted board, and only appetite still looked real.

⑤ Pierre calculates that he is fated to kill Napoleon and ends up carrying a child out of a fire. Tolstoy thinks the second mattered more, and he is right.

⑥ The man Andrei hunted for six months ends up on the next table having a leg sawn off: remove the distance and hatred has nowhere to stand.

⑦ Karataev's composure is worth learning — while remembering who paid for that lesson with his life and who paid with a few months of bad food.

⑧ Kutuzov's talent is knowing when to do nothing: the book's most untimely and most durable definition of leadership.

⑨ The epilogue is not a failure of nerve, it is the bravest part of the book; what is uncomfortable is the half it admires, not the half it records.

⑩ The last image isn't a battlefield. It's a fifteen-year-old boy in the corner, planning to do something one day that would have satisfied his father — and you have just read a thousand pages about where that thought goes.
