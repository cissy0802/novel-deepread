Novels, Deep-Read · Chen Zhongshi · No.34

White Deer Plain

Bailuyuan · Chen Zhongshi · 1993

中文 →

Heavy snow has shut the whole tableland down.
Walking home from his father-in-law's, Bai Jiaxuan crosses the useless strip of slope that belongs to Lu Zilin and sees a patch where the snow has melted through, uncovering a plant.
The leaves are green. The shape is a deer: haunch, four legs, a lifted head.
A gust comes across and the thing sways once, as if about to run.
He crouches there a long time, stands, measures the slope with his eyes, and walks home without saying a word to anyone.
What he will do next is trade his best irrigated field for that worthless slope, throwing in cash to sweeten it — and then, on a moonless night, move his father's grave up onto it.

This page contains complete spoilers—including how that field was acquired, whose hand kills Tian Xiao'e, why Bai Jiaxuan builds a brick tower on top of a woman, who puts Bai Ling in the pit, and why Hei Wa is executed after he has genuinely reformed. Everything worth discussing here lies past the ending: if you don't know how the final account is settled, "the village of White Deer, village of righteousness" is just a plaque. If you want the suspense, read the novel first.

In One Sentence

In a village on the Guanzhong plain outside Xi'an, two clans descended from one ancestor — Bai and Lu — spend generations grinding against each other over an invisible ranking; from the fall of the Qing through to the years after 1949 the village is ploughed over in turn by warlords, famine, plague, the Nationalist–Communist split and a Communist purge. The patriarch Bai Jiaxuan holds up the village's name for righteousness with a strip of land he obtained by deception and a Village Covenant carved into stone, while keeping a runaway woman, Tian Xiao'e, permanently outside the ancestral hall; his rival Lu Zilin turns that excluded woman into a blade and destroys Bai Jiaxuan's eldest son with it; the family's lifelong hired hand kills his own daughter-in-law with a spear, and after the plague Bai Jiaxuan burns her bones and builds a tower over them. The children of both houses die variously in front of Nationalist guns, in a pit dug by their own comrades, and in the churn between bandits and the militia — and the man who survives into the new state is the worst human being in the book, who becomes county magistrate and whose first major act is to have the reformed Hei Wa shot. At the end Bai Jiaxuan, bent over a spine somebody broke for him, goes to see his ruined old enemy and confesses the one shameful thing he did in his life.

Coordinates

Chen Zhongshi (1942–2016) was born in a village outside Xi'an, in Shaanxi province, to a farming family; he taught in a village school and worked as a commune cadre. He was in his mid-forties before he started this book, and he began by reading his way through the local gazetteers of several counties: he started writing in 1988, finished in 1992, and published in 1993. He said he wanted to write a book he could use as a pillow in his coffin — not a flourish, but an ultimatum from a man who suspected he had spent his first forty years writing filler.

The year it came out, several big Shaanxi novels arrived in Beijing at once, an event the press called "the Shaanxi army marches east." In 1997 it won the Mao Dun Literature Prize, but the prize came with conditions: the author made revisions along the lines the judges wanted, cutting back some of the sexual material and some of Master Zhu's political remarks — and the revised text is the one most widely sold today.

The epigraph is a line from Balzac:

"The novel is the secret history of a nation."—epigraph to White Deer Plain, quoting Balzac

That epigraph puts the ambition on the table before page one: official history writes provinces and states, this book writes one village, and it is betting that the village keeps the truer ledger. Its slot in literary history: the heaviest and hardest of the modern Chinese novels that treat a family chronicle as a national one — kin to Dream of the Red Chamber in the way it watches a house come apart, and to the county gazetteer in the way it keeps its records. The soil is loess, not the Caribbean.

The Story

Act One · Seven wives and a plant shaped like a deer

The novel's first sentence fixes the register for everything that follows:

"What Bai Jiaxuan later took pride in was that in one lifetime he married seven wives."—White Deer Plain, opening of chapter one

Not seven loves — seven wives, counted like sacks in a barn. The first six die one after another and the village starts saying his fate is too hard for a woman to survive. His mother's view of the matter is the book's first quiet blow to the head:

"A woman is no more than the paper pasted over a window: when it tears, you peel it off and paste on a new sheet."—White Deer Plain, Bai Jiaxuan's mother

That line is not given to a villain. It is spoken by a kindly old woman at her own stove — the coldest things in this book are never said by bad people. The seventh wife is Xiancao, from a family on the southern plain; on the day she arrives the whole village is waiting to see how many months she lasts. She lives, and bears three sons — Xiaowen, Xiaowu, Xiaoyi — and a daughter, Bai Ling, who from the beginning refuses to grow in the shape provided.

The Bai house has land and a seat in the ancestral hall; the Lu house has cash and the itch to climb — its founder was a boy who fled famine, learned to cook, made money elsewhere and came back to buy fields, leaving instructions that some descendant of his was to become an official. The two surnames come from one root and have grown into two ways of living: the Bais hold, the Lus push.

Then the plant in the snow. He tells nobody. He invents a reason to trade his good irrigated field, with cash on top, for the worthless slope Lu Zilin owns, and moves his father's grave onto it at night. SEED ① Lu Zilin thinks at first that he has robbed a fool; by the time he works out what happened, the ditch between the two houses has stopped being a matter of surnames and become an account. This is the first brick in the wall: the foundation of the village's righteousness is a deception nobody was told about.

His fortune has a second source: he is the first man on the plain to plant opium poppy at scale. SEED ③ White flowers over the slope, silver dollars carried home by the sackful, the whole plain copying him. He plants that crop during the same years he is turning his household into the moral exhibit of the district — the two projects run side by side and nobody finds it awkward.

The Qing falls somewhere beyond the mountains. When word reaches the plain, what people want to know is whether the queue still has to be worn and whether the grain still has to be handed over. Passing armies, requisitions and inventive new levies follow. The plain is never quiet again.

Act Two · The Covenant: the year a stone goes up

Bai Jiaxuan's brother-in-law is Master Zhu, head of the White Deer Academy and the acknowledged Confucian scholar of the region. His entrance is the most legendary passage in the book: a Qing provincial governor is coming across with two hundred thousand men in retreat, and Master Zhu rides a donkey into the camp, argues through the night, and in the morning the army turns around. A scholar talks two hundred thousand soldiers into leaving — the novel does not explain how this is possible, because this is simply how people on the plain tell the story.

Then the poppy ban. Master Zhu yokes an ox and ploughs up the crop himself, and the first field he destroys belongs to his brother-in-law. Bai Jiaxuan neither stops him nor says anything; he ploughs the rest of his own under. Master Zhu gives him a lesson with an ox, and by that evening he has converted the lesson into village law — after which nobody on the plain grows poppy, until many years later his own eldest son is lying on a brick bed with a pipe.

Master Zhu then writes a Village Covenant. Bai Jiaxuan has it cut into a stone tablet, stands it in the ancestral hall, and makes every male in the village recite it: honour your parents, keep peace with your brothers and neighbours, no gambling, no idleness, no theft — break a clause and you are punished in the hall in front of everyone. SEED ④ The county sends a plaque naming the place a village of righteousness. Those are the best years the plain ever has: the gamblers give it up, the thieves change trades, and Bai Jiaxuan's back is straight as a post, shoulders level when he walks. SEED ②

But there is one thing the Covenant does not contain: it governs what people do, never why they suffer. The door of the hall is a sluice gate — those who may pass through it are members of this village, and those who may not do not even qualify to be punished.

Act Three · Tian Xiao'e: the cave outside the door

Lu San has been the Bai family's hired hand for half a lifetime; Bai Jiaxuan says he has never thought of him as a servant, which is half true, and the two men are as close as kin. His son Hei Wa will not be managed: Bai Jiaxuan pays for his schooling and he runs off after a few days. He dislikes the sweetness in that house, and he dislikes the straightness. He says something later that turns out to be lethal: that ramrod back of Bai Jiaxuan's frightened him from childhood.

Hei Wa goes north of the Wei river to work for a rich old provincial graduate named Guo, and there he collides with Guo's concubine, Tian Xiao'e. She is a scholar's daughter whose father sold her into that household, where beyond the daily humiliation she is required to soften dates inside her own body overnight so the old man can eat them in the morning as a tonic. This is the most disgusting and most necessary detail in the novel: it establishes in a single stroke what this woman is inside that order — not a person, but a processing stage in the preparation of a medicine. The two of them are found out and thrown out together.

He brings her home to White Deer Plain. Bai Jiaxuan's ruling is one sentence: these two do not enter the ancestral hall. SEED ⑤ Not being able to bow to the ancestors means the marriage does not exist, so they set up house in an abandoned cave dwelling outside the village. Lu San disowns his son on the spot; his reason is that he cannot afford the shame. The whole village agrees with the ruling, including the men who walk out at night to listen at the cave wall.

Then comes the revolution of the mid-1920s. Lu Zilin's elder son, Lu Zhaopeng, has joined the Communist Party while away, and returns to organize a peasant association; Hei Wa, with nothing to lose and a stomach full of grievance, is pushed forward as its chairman. Their first great act is called Wind Stirring the Snow: denouncing the local gentry, parading them, burning land deeds, and finally storming the ancestral hall and smashing the Covenant tablet to gravel. PAYOFF ④ ↑ Bai Jiaxuan stands in the broken stone and refuses to kneel; they push his head down and he still will not. And Tian Xiao'e, the woman not permitted through the door, walks into the hall that day surrounded by a crowd — the first thing the revolution gives her is a threshold.

The revolution lasts a few months. The alliance breaks, the reprisals arrive, the association's members are arrested or killed. Hei Wa runs, and goes bandit. He leaves behind, alone on the plain, a woman who has just acquired a threshold.

Act Four · A spear, a plague, a tower

With Hei Wa gone, Tian Xiao'e becomes the easiest thing on the plain to take. Lu Zilin comes to the cave, first promising to find out where Hei Wa is, then taking her more or less as a matter of course. What he wants is not her but a serviceable weapon — and the weapon is aimed at the place where Bai Jiaxuan is most tender: the heir to the clan headship, his eldest son Bai Xiaowen.

Bai Xiaowen is the Covenant's finished product: correct, obedient, humourless, ashamed even of wanting his own wife. Tian Xiao'e is ordered to work on him, tries several times, and succeeds; Lu Zilin arranges the timing of the discovery and the audience for it. In the ancestral hall, in front of the entire village, Bai Jiaxuan beats his eldest son with a water-soaked spiked brush, a layer of skin at a stroke. Then the household is divided and the son is expelled. He is not beating a son. He is beating his own plaque.

From that day Bai Xiaowen rots: sells his land (the buyer is Lu Zilin), takes up opium, lets his wife starve to death on the brick bed, and ends up at a famine soup station fighting dogs for a bowl of gruel. PAYOFF ③ ↑ The white flowers his father planted on that slope twenty years earlier come back a generation later, in the son's lungs.

Tian Xiao'e takes no pleasure in any of it. She admits to Bai Xiaowen that she did this to him; and once she works out exactly how Lu Zilin has been using her, she goes to his house and, catching him off guard, urinates in his face. It is the only time in the whole novel that a humiliated person strikes back, and she does it with the one instrument she has.

The man who kills her is Lu San. This lifelong model of probity, who would not take an extra stick of firewood from his employer, walks into the cave at night and drives a spear into her back. She turns her head and gets out one syllable — Da, the word for "father" in that country. The identity she claims as she dies is exactly the one the village would never give her: daughter-in-law.

Then the plague comes. People go down in swathes; Bai Jiaxuan's wife Xiancao dies in it. Lu San loses his mind and begins speaking in Tian Xiao'e's voice, weeping, asking whom she ever harmed in this village. The village raises money to build her a shrine and propitiate her; Bai Jiaxuan forbids it. He has the cave opened, her bones burned, and a six-sided brick tower raised over the spot to hold her down forever. PAYOFF ⑤ ↑ The threshold story completes its three steps here — kept outside the door, carried inside by the revolution, and finally nailed under the ground outside the door by a tower. And the man who gives the order has reasons that are complete, respectable and endorsed by everyone. This novel never once shows a bad man becoming clan head. It shows what a good man looks like after he has enforced the rules all the way to the end.

Act Five · The children: a coin decides a side

While the old men are still fighting over a field, the young have been led away by something else.

Bai Ling is Bai Jiaxuan's only daughter: unbound feet, a runaway engagement, school in the city, a betrothal she overturns to her family's face. She joins the Communist Party and works underground in Xi'an. Her father's response is to announce that the household no longer has this daughter — he holds a spoken funeral for a living woman.

Lu Zilin's two sons take the two roads of the era: the elder, Lu Zhaopeng, is a Communist; the younger, Lu Zhaohai, a Nationalist officer. Lu Zhaohai and Bai Ling were in love. They were young, serious, and convinced that doctrine ought not to separate people, so they did something very much of their moment: they tossed a copper coin to decide which of them joined which party. SEED ⑥ When it landed they laughed and called it fate.

They discover later that fate does not handle the enforcement. Bai Ling really does become a Communist and Lu Zhaohai really does stay with the Nationalist army; in the same city they drift until meeting at all is dangerous, and Bai Ling ends up with Lu Zhaopeng — the brother, the superior — first as a false married couple and then as a real one. And Lu Zhaohai dies at the front: the notices and the memorial say he was killed fighting the Japanese, when in fact he died in northern Shaanxi, in an action against Communist forces. PAYOFF ⑥ ↑ Thrown in the air the coin was a game; where it landed decided, more than a decade later, which side a man died on and whether his death could be called by its real name.

Lu Zhaopeng's other debt is at home. His father forced him to marry Leng Qiuyue, the daughter of the plain's best physician; he never touches her and is away for years. The young woman goes mad inside a courtyard where she is not permitted to speak, and begins shouting in public everything one does not say. Her own father mixes her a drug that takes her voice, and then watches her die. A father silences his daughter to protect her reputation: no revolution here, no war, no bandits — the purest murder in the book.

Bai Ling's end is the cruellest. She survives the enemy's prison and the assassins in the city, and dies at the hands of her own side: a purge sweeps the base area in the north, she is taken for a spy, and she is buried alive in a pit. The novel gives her no trial, no defence, no stated charge — because the real people did not get those either. The rehabilitation and the posthumous honours come many years later and have nothing to do with her.

Act Six · Settling up: the reformed man is shot, the rotten one becomes magistrate

Hei Wa spends years in the hills as a bandit. One year he sends men down to the plain, into the Bai courtyard, and a wooden club breaks Bai Jiaxuan's spine. PAYOFF ② ↑ The reason is not a feud, not money, not land: it is that the man's back had been too straight all his life, and it had frightened him since he was a boy. This is the bloodiest piece of rhyming in the novel — that straight back is the physical object in which Bai Jiaxuan's whole character is stored, and the author has a stick bend it into a hump. The motive is almost transparent: the feeling of being oppressed does not always come from what the oppressor does. Sometimes it comes from how straight he stands.

But Hei Wa's second half turns. He is brought in from the hills as a militia officer, and then does what nobody expects: goes to the White Deer Academy, kneels, takes Master Zhu as his teacher, learns to read and write from the beginning, marries a respectable young woman named Gao Yufeng, and conducts himself by the book. Master Zhu's assessment is that he is the best student of that whole cohort. He even returns to the plain to make offerings to the ancestors — in the hall whose Covenant tablet he smashed with his own hands, with the hunched Bai Jiaxuan presiding. The door opens, and the man kneeling through it is the man who broke it.

Meanwhile Bai Xiaowen climbs back from the bottom. He gets off opium, joins the militia, rises to battalion and regimental command, comes home in style and makes his own offerings in the hall. He has learned exactly one thing, and he says it himself: face, once you have lost it, is never worth anything again — after which nothing he does requires hesitation. On the eve of 1949 he and Hei Wa both take part in a defection to the new side, and on the ledger of credit he assigns the larger share to himself.

The new state is founded and Bai Xiaowen becomes magistrate of Zishui County. One of the cases he handles on taking office is to classify Hei Wa as a counter-revolutionary, hold a public trial, and have him shot. The man who reformed is executed; the man who never reformed announces the execution from the platform. Master Zhu is already dead by then — in his last years he gathered a few old scholars and compiled the county gazetteer, entering the events of this plain one line at a time, and asked to be buried plainly. His grave is dug open many years later. There is no silver in it, only a brick with a sentence cut into it, asking in effect: how much longer is all this thrashing about going to go on?

Lu Zilin collapses too. A lifetime of manoeuvring, and the land and the standing go back out the way they came; he ends in prison, and comes out with his mind gone, recognizing nobody, and dies on the plain in winter. Bai Jiaxuan, bent over his broken back, goes to see the man he fought his whole life; and to someone who can no longer understand a word, he confesses the one shameful act of his life: the field was taken, and he knew all along what lay under it. PAYOFF ① ↑

Set the fifty years end to end and the fuse is not the revolution, not the war, not the famine. It is the moment Bai Jiaxuan crouches in the snow — a man sees something good, decides to acquire it quietly, and then covers the act with a Covenant. What follows is not divine punishment for that fraud; the novel is not that cheap. It is the same disposition unfolding: he takes the field, beats the son out of the house, declares the daughter nonexistent, burns a woman to ash and pins her under a tower — and every time he is standing on the side of the rules, every time he is beyond reproach, and every time somebody dies of it. When the account is finally settled he admits only the first item, because only the first was private. The rest, to his last day, he considered public business.

Three Slow-Motion Replays

1 · The spear in the cave: why the killer has to be the kindest man

The power of this scene is entirely in the casting. There is a queue of people who want Tian Xiao'e dead: Lu Zilin, who fears what she can say; Bai Jiaxuan, who hates what she has done to the rules; every woman in the village; and the men are not clean either. The author hands the spear to the one person who has no grievance against her and who never took anything from her. Lu San kills her not for gain, not for lust, not in rage, but for one reason: this woman makes it impossible for him to hold his head up.

He is given no interior monologue. What you see is an old farmhand walking into a cave at night and putting a spear through a back — the motion as clean as work in a field. And the syllable she turns and produces flips the whole act inside out: she claims him as her father; he regarded her as his disgrace. One word completes the conversion from pest control to killing a family member, and it is the victim who performs the conversion on the killer's behalf.

Nor does the aftermath go through any law. Nobody reports it, nobody thinks he did wrong; the punishment comes from another direction entirely — plague, possession, his own throat producing her voice. In that village the law would never have taken her side; the only advocate available to her is a ghost.

2 · The brush in the hall: a father beating his son for an audience

The whole tension of Bai Xiaowen's punishment comes from who is watching. The hall is packed, the father has the spiked brush, and Lu Zilin is standing in the crowd — the true lead in this scene is the impassive spectator, because he arranged every part of it, the timing of the discovery included. The reader knows this and the father does not, and all the pain comes from that gap.

The prose is severe about the physical detail and silent about feeling: no tears from the father, no pleading from the son, only the brush dipped, raised, brought down, a layer of skin at a stroke. The less interior life we are shown, the more clearly we can see the hand shaking. And the beating is followed instantly by the division of the household and the expulsion — sentence welded to punishment, not one second left over for emotion. This is exactly how the Covenant runs: it does not process people, it processes clauses. The echo arrives hundreds of pages later, when it turns out that the beating did not put the son back on the road; it merely removed the last of his shame.

3 · The pit: writing "our own side did it" without a single slogan

Bai Ling's death is the quietest passage in the book. Every earlier danger was loud — arrest, escape, gunfire in a street. For the one that actually kills her the sound is switched off: no enemy, no exchange of fire, only a meeting among her own people, a list, and a hole in the ground. The author's choice is to withhold the reason: the logic of the purge goes unexplained, the men doing it are given neither names nor faces, and Bai Ling gets no speech in her own defence. That blank is closer to the truth of the thing than any denunciation — she dies precisely because nobody is required to explain anything to her.

Then there is the white deer. The plain is named for a legend: long ago a white deer ran across it, and wherever it passed the crops surged and the sickness withdrew. It changes form several times in the book — the plant in the snow, the white shape Master Zhu's widow sees leap from the house and drift west at his death, the dreams people on the plain have when the young die. The author never identifies the deer with any one person, because the moment he did, it would drop from being a whole people's shared imagining to being one character's symbol. It works more like the book's question to itself: that thing that once made the crops surge — where exactly did it get to during these fifty years?

The Book's Core Device: compressing a national history into one village

White Deer Plain does not stand on a single startling idea. Its core device is an arrangement, three parts meshing together.

One: two surnames from a single root. Bai and Lu were one house that forked; one holds, one pushes. This is not a simple hero-and-villain setup — it is an engine that can run indefinitely: the shared root guarantees the two families must coexist, and the fork guarantees there is always something to measure. Every historical event that enters (the 1911 revolution, warlords, the peasant association, the war, land reform) is automatically machined by this engine into "is the Bai house up or is the Lu house up." History stops being background and becomes fuel.

Two: the ancestral hall as the only bench. There is not one functioning act of justice in the entire novel. The county government arrives and leaves, the militia arrives and leaves, the guerrillas arrive and leave; the only tribunal in continuous operation is the hall — who may enter, who kneels where, who is beaten, who is expelled. Writing one clan hall as the only stable court in modern Chinese history is the book's largest judgement, and also the one most worth interrogating.

Three: legend and archive, alternating. On one side, a donkey turning back an army, a ghost speaking through a living man, a deer appearing in the snow — the voice of village storytelling. On the other, dates, grain prices, tax categories, the format of a county gazetteer — the voice of the record office. The two vouch for each other: the legend persuades you the place has a soul, the archive persuades you it happened. "Secret history" grows out of that seam.

What It Forces You to Think About

QUESTION 1
Is Bai Jiaxuan a good man — and if so, why does a man who takes righteousness to its limit end by pinning a woman under a tower forever?

Take the defence to its strongest, stronger than his admirers would put it. In a collapsing country with no law, no police and no land registry, the only thing that keeps a village from dissolving is a set of rules everyone accepts and one man willing to take the punishment first. What is remarkable about Bai Jiaxuan is not that he punishes others but that he punishes himself: Master Zhu ploughs up his field and he says nothing; his own son offends and he strikes hardest of all. He never took a coin of common money, never leaned on a widow, opened his granary in the famine year and stayed put during the plague. He does not crush Tian Xiao'e out of spite either: he sincerely believes that if the malign thing wins, the rules go, and if the rules go, everyone dies.

Now the prosecution, also at full strength. The cost of this system is never borne by the man who wrote it: to Bai Jiaxuan it is an honour, to Tian Xiao'e a death sentence, to Leng Qiuyue a bowl of poison, to Bai Ling a funeral held while she is alive. Worse, its first brick is dirty — the field was obtained by fraud, and he admits it only at the end, to a madman. He spent a lifetime using a system that demanded absolute honesty of everyone else to cover his one act of dishonesty. A man who breaks the rule only where nobody can call him on it is a man who knows exactly what the rule is and who it is for. And an even less comfortable layer: he is not a hypocrite, he is a believer — which is worse, because at least a hypocrite gets bitten once by his conscience.

Bring it home: your company, your school, your family all run on "how we do things here." Try asking one question: who pays the most under those rules, and did that person have any part in writing them? The answer is usually no — and it is precisely because the answer is no that the system runs so smoothly.

QUESTION 2
Is Tian Xiao'e purely a victim? Everyone in the book uses her — and she also destroys a man who never did anything to her.

Take "she is beyond reproach" all the way. Sold by her father, used as a medicinal apparatus by an old man, abandoned on the plain by her lover, wielded as a blade by Lu Zilin, killed by her father-in-law, burned to ash by the clan head. She is the only person in the novel who never at any point has a choice, and the only resource she can deploy is a body that can be taken from her at any moment. As for seducing Bai Xiaowen: that was Lu Zilin's instruction, and her situation was that disobedience meant not eating tomorrow. To demand morality first from someone who depends on other people's charity for food is itself a form of charity.

But the other side has to be given its full weight, not waved away with "she had her reasons." Bai Xiaowen never harmed her. She knew what she was doing and she knew the consequences — that man would be flogged, expelled, finished. Being oppressed is not the same as being absolved; a person can suffer an enormous wrong and that still does not make a third party fair game. She admits it herself afterwards, which tells you the author had no intention of issuing her a clean victim's certificate. Writing her as a perfect victim would be one more simplification of her — it would amount to saying that only the innocent deserve sympathy.

What should survive this section is not a verdict but an observation: nearly all the evil in this book is passed by the humiliated to someone weaker. Lu San cannot bear his shame and kills a daughter-in-law with even less standing than he has; Tian Xiao'e cannot bear being handled and ruins a boy with even less experience; Bai Xiaowen loses face and ends by executing the one man who truly reformed. Violence on this plain does not rain downward. It is handed along, person to person, and at each handover the receiver feels he is settling a score.

Bring it home: the last time you thought "I had no choice" — where did the cost finally land? On the person who cornered you, or on someone easier to push? Those two versions of "no choice" look identical from outside and are an entire character apart.

QUESTION 3
Bai Ling gives her life for an ideal, and that ideal's own organization kills her. Does her life still count?

"It counts" is not the soft position. Her choice was right at the moment she made it: what she was facing was a world that sold daughters, bound their feet, and declared a disobedient one dead, and the things the new world promised — literacy, a way out, choosing her own husband, choosing her own death — she actually got, even if only for a few years. The worth of a life cannot be assigned retroactively by the people who ended it. And she died of the organization's disease, not of its aim; collapsing the two is to concede that everyone killed by their own side lived for nothing.

"It doesn't count" is at least as strong and cuts deeper. An ideal is not a text; it is whatever a group of people actually execute. If the executors can bury the most loyal person alive with no evidence, no hearing and no charge, then the real-world form of that ideal is the pit, and everything else is publicity. Bai Ling died without knowing why, which means she could not even hold on to the thought that she was dying for something — she was disposed of. When a person has no right to know the reason for her own death, calling it self-realization is a bit cruel. The novel declines to conclude: it refuses to let the later honours cancel the pit, and refuses to let the pit erase her choice.

Bring it home: you have given serious time and trust to some organization — a company, a team, a cause, a relationship. If it turns and damages you, do you say you misread it from the start and write the whole thing off, or do you hold that the judgement was correct at the time and the people executing it went bad? Those two accountings produce two entirely different second halves of a life.

QUESTION 4
Hei Wa reforms and is shot; Bai Xiaowen rots all the way through and becomes magistrate. Does a society leave a road open for changing?

Give the ugly side its full strength first: Hei Wa deserves it, and he is not wronged. He was a bandit; he had a man's spine broken; he robbed and burned and hurt people who had nothing to do with him. Reading the classics afterwards, marrying well and kneeling in a hall constitute no compensation to anyone he harmed — reforming is your business, the account is still the account. If repentance could void the past, the calculating would offend first and repent second, and the price of doing harm would fall. That logic is hard.

But the logic the novel supplies is blacker. He is shot not for any of those debts, but because charging him is the cheapest way to demonstrate the new authority's teeth and to make the man passing sentence look clean. Bai Xiaowen knows better than anyone that Hei Wa reformed — they changed sides together. That is exactly why he has to die: people who know what you used to be are best not left alive. The question stops being "does society leave a road open" and becomes "who is running the tollbooth on that road."

Worth pausing on Bai Xiaowen's own confession: once face is lost it is never worth anything again. Shame is the last brake on a person, and that brake was removed by a piece of family discipline executed flawlessly. The worst man in the book did not come in from outside; the village of righteousness manufactured him.

Bring it home: is there someone in your life who cannot come back — who got into trouble, was disgraced, did time, whose name is bad in some circle? You would probably say they deserve another chance; but at the point of actually working alongside them, of letting them into your house, what do you choose? The day Hei Wa knelt in that hall, White Deer Plain made the best decision in its history; a few years later it voided that decision itself — and the same people did both things.

Motifs: what Chen Zhongshi kept writing

Chen Zhongshi produced one large book, and the earlier novellas now read like its sketches. The village schoolmaster in The Man in the Blue Gown, tied hand and foot by an old-style upbringing, is a shared draft for Master Zhu and Bai Jiaxuan; The Kang Family Courtyard follows a country woman deforming inch by inch inside a marriage nobody consulted her about — the ancestor of Xiancao, Leng Qiuyue and Tian Xiao'e. The first obsession he keeps returning to is the back: that dignity of the Guanzhong farmer, held up by rules, and how many lives it takes to keep it upright.

The second is land. The relationship between his people and their soil is not lyrical, it is accountancy: land can be traded, sold, swindled, planted with white flowers and turned into silver, or altered in its luck for generations by the burial of one set of bones. Almost every moral problem in this book can be traced back to a specific field.

The third is sex meeting propriety head-on. His nerve in writing this book was not political but bodily: a village can absorb killing, famine, informers and civil war, and cannot absorb a woman deciding for herself who she sleeps with. That is where a good half of the "secret" in "secret history" lives — official history has no column for Tian Xiao'e.

Criticism and Controversy

  • The prize and the revisions. For the 1997 Mao Dun Prize the author revised the novel along lines the judges indicated, cutting back some sexual material and some of Master Zhu's political commentary. The most widely circulated text today is that revision, and many readers do not know that what they hold is not the 1993 book. A novel about a nation's secret history turns out to have one of its own.
  • The women. This is the hardest charge to answer. Tian Xiao'e is the most alive person in the book, and yet nearly every woman enters the narrative through her body: concubine, possession, silenced wife, childbearing machine. Xiancao endures, Leng Qiuyue goes mad, and Bai Ling, independent as she is, has her fate settled by the party allegiances of two men. A novel written on behalf of the humiliated still looks at the humiliated in a male way — and "those were the times" does not dispose of the objection, because the narrator belongs to ours. The date-soaking detail has also been called out as sensationalism.
  • Master Zhu is idealized. A donkey ride that turns back an army, uncanny foresight, a deer at his death: the character verges on faultless and then on the supernatural. Writing the Confucian gentry to that pitch skirts what the gentry system actually did to farmers — the very code that makes Bai Jiaxuan so imposing is part of the apparatus of domination.
  • Its history satisfies nobody. Writing the purge, the brutality on both sides of the civil war, and the falsification of Lu Zhaohai's death took real nerve in 1993, and duly attracted the charge of rewriting settled verdicts; from the other direction, critics note that the book finally deposits its resolution back in Bai Jiaxuan's clan order — fifty years of upheaval and the answer is still that hall.
  • Structural imbalance. The density of characters and events spikes in the second half, and the Bai Ling, Lu Zhaopeng and Lu Zhaohai strands are visibly thinner than the first half; an event as large as Bai Ling's death gets very few pages. Some read this as scar tissue from the cuts, others as the author's lesser feel for urban and party life.
  • Adaptations versus the book. The 2012 film reduced the novel to Tian Xiao'e's story, with the Bai Ling and Master Zhu strands essentially gone. The result is that many people believe White Deer Plain is a tragedy about desire, when in the novel desire is only the most conspicuous scratch on that threshold.

Ten Sentences to Take Away

1. The fuse is not the revolution or the famine. It is a man crouching in snow and deciding to acquire a good field quietly — the foundation of "the village of righteousness" is a deception nobody was told about.

2. The coldest line in the book is not given to a villain: a woman is paper pasted over a window, said by a kindly old mother at her stove.

3. The first poppy field Master Zhu ploughs under belongs to his brother-in-law; twenty years later those white flowers come back in the brother-in-law's son's lungs.

4. The door of the ancestral hall opens five times — stone, shut, smashed, sealed, reopened — and that threshold, not either patriarch, is the real protagonist.

5. Tian Xiao'e dies producing one syllable, the word for father: the identity she claims at the end is exactly the one the village refused her.

6. The village wants to build her a shrine; Bai Jiaxuan burns her bones and raises a tower instead. He is not exorcising a ghost, he is proving the rules reach the dead.

7. Hei Wa has the patriarch's spine broken for a transparent reason: that back had been too straight all his life and it frightened him as a child.

8. A coin tossed for fun decided, a decade later, which side a man died on and whether his death could be called by its real name.

9. Bai Ling survives the enemy's prison and dies in her own side's pit — no name, no charge, no defence; the blank is closer to the truth than any denunciation.

10. The book's least negotiable sentence is on its last pages: the reformed man is shot, the unreformed man becomes magistrate, and the magistrate was manufactured by a piece of family discipline executed flawlessly.
