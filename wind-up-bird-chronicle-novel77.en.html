Novels, Deep-Read · Haruki Murakami · No.77

The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle

Nejimaki-dori kuronikuru · Haruki Murakami · 1994–1995

中文 →

Outer Mongolia, 1938. A Japanese officer lies at the bottom of a deep, dry well on the open steppe, one leg injured, no water, no food, a small round sky far above him.
Once a day the sun passes directly over the mouth of the well and a column of light drops straight down onto him. It lasts ten or fifteen seconds. In that light he feels that something is about to arrive — close enough to touch — and then the light slides away and the dark closes back in.
Forty-six years later, in a quiet Tokyo suburb, a man who has just quit his job is cooking spaghetti, with Rossini's overture to The Thieving Magpie on the radio. The phone rings.

This page contains complete spoilers — why Kumiko leaves, who the woman on the phone really is, what happens in Room 208 at the bottom of the well, how Noboru Wataya falls, what Kumiko pays for it, and how the light Lieutenant Mamiya waited for in his well hollowed out the rest of his life. Many of this book's riddles are never solved. The ones that are, you need to know before you can talk about it.

In one sentence

An unemployed Tokyo man loses his cat, then his wife; to get her back he climbs down into the dry well of an abandoned house nearby and sits there for days — and the well turns out to open onto a hotel room that exists nowhere, and onto the atrocities Japan committed half a century earlier in Manchuria and on the Mongolian steppe; in the dark he swings a baseball bat and kills a man whose face he never sees, and in the waking world the brother-in-law who "defiled" his wife collapses at the same moment.

Where it sits

Haruki Murakami (born 1949). The novel came out in three volumes: Book One, The Thieving Magpie, and Book Two, Bird as Prophet, together in 1994; Book Three, The Birdcatcher, in 1995. It won the Yomiuri Prize the following year. Murakami wrote much of it while living in the United States, and he himself has called it a turning point: the move from the "detachment" of his early urban fables to "commitment" — the first time he put Japan's own wartime violence at the center of a novel.

On the literary map it is his longest and darkest book: a drifting first-person narrator, wrapped in Kafkaesque absurdity and the wells and dreams of magical realism, with several near-documentary war testimonies set into it like stones.

The story

Act One · A cat, a phone call, and an alley that goes nowhere

The narrator is Toru Okada, thirty, who has just quit his job as a gofer at a law firm because it stopped meaning anything to him. His wife, Kumiko, edits a health-food magazine and keeps the household afloat. It is the summer of 1984.

On the first page he is boiling spaghetti when the phone rings. Seed ①A woman he doesn't recognize says she knows him, and that if he gives her ten minutes the two of them can come to understand each other. Then the talk turns explicit, like a dirty call. He hangs up. She calls again over the following weeks, always seeming to know things about his home. He can't place her.

Then Kumiko calls: the cat hasn't come home in a week; go and look for it. The cat is named Noboru Wataya — after Kumiko's older brother, because the couple thought it had exactly his look. The name was half a joke, half a way of letting off steam.

Looking for the cat takes him into the "alley" behind the house. It isn't really an alley; both ends have been blocked off and nobody can pass through. At the far end stands a house that has been empty for years. The neighbors call it the "hanging house": the Miyawaki family who owned it lost their business, ran into debt and vanished, and everyone who lived there before them came to grief too. In the yard stands a stone bird, and in the weeds there is a dry well.

By the alley he meets May Kasahara, sixteen, skipping school, sunbathing in her yard in a bikini and doing a street survey for a wig company — counting how many passers-by are bald. She calls him "Mr. Wind-Up Bird," because he tells her about a bird in the trees nearby whose cry sounds like someone winding a spring, creak, creak, as if it were winding up the world each day. Nobody has ever seen it. It is only ever heard.

The cat stays lost, so Kumiko sends him to meet Malta Kano, a psychic who works with water and wears a red vinyl hat, in a hotel coffee shop in Shinagawa. She tells him the cat is no longer nearby, that the "flow" around him has changed, that something has been blocked. Then her younger sister, Creta Kano, turns up at his door with an outlandish story. She lived her whole childhood in unbroken physical pain, so bad that she tried to kill herself by driving into a wall. She survived and woke up feeling nothing at all. To pay off her debts she became a prostitute, and then she met a client: Noboru Wataya. He did something to her that she says was not sex but a kind of defilement, as if he had reached in, taken things out of her and put them back in the wrong places.

Noboru Wataya himself is Kumiko's brother: a University of Tokyo economist who wrote an unreadable book, then became a celebrity pundit on television, fluent and endlessly flexible, and later took over his uncle's political base in Niigata to run for the Diet. Toru loathed him from the first meeting — not because he seemed wicked but because there was nothing inside him. Everything he said was correct, and the sum was empty, a carefully assembled shell built for drawing other people's substance into itself.

When Toru married Kumiko, her parents objected and set an odd condition: once a month before the wedding he had to visit a Mr. Honda, a nearly deaf old man the Wataya family trusted as a seer. Honda kept the television blaring and said disconnected things, Seed ②one of which stuck: when it's time to go up, find the highest tower and climb to the top; when it's time to go down, find the deepest well and go to the bottom (roughly). At the time it sounded like an old man rambling.

Act Two · Lieutenant Mamiya's well

Mr. Honda dies. An old man named Mamiya, with an artificial left hand, comes to deliver a keepsake. The package is wrapped with great care. At home Toru opens it: an empty whisky box, nothing inside.

Sitting in Toru's living room, Mamiya tells a story he has never told to the end. In 1938 he was a young officer in the Kwantung Army, assigned to an intelligence man named Yamamoto, with Honda — then a corporal — among the small party that slipped across the Khalkha River into Outer Mongolia. Mamiya never fully learned what the mission was. They were caught by Mongolian soldiers and a Russian officer, who ordered a Mongol to skin Yamamoto alive — starting at the shoulder, slowly, in one piece, like peeling a peach — until Yamamoto stopped screaming. Mamiya was made to watch. The Russian's name was Boris. Years later, Mamiya ran into him again in a Siberian prison camp — this comes in a long letter Mamiya writes to Toru in Book Three — where Boris had become the man who ran the camp; Mamiya got one chance to shoot him and failed to kill him.

On the steppe they gave Mamiya a choice: a bullet, or the well beside them. He jumped. The well was deep; he hurt his leg in the fall and lay at the bottom. Each day around noon the sun shone straight down for ten or fifteen seconds. In that light he felt something like grace about to arrive, about to take shape — and every time it fell just short. Days later it was Honda, following some intuition he could never explain, who found the well and pulled him out.

Mamiya survived. He survived the rest of the war and the Siberian camp. But his real life, he says, ended in that well. The light never came, and the forty-odd years since have been empty; he taught school and went through the days like a man already dead. He didn't understand why Honda left him an empty box, until he did: perhaps it was meant to tell him exactly that — nothing remains.

Toru has nothing to say. None of it seems to have anything to do with a missing cat or with his wife.

Act Three · Kumiko leaves, and he goes down the well

Then Kumiko doesn't come home.

The morning before, on her way out to work, she had asked him to zip up the back of her dress. Seed ③As he did, he caught an unfamiliar scent behind her ear — a cologne he hadn't given her and that wasn't the kind she bought. He didn't ask. She left, said she'd see him that evening, and never came back.

A few days later Malta Kano brings Noboru Wataya to meet him. Noboru calmly explains that Kumiko has another man, doesn't want to see Toru, and wants a divorce. Toru doesn't believe it. Then Kumiko's letter arrives.

It is composed and cold. Payoff ③ ↑She says she has slept with another man, more than once, because she wanted to, not because anyone tricked her; she no longer knows who she is; please don't look for her. The strange cologne had registered as a small, unspoken unease. Here it lands. She was already leaving as she walked out of the door, and the zip at her back was the last thing she had him do for her with his own hands. The cruelty of the device is that the information was handed over inside the most ordinary gesture of a marriage, and neither the narrator nor the reader caught it.

He goes back over small things from their years together. Kumiko hated beef stir-fried with green peppers; once he made it and she flew into an anger he couldn't account for. One year, while he was away on a work trip, she had an abortion on her own and told him only afterward — she said the child couldn't be, and couldn't say why. He had always assumed he knew her. Now he saw that he had never asked.

He decides to go down the well.

He buys a rope ladder at a hardware store, climbs into the yard of the hanging house and lowers himself to the bottom of the dry well. Payoff ② ↑In Act One, Mr. Honda's line about towers and wells was an old man's verbal tic. Here a man who has lost all sense of direction takes it literally and goes to the bottom of the nearest well he can find. And the reader has just learned from Mamiya that Honda was the one who pulled a man out of a well on the steppe. When the old man said "well," he was never speaking in metaphor.

He sits in the dark and thinks about Kumiko. The next day the ladder is gone: May Kasahara has pulled it up. She talks to him over the rim, says she wants to know what happens to a person in a place like this. She tells him about herself: riding on the back of her boyfriend's motorbike, she put her hands over his eyes as a joke; they crashed, and the boy died. She says there is a "gooey thing" inside her that keeps growing and keeps drawing her toward death. She puts the lid back over the well and leaves.

In total darkness Toru begins to dream — or to walk through the wall. He finds himself in a hotel. At the end of a corridor is Room 208, pitch black, and inside a woman talks to him in a voice like the one on the phone. Someone knocks; she tells him to hide. He passes back through the wall to the bottom of the well.

A few days later he is pulled out. He looks in the mirror: Seed ④on his right cheek there is now a blue-black mark, the size of a baby's palm, faintly warm to the touch.

Act Four · A bat, a mark on the face, a bench in Shinjuku

From here on, Toru's world begins to take on the color of violence.

Years before, in a bar in Sapporo, he had watched a folk singer who stopped mid-song, held his palm over a candle flame and kept it there, saying he wanted the audience to feel the pain. One day in Tokyo Toru spots a man carrying a guitar case who looks very like that singer, and follows him into an apartment building. Seed ⑤In the stairwell the man turns, pulls a baseball bat out of the case and swings at him. Toru wrenches the bat away and beats him to the ground, and keeps beating him, until he frightens himself — he had no idea there was that much hatred in his body. He leaves the man lying there and takes the bat home.

His uncle, who owns some property, once gave him a piece of advice: if you want to know whether a place is any good, sit in the street and watch people's faces. So Toru goes to the west exit of Shinjuku station every day and sits on a bench from morning to evening, looking at faces. A well-dressed middle-aged woman notices him. Her name is Nutmeg Akasaka, and she has a son, Cinnamon, who does not speak.

Nutmeg offers him work that is hard to describe. Wealthy, well-dressed women of a certain age are quietly driven to him by Cinnamon; each spends an hour with him in a room, and some reach out and touch the mark on his face — and afterward, they say, something inside them that has no name is eased. Nutmeg did this herself for years; she can't any more, and the mark tells her he can take over. The money is absurd. Through Nutmeg's company he uses it to buy the land of the hanging house; the old house is torn down and a new one built over the dry well. Now the well is his.

Meanwhile the cat comes home on its own after almost a year away, its tail bent and a little shorter. Toru gives it a new name: Mackerel. It is no longer Noboru Wataya.

Nutmeg tells him where she comes from. In August 1945 she was a little girl; her father was the veterinarian at the zoo in Hsinking, the capital of the Japanese puppet state of Manchukuo (today's Changchun). Before the Soviet army arrived, the Japanese military ordered every large predator in the zoo shot so that none could escape and attack people: tigers, leopards, wolves, bears, one after another, killed by soldiers in front of the vet. Nutmeg and her mother took a ship back to Japan. She never saw her father again.

Cinnamon has written these stories into his computer as a series of files, every one titled "The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle." On the screen Toru reads what happened next. The day after the animals were killed, the same soldiers brought in several Chinese cadets from the Manchukuo military academy who had killed their Japanese instructors and fled, and been caught. The young lieutenant ordered them executed on the spot without guns — bullets had to be saved. Three were bayoneted. The last was beaten to death with a baseball bat. The vet stood and watched. Payoff ⑤ ↑The bat Toru pulled out of a guitar case and carried home now shows where it really comes from: it isn't just a weapon, it's the same weapon appearing a second time. What an unemployed man swung in a Tokyo stairwell in 1984 is the bat that killed a man outside Hsinking in 1945. The novel never explains the link. It simply lets you watch one gesture happen twice, in two eras.

That mark is the reason Nutmeg stopped in front of the bench in Shinjuku: Payoff ④ ↑her father — the vet who watched the tigers shot and the cadet beaten to death — had a blue-black mark on his right cheek, exactly like Toru's. When it surfaced on Toru's face in the well, it read as a piece of Murakami strangeness, a supernatural brand. Now it has a lineage: it is the mark of a witness. A man who stood by during a massacre and could do nothing carried it once; now it sits on the face of an idle Tokyo husband forty years later. The elegance is that nobody explains it. The same mark appears in 1945 and in 1984, and the reader lays one face over the other.

Act Five · Room 208

All this time Toru has been talking to Kumiko through Cinnamon's computer — an arrangement made by Noboru's side, line after line of text on a screen. She says she is ill, that she carries the same thing inside her that her brother does, that she can't come back. Toru grows certain: Kumiko didn't simply leave. She is being held somewhere, and the man holding her is Noboru Wataya.

Noboru sends an emissary named Ushikawa: short, bald, ugly, obsequious and oily. He has terms to offer. The weekly magazines are sniffing around the new house on the hanging-house lot and the luxury cars that come and go at night, and the story threatens to touch Mr. Wataya; if Toru will just give up the land, anything can be arranged. Toru won't.

He goes down the well one last time, and this time he takes the bat.

He passes through the wall into the hotel again. A television in the lobby is running a news report: Noboru Wataya, member of the Diet, has been attacked. Toru finds Room 208. It is dark as before, and the woman is still talking. Payoff ① ↑This time he finally says her name: she is Kumiko. The "just ten minutes" call on the very first page, the voice he couldn't place and found both lewd and frightening, was his wife — the part of Kumiko he had never known, released by Noboru Wataya and then trapped by him, calling for help. The thing in the opening chapter that looked most like a prank was the book's first cry for help, and he hung up on it on page one.

Someone is outside the door. A man whose face he cannot see comes in with a knife. In the dark Toru swings the bat. He is cut, but he keeps swinging until the other man stops moving. He passes back through the wall into the well, and the well, dry for who knows how many years, begins to fill with water. It rises past his waist, his chest; he nearly drowns before Cinnamon hauls him out.

In the waking world, Noboru Wataya collapses in Nagasaki with a cerebral hemorrhage and is taken to hospital in a coma.

Epilogue · Pulling the plug

Kumiko sends Toru a final message. She will go to the hospital and switch off her brother's life support herself; then she will turn herself in and stand trial. It is something she has to do on her own. She asks him to wait for her.

She does it. Toru goes to see May Kasahara, who has dropped out of school and is working at a wig factory in the mountains; through Book Three her letters to "Mr. Wind-Up Bird" have been threaded between the chapters. They sit together and talk. May looks at his face and tells him the mark is gone. The witness's mark has faded once the thing is done — Toru took it on, carried it down the well, and swung the same bat on behalf of the people who were beaten and flayed somewhere else.

He will wait. That is where the novel stops.

If you want the fuse this story burns along, it isn't the cat, and it isn't the phone call. The fuse is the "thing" in the Wataya bloodline — a power to hollow other people out, defile them and take them over. It is in Noboru, and in Kumiko too; the novel hints that it has run in the family for generations, and that Noboru's uncle was a wartime bureaucrat with ties to Manchuria. Boris the Manskinner, the lieutenant outside Hsinking and the pundit on television are faces of one force in different decades. Every step Toru takes that year — searching for the cat, going down the well, sitting on the bench, swinging the bat — is the aftershock of an ordinary man finally saying no to it. And the light that never came for Mamiya is the first hole it left in a human life.

Two timelines side by side. The novel never claims cause and effect; it just lets the same things happen twice.

Three slow-motion scenes

One · Mamiya's testimony: a flaying told in a living room

Mamiya's story is the least Murakami-like passage in the book: no jazz, no playful similes, just an old man at a coffee table walking through it step by step in the tone of a report. The skinning is told slowly and in precise detail.

Why does it work? Because of where it is placed — the safest possible room, a suburban living room on an idle man's afternoon. A hundred-odd pages have trained the reader into the lazy rhythm of a man looking for his cat. When Mamiya starts talking, the rhythm doesn't change and the tone doesn't change; only the content drops to the very bottom of history. That sameness is more frightening than any acceleration: it tells you this is the tone in which such violence happens, and the tone in which it gets told.

Two · The well: put a man in total darkness and give him nothing to do

Toru's days at the bottom of the well contain almost no events. He sits, thinks, sleeps, gets thirsty, gets hungry, listens to May at the rim, and then she covers the hole.

Why does it work? Because Murakami writes "nothing happens" as a descent. The senses are taken away one by one — light, time, food, certainty — until all that's left is memory, and the small things he had skated over (the zip, the cologne, the abortion, the beef with green peppers) float up one after another. The well is a technical device here: it forces narrator and reader to reread the plot so far. And because Mamiya's well has just been described, the two wells overlap: one man thrown in, one man walking down; one waiting for light, one waiting to understand.

Three · Room 208: killing an unseen man in a place no one can see

The climax is short and very dark: no face, no visible blood, only sound, the feel of a blade opening skin, the shock of the bat landing. Toru doesn't know whom he has killed.

Why does it work? Because it puts both the evil and the resistance where no one can witness them. If Toru tracked Noboru down in the real world and beat him in person, this would be a revenge novel. Murakami moves the fight into the depths of dream and well, and lets the real man "happen" to collapse from a stroke. The reader can't say exactly what Toru did — or whether he should answer for it. And we already know where that bat comes from. The one who resists picks up the tool the perpetrators used. The novel leaves that discomfort in place on purpose.

The book's core device

One · The well: a vertical passage

Murakami's fiction is full of holes: underground spaces, tunnels, wells, pits. In this book the well is a device with fairly strict rules (the novel's rules, not anybody's occult system):

First, a well can be forced on you, or chosen. Mamiya is thrown in; Toru climbs down. That is the book's central pairing: the same space as punishment and as discipline. Second, the well connects to another place. The hotel and Room 208 are on no map; they are more like a layer beneath everyone's consciousness where things touch — down there Toru meets another part of Kumiko and strikes down another part of Noboru. Third, the well connects to history. The well in the hanging-house yard and the well on the Mongolian steppe read like two mouths of a single shaft.

To be honest about it: the logic of the passage holds up as literature, and is deliberately left open as causation. Toru hits a man in a dream; Noboru falls in the waking world. The novel neither says the first caused the second nor says it didn't. That isn't carelessness; it's a position — some forces operate on a level where nothing can be proven. But it is also one of the book's most criticized moves, as we'll see.

Two · The wind-up bird: the unseen one who winds the world

Nobody in the book ever sees the bird in the title; they only hear it creak. May calls Toru "Mr. Wind-Up Bird," Cinnamon's files are all called "The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle," and the vet at the Hsinking zoo seems to hear the same cry on that terrible day (roughly).

The idea, more or less: the world doesn't turn by itself; some small, unseen creature has to wind its spring. In Hsinking in 1945 no one wound it, and the world stopped inside a massacre. In 1984 the name is handed to an unemployed man. It is Murakami's answer to the question of what use an ordinary person is: not a hero, but someone who winds the spring — a little each day, unnoticed.

Three · A big book that refuses to tie its threads

The novel trails loose ends: what becomes of the Kano sisters, the unsolved murder of Nutmeg's husband, why Cinnamon doesn't speak. Its narration is a patchwork of memory, testimony, letters, computer files and magazine reports. It sets one man's story among many voices and deliberately lets them fail to form a complete picture — which is how history tends to reach us.

What it makes you think about

One · She says "don't look for me." Should he?

Kumiko's letter is perfectly clear: she slept with someone else, she wanted to, she is no longer who she was, please don't look for her. An adult has said no in her own words. On what grounds does Toru decide she "really" needs rescuing?

First, the strongest case for leaving her alone. "She doesn't mean it, she's being controlled" is, in real life, the favorite line of stalkers and possessive partners. Someone decides to leave, and those around her immediately conclude she must have been brainwashed, coerced or stolen by a worse man — which is itself a refusal to grant that she has a will of her own. Kumiko's letter admits desire, admits there is something in her Toru doesn't know; his answer is: that isn't you, someone else put that in you. That is a more complete rejection than divorce. And he never asked why she hated beef with green peppers, or why she went alone to end a pregnancy. Inside the marriage he didn't see her; outside it, he is certain he understands her best.

The other side: some "no"s really are spoken from inside someone else's grip. Kumiko's letter was written on Noboru's ground; the computer conversations were arranged by Noboru's people; she says she is ill. A brother who could defile Creta could get his sister to write "don't look for me" herself. If Toru had simply signed the divorce papers, he might have respected a letter and abandoned the woman who wrote it. Where the novel stands is clear enough: it has Kumiko finally say "wait for me." But it also has her pull the plug and turn herself in — in the end it's Kumiko, not Toru, who saves Kumiko; he only opens the passage.

Bring it home: a friend suddenly joins a group you find suspicious, moves in with someone you think is dangerous, or simply stops answering. Do you respect her choice, or trust what you think you see? Where's the line?

Two · Against a man who defiles people, can you use the same bat?

Noboru Wataya has committed no crime in any legal sense. He goes on television, runs for office, says fine things; what he did to Creta and Kumiko leaves no evidence, no wounds, nothing to prosecute. Toru kills that kind of evil in the dark. Is that justice, or an ordinary man finally learning violence?

First, the strongest case for acting. There is a kind of evil that lives precisely where law and evidence can't reach: it leaves no wound, only an emptiness; it happens in respectable settings, wrapped in respectable language. Against it, "be reasonable, follow procedure" is exactly what it wants — Noboru is a master of being reasonable. Mamiya stood on the steppe and watched Yamamoto flayed, did nothing, and his life was empty ever after. Not resisting hollows you out too. When Toru goes down the well with the bat, a man who has never fought anyone admits for the first time that some things must be stopped, even at the cost of blood on his hands.

The other side is just as hard: the bat in his hands killed a Chinese cadet outside Hsinking. The history of violence has always been written in the conviction that this time it's aimed at the real villain — the lieutenant probably believed men who killed their instructors and ran deserved to die. When Toru beat the guitar man in the stairwell, he frightened himself; what he saw in that moment wasn't justice but the same force inside his own body. And the novel lets Noboru conveniently suffer a stroke, leaving Toru's hands legally clean. Is that too neat — letting the reader enjoy the revenge without paying for it? The one who actually bears the consequences is Kumiko, who pulls the plug and goes to prison.

Bring it home: an online exposure of someone "the law can't touch," a collective pile-on, the phrase "you don't owe people like that the rules." When would you decide it's the only way? And how would you know that what you're holding isn't that bat?

Three · Does a man boiling spaghetti in Tokyo in 1984 answer for the steppe in 1938?

Toru was born after the war, never fought, and no one in his family ever told him about Manchuria. Mamiya's story and the vet's story have nothing directly to do with his life. Yet the novel makes them come to his door one after another, and makes him recognize them in himself. Should he carry this?

First, the strongest case for no. Guilt belongs to individuals, not to bloodlines or nationalities. Making people who took no part answer for their grandparents' crimes is a dangerous logic — the mirror image of "that people is guilty by birth." And "everyone is responsible" often means no one is: the people who actually gave the orders dissolve into a collective act of contrition. Perhaps the most honest stance an ordinary person can take is: "This has nothing to do with me, but I know it happened" — and then live well.

The other side: these things aren't past; they're still here. Noboru's uncle was a wartime bureaucrat; the Wataya family's power was inherited straight from that era; the force that treats human beings as raw material has put on a new suit and gone on television. A society that locks such stories in old men's living rooms and lets them die with the tellers will meet them again in another shape. Toru doesn't have to plead guilty to 1938. What he has to do is stop treating it as none of his business. That, roughly, is what Murakami means by "commitment."

Bring it home: your family, your city, the company you work for — is there a past nobody mentions? Once you know it, can you go on living as before? Should you?

Motifs: what Murakami keeps writing

Murakami keeps returning to a man who has lost a woman and goes down into some underworld to bring her back — very nearly the Orpheus story. In A Wild Sheep Chase (1982) it is a vanished friend and a sheep; in Norwegian Wood (1987) a girl lost to suicide and a sanatorium in the mountains. In this book, for the first time, that underworld connects to Japanese history.

The second obsession is a system that devours people: faceless, running on respectability, specializing in hollowing people out. After this novel he wrote the nonfiction Underground (1997), interviewing victims of the 1995 sarin gas attack on the Tokyo subway — that force taking shape in the real world. In 1Q84 (2009–2010) the cult and the returning Ushikawa are another appearance of it in fiction.

The third is the hole itself. In Killing Commendatore (2017) the narrator uncovers a stone pit behind a house in the mountains — again a hole opening onto another layer of the world and onto memories of war (Nanjing, Vienna). The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle is the deepest well on that line.

Controversy and criticism

Are the women instruments? This is the most common criticism and the hardest to rebut: Malta Kano carries messages, Creta Kano supplies sex and redemption, May Kasahara supplies adolescent philosophy, Nutmeg supplies money and work, and Kumiko supplies a goal to be rescued. Each has an extraordinary backstory, but the narrative's gravity always pulls toward the male narrator, and their function is often to help him complete his descent.

Too many loose threads — openness or loss of control? Critics argue that the abandoned subplots (the murder of Nutmeg's husband, the fate of the Kano sisters, the guitar man) are not deliberate gaps but things the author himself hadn't worked out; Book Three was written after the first two were published, and the seams show. Defenders see this as the book's honesty: history leaves us fragments.

How it writes the war. Many readers regard it as one of the major works of contemporary Japanese fiction to face the atrocities of the invasion of China and the Nomonhan campaign head-on. Others point out that its Chinese and Mongolian characters are almost all nameless and voiceless, present only as those who suffer violence, while the narrative's center of pain remains Japanese — Mamiya, the vet, Toru. The explicitness of the skinning scene has also made some readers ask whether it bears witness to horror or consumes it.

The abridged English edition. Jay Rubin's English translation appeared in 1997. Because of the publisher's length requirements, it omits some passages and chapters and rearranges the order of others, something the translator has openly discussed. English-language readers have been reading a trimmed version; some find the cuts clean and sensible, others think layers of Books Two and Three were lost.

Ten lines to take with you

① A man loses his cat, then his wife, and finally discovers that what he has lost is a whole set of assumptions about the world.

② The "just ten minutes" call on page one was his wife's first cry for help. He hung up.

③ The fuse isn't the cat; it's the thing in the Wataya blood that hollows people out — on television, and in Manchuria.

④ Mamiya is thrown into a well; Toru walks into one. Same space: one a punishment, one a choice.

⑤ Fifteen seconds of light that never quite arrived, and a man's life stood empty for forty years.

⑥ The bat Toru swings once killed a Chinese cadet outside Hsinking; the resister picks up the perpetrator's tool.

⑦ The blue-black mark is a witness's brand: the vet bore it, Toru took it on, and it faded when the thing was done.

⑧ No one has ever seen the wind-up bird. It just creaks once a day and winds the world a little.

⑨ In the end it isn't Toru who saves Kumiko; it's the plug she pulls herself.

⑩ He will wait. The book stops at a man who has finally learned to wait — and finally learned to act.
