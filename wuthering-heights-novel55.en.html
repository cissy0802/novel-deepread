Snow has buried the road back to town and the landlord is about as warm as the stone he lives in, so the tenant has to make do with a night in the strange farmhouse. He is put in an old bed with oak panels that close around it like a box. Before sleeping he reads a few lines scratched into the window ledge, and then he dreams twice. In the second dream a broken branch is knocking at the glass; he reaches out to push it away and closes his hand on a small, ice-cold one. Outside, a child is sobbing to be let in — she has been lost on the moor, she says, for twenty years.
The first thing this perfectly well-mannered gentleman does is drag her wrist back and forth across the jagged edge of the broken pane until the blood soaks the bedclothes.

This page contains full spoilers — why Catherine marries someone else, where Heathcliff spent his missing three years (and why the author never intends to tell you), exactly how he takes possession of both houses, why he stops eating once he has won, and why the last sentence of the novel is a piece of irony. This site tells the story whole, because everything worth arguing about here lies past the ending: without the fate of the second generation you can only read this as a love story, and a love story is precisely what it is not.

In one sentence

A nameless boy picked up off the streets of Liverpool grows up in a Yorkshire house where surnames and land are the only currency; the son of the family strips him of his education, and the one person he loves gives him up because marrying him "would degrade" her. He vanishes for three years, comes back with money nobody can account for, and using strictly legal instruments — gambling debts, marriage, wills, the law of inheritance — takes both families' houses and both families' children into his own name, then raises the next generation exactly as he was raised; until he finds he has won so completely that there is no one left to hate, stops eating, and dies in that same oak bed with the window standing open.

Coordinates

Emily Brontë (1818–1848) grew up in the parsonage at Haworth, in the West Riding of Yorkshire, one of three sisters, and barely left the moors in her life. In 1846 the sisters self-published a book of poems under three deliberately male-sounding pen names; it sold two copies. In December 1847 Wuthering Heights appeared under the name Ellis Bell, bound in the same three-volume set as her sister Anne's Agnes Grey. A year later Emily was dead of tuberculosis at thirty. It is her only novel.

Its place in literary history is peculiar: it was born in the Victorian era but it is not a Victorian novel. Novels of that moment were expected to hold court — the good rewarded, the wicked judged, a narrator stepping forward at the crucial moment to tell you what to think. This book does none of it. It hands the cruellest stretch of human behaviour to a talkative housekeeper and stands well back. Contemporary reviewers were near-unanimous that it was coarse and disagreeable. It took more than half a century — until readers had got used to the idea that a novel need not arbitrate — for it to stop being a curiosity and become a summit.

The story

Act one · 1801: a tenant catches a hand at the window

Remember two houses; that is the whole physical world of the book. Wuthering Heights stands high up — hard wind, thick stone, small windows, "Hareton Earnshaw 1500" cut over the door — and belongs to the Earnshaws. Thrushcross Grange lies four miles below in the valley, with crimson carpets, and belongs to the Lintons. The entire novel is the story of how a man with no surname comes to own both, and why he no longer wants them once he does.

In 1801 Lockwood, a gentleman from London, takes the Grange to nurse his fashionable misanthropy. He calls on his landlord up the hill and walks into a household whose relations nobody will explain: the glowering Heathcliff; a handsome, savagely rude young widow; a young man dressed like a labourer who nonetheless answers to the name Earnshaw. Snow closes the road, and he is put up for the night in a disused chamber, in a bed panelled with oak.

The ledge beside the pillow is scratched over with writing — the same name again and again, only the surname changing: Catherine Earnshaw, Catherine Heathcliff, Catherine Linton.Seed ① There are old books too, their margins crowded with a girl's diary complaining about being made to sit through sermons. He falls asleep on all this and dreams twice; in the second dream a branch taps the window, he reaches to break it off, and his fingers close on ice. A child outside, calling herself Catherine Linton, is begging to be let in — she has been a waif on the moor for twenty years. In pure terror, Lockwood saws her wrist across the broken glass.Seed ②

His screaming brings Heathcliff. The man who was iron all day listens to the dream, drives his tenant out of the room, then wrenches the casement open and sobs into the blizzard, begging her to come back just once. The book shows you its protagonist's hand on the first night: he is not afraid of the ghost; he is pleading with it. Back at the Grange, Lockwood falls ill and badgers the housekeeper, Nelly Dean, into telling him about these people. Three-quarters of the novel is her spoken account; Lockwood is only the man writing it down.

Act two · the child brought back from Liverpool

Thirty years earlier, old Mr Earnshaw walked sixty miles to Liverpool and back and came home with a dark, filthy boy inside his greatcoat who spoke gibberish nobody could follow: he had been starving in the street, and the old man could not leave him. The two children of the house — Hindley and Catherine — want nothing to do with him. The boy is christened Heathcliff, the name of a son of the family who died in infancy: even his name is second-hand, and it comes without a surname. In a world where ownership travels by surname, that detail will eventually kill several people.

Mr Earnshaw comes to prefer him to his own son, which fixes Hindley's hatred in place for good. Catherine goes the other way and is soon fused to him: two wild children skipping prayers and lessons, out on the moor all day, unimproved by beatings — not like siblings, not like lovers, more like two people sharing one set of bones. When the old man dies, Hindley comes home from school with a new wife and rules on Heathcliff's status within the day: no longer a foster son, a farmhand. Lessons stopped, sent to the fields, put to eat and sleep with the servants, and the curate need not trouble to teach him his letters.Seed ③ This is the crucial injury of the novel, and it does not involve hitting anyone: it cancels a person's future. Literacy in that time and place decided whether you would spend your life as a servant. How he keeps this account is something we only see in act five.

Act three · five weeks at the Grange, and half a sentence

One evening the two children run down the hill and press their faces to the windows of Thrushcross Grange. Inside is another world — carpet, silver, two immaculately dressed children, Edgar and Isabella Linton, shrieking and tearing at each other over a small dog they are nearly pulling in half.Seed ④ Heathcliff reports the scene afterwards in a tone of contempt: so this is what breeding looks like.

They are spotted, and the Lintons' guard dog seizes Catherine by the ankle. The family recognises her as Miss Earnshaw, carries her indoors and keeps her five weeks to mend; the dark boy who came with her is called a gypsy and turned out. Those five weeks are where the book forks. Catherine comes back in a riding habit and feathers, afraid to fling her arms round him in case she dirties her clothes, and laughs at how filthy he is. One dog bite delivers a child of the moor into a social class.

Three years on, Edgar proposes and she accepts, then explains herself to Nelly in the kitchen: she is marrying Edgar because he is handsome, rich and respectable, and because it would degrade her to marry Heathcliff; she also means to use Edgar's money to lift Heathcliff out of her brother's power.Seed ⑤ The trouble is that Heathcliff is sitting behind the settle. He hears the word "degrade", gets up and goes — and so misses what follows: that her love for Edgar is like foliage, which the seasons will change, and her love for him is like the rock underneath. He misses this:

"Nelly, I am Heathcliff."— Catherine to Nelly Dean (original text, vol. I, ch. 9)

Within her own logic there is no contradiction: if he is her, then whoever she marries she has not left him. But the one person who needed to hear the accounting had already walked out into the rain. Six hundred pages of tragedy rest on a sentence that was heard halfway through.

Act four · the gentleman who comes back three years later

Catherine marries into the Grange and has some months that cannot be called unhappy. Then Heathcliff returns: upright, almost military in bearing, well dressed, and carrying money nobody can explain. Where he went and how he got it, Emily Brontë does not write a single word about — he disappeared a farmhand with no future and returns a gentleman with capital, and that gap is the one miracle the book allows itself.

He has not come back to steal a wife; he has come back to collect, and every instrument he uses is legal. Step one: move into Wuthering Heights. Hindley has collapsed since his wife died in childbirth, drinking and gambling day and night, with a small neglected son, Hareton, in the house. Heathcliff sits down to play with him and takes the land in mortgage, parcel by parcel. Hindley is soon dead and deep in debt, and the last male Earnshaw is a dependent under another man's roof.

Step two: marry Edgar's sister. Isabella believes she has fallen in love with the brooding aristocrat of her novels. Heathcliff tells Nelly without a flicker of embarrassment that he feels nothing whatever for the girl and is marrying her because she stands second in line to the Linton property. On the night of the elopement Nelly finds Isabella's little dog strung up on a hook behind the door by a handkerchief, still just alive.Payoff ④ ↑ Years before, he had laughed at two Linton children nearly tearing a dog apart; now he hangs the Linton dog himself, in the same gesture. The beauty of it is that nothing is explained: the novel's verdict on this marriage hangs on that hook. Isabella eventually escapes, bears a son, Linton Heathcliff, near London, and dies a dozen years later.

Step three he never has to take; Catherine does it herself. With Heathcliff constantly at the Grange, Edgar finally forces her to choose between them. Her answer is to lock herself in and starve herself into delirium — she never wanted to choose, she wanted to keep both.

They meet for the last time in the Grange parlour. She is wasted; he holds her, and they accuse each other — she says he has killed her, and he refuses to give an inch: what right had you to leave me? I have not broken your heart — you have broken it, and in breaking it you have broken mine.Payoff ⑤ ↑ Only here does the weight of that half-heard sentence come clear: he will die not knowing she also said "I am Heathcliff" that day. The great information gap in this novel is not between reader and character, but between two people who believe they are one person.

That night she gives birth to a seven-months daughter — young Cathy — and dies two hours later. Heathcliff, waiting in the garden, takes the news and beats his head against a tree until the blood runs down his scalp. He does not pray for her to rest; he begs her not to:

"I cannot live without my life! I cannot live without my soul!"— Heathcliff, after Catherine's death (original text, vol. II)

He asks her to come back in any shape she likes, to drive him mad if she must, so long as she does not leave him alone where he cannot find her. This is not a eulogy, it is a commission: please haunt me. The night of the burial, he prises the coffin lid open once.

Act five · raising the hatred to the second generation

Many readers assume the book can be closed here, and this is exactly its watershed: the heroine is dead and half the pages remain. What Emily Brontë came to write starts now.

What Heathcliff does to Hindley's son Hareton is a precision instrument. He does not abuse him; on the contrary, the boy grows up regarding him as his one protector. He simply does not let him learn to read — the lawful heir of the Earnshaws grows into a broad-spoken, illiterate farmhand with calloused hands inside his own ancestral house.Payoff ③ ↑ This is the coldest symmetry in the book: what Hindley did to the boy brought in off the street, he does to Hindley's boy, step for step, with nothing added. He admits to Nelly that the material in Hareton is better than anything in the Linton line, and that this is precisely why he wants to watch it rot — gold put to the use of paving-stones is the pleasure of the thing.

The rest is property. When Isabella dies, her sickly, peevish, permanently breathless son Linton is fetched back to England, and Heathcliff claims him the same day — not as a son but as a component of inheritance: if Edgar leaves no male heir, the Grange descends through Isabella's line to Linton. So he lets young Cathy correspond and visit with this "cousin" until the sheltered girl feels sorry for him; then, with Edgar dying, he lures her to the Heights and locks her up until the wedding is performed. She gets home as her father breathes his last. Edgar had meant to alter his will and put the property in trustees' hands for his daughter, out of a husband's reach — but the attorney had been bought, and arrived only after the old man was dead.

Months later Linton dies too, having willed everything he owned to his father as instructed. Under the law of the day a wife's property passed to her husband, and the husband's to his father — two houses, two families and three generations of land went, without a single item lost, into the name of the man who had once not even had a surname. The odd household Lockwood blundered into on his first day finally makes sense: the glowering landlord, the young widow kept there to work, the farmhand called Earnshaw.

Act six · he suddenly stops wanting to win

Having won, Heathcliff begins to malfunction.

The trouble is the two young people. Cathy starts out sharp-tongued, mocking Hareton for stumbling over his own name; Hareton, humiliated, throws his secretly-acquired books into the fire. The turn is very small: one afternoon she pushes a book across to him and starts teaching him to read. The most devastating reversal in the novel is a girl teaching a farmhand his letters in a kitchen. Heathcliff spent twenty years holding a man down in illiteracy; dismantling the device takes a few pages, because it was never anything but a gate somebody had closed.

Worse are their faces. Hareton has the Earnshaw eyes, and so does Cathy. Heathcliff tells Nelly that he is now surrounded wherever he goes — the flagstones, the clouds, the trees, the air at night, every face by day, the whole world turned into an open memorandum reminding him that she existed and does not now. He says he has raised the spade and finds he has no strength to bring it down. He holds the fate of both families in his hands and has abruptly lost all appetite for destroying them: the more completely the revenge is executed, the more plainly it proves that nothing has been bought back.

Earlier he had done one more thing: on the day Edgar was buried, he paid the sexton to remove one side panel of Catherine's coffin, with instructions that the same be done to his own so that the two would lie open to each other and the dust mingle. He will not entrust even the afterlife to God; he will cut the hole in the boards himself.

For his last four days he barely eats, walks the moor at night, and comes back with shining eyes like a man keeping an appointment. On a wet night he dies in that oak bed with the window wide open, rain on his face and throat, the swinging casement having grazed the hand he left lying on the sill.Payoff ② ↑ A year and a bit earlier, in the snow, Lockwood lay in that same bed at that same window and sawed a wrist that was begging to be let in across the broken glass. The opening dream was not atmosphere; it was the ending played early: the first scene and the last are one window and one hand cut on the glass, except that this time the window stands open. Nelly cannot close his eyes, and what is on his face looks less like release than triumph. He is buried beside Catherine.

Cathy and Hareton are to marry on New Year's Day and move down to the Grange, leaving the stone house on the hill to old Joseph. And the path her surname takes across a lifetime is precisely the three names on that window ledge, run backwards — born Catherine Linton, married once into Catherine Heathcliff, and by marrying Hareton she becomes Catherine Earnshaw.Payoff ① ↑ Her mother's three names were a road downhill; the daughter walks it back up. The scratches looked like a child's idle graffiti; they were the table of contents.

Lockwood passes the churchyard one last time and sees three headstones side by side: Catherine, Edgar, Heathcliff, with heath and harebells growing over them. He stands a while under that benign sky and wonders how anyone could imagine unquiet slumbers for sleepers in so quiet an earth. — And a few pages before he says it, a shepherd boy has just told him in tears that Heathcliff and a woman are walking out there on the hill.

Six acts gathered onto one fuse: this is not a story about love, it is a story about what happens when a child is carried into a house where only surnames count. He has no surname, so the day the old man dies he is nothing; being nothing, he can have his education cancelled by Hindley; having no education, marrying him means marrying downward; she marries upward, so he leaves, gets rich, comes back, and takes the surnames and the land away piece by piece — and having taken them discovers they were never what he wanted. Every marriage, will and gambling debt that follows is one man using the world's own rules to punish the world, which does not notice.

Three slow-motion scenes

1 · The window and the wrist: handing the horror to a civilised man

The convention of a ghost story is that the ghost wants out and the living shut the door. This scene inverts it — the ghost is outside begging to come in, and the man inside puts her wrist to the glass. One gesture overturns the moral coordinates of the whole novel: there are no innocent bystanders here, and the most civilised person present is the one who draws blood.

Technically, giving this to Lockwood is very shrewd. He is a townsman convinced he understands people and incapable of reading a room; with him as first narrator, everything the reader receives at first hand arrives with a layer of misreading on it. Better still is the timing — at this point we do not know who Catherine is, only that a child has been out in the snow for twenty years. The suspense is not "who is she" but "why will nobody let her in", and the answer arrives six hundred pages later: the only man who wants to open the window is downstairs, crying.

2 · The kitchen sentence: the most famous declaration in English fiction, spoken by a woman announcing another marriage

"I am Heathcliff" has kept its value for a hundred and seventy-odd years because of where it sits: it is not a love speech, it is part of an explanation of why she is marrying someone else. Move it — put it in a carriage during an elopement — and it is merely romantic. Placed here, it is simultaneously the highest form of love and the most complete act of possession. She is saying "you are me", and the other face of "you are me" is "you need not have a life of your own".

Then the staging. Three people are present: Catherine speaking, Nelly listening, Heathcliff behind the settle. The author does not write him listening — she lets Nelly realise only after he has gone, so the reader learns a beat late. That single beat is the engine of the book: we know he did not hear it all, and nobody inside the novel ever does. From here on, every time they wound each other the reader flinches — if he had stayed ten seconds longer. And those ten seconds are handled without a drop of sentiment: no narrator regretting anything, only a clap of thunder, a chimney split at the corner of the house, and a man who is no longer there. She never grieves on the reader's behalf, which is why she is harder than everyone who has imitated her since.

3 · The coffin: the subject is not love, it is refusing to be separated

Heathcliff tells Nelly calmly how he lifted the lid, how he squared the sexton, how the two coffins are to be opened to each other after his death. The horror is not in the content but in the register: it is delivered in the voice of household business — a few pounds, a spade, which panel.

Here you can see the narrative machine working. Nelly is decent, superstitious, faintly superior, and she keeps breaking in to say that he is hardly a human creature. An extreme passion is being carried in the vocabulary of a perfectly ordinary person, and the effect is not to shrink it but to magnify it — like measuring a flood with a kitchen jug; you judge the water by how much her hand shakes.

The scene also settles the most misread question about the book: what these two want was never "a life together". They are almost never shown behaving like lovers — no endearments, no plans, no imagined future. What they want is not to be separated, including by death, and for that a coffin panel can come out. Selling this as the great love story turns a nearly monstrous demand for sameness into romance.

The device: a novel that refuses to arbitrate

Two layers of hearsay: who is looking for you

The book is a nest of boxes. Lockwood writes down what Nelly says; Nelly relays what she was told (Isabella's letter, the servant Zillah's account, Heathcliff's own confessions), so several key scenes reach us third or fourth hand — nothing in this novel is said by "the author".

The consequence is decisive. A contemporary novelist would step out to reassure the reader when the villain prospers; Emily Brontë does not step out, and does not even supply a reliable relay. Nelly is not neutral: she suppresses letters, downplays illnesses, and reliably decides not to tell the master just yet. Since the mid-twentieth century a whole strand of criticism has argued that hers is the hand that pushes events past saving. It need not be the only reading, but the fact that it holds up at all shows how modern the construction is.

Symmetry: an argument made out of structure

Set the generations side by side and almost everything happens twice, the second time as a mirror of the first: a child carried into Wuthering Heights (Heathcliff, then Cathy); an heir deprived of education (Heathcliff, then Hareton); a marriage across the two houses (Catherine marrying down the hill, Cathy marrying up it); a woman locked in at the Heights. Even the names are symmetrical: the mother's three surnames run downhill, and the daughter runs them backwards.

This is not showmanship, it is the argument: harm travels in a shape — you will do to the next person the procedure that was done to you, in order, with nothing omitted. What breaks it is not the completion of revenge but somebody doing one link differently: the afternoon Cathy pushes the book across.

The moor: the third protagonist

The two houses stand for two orders — roughness above, cultivation below — while the moor is the only ground that belongs to nobody: no walls, no deeds, no surnames. All of Catherine and Heathcliff's happiness happens there, and their tragedy is that no adult can live on a moor; adulthood means entering one house or the other and accepting its rules. At the end the young people move down the hill. That is a compromise, not a victory.

What it makes you think about

Question 1
Catherine's calculation — "I marry another man so that I can help him" — is that betrayal, or the only leverage she could get?

Take her side at its strongest. In late-eighteenth-century rural Yorkshire, a woman without property of her own controls exactly one thing: whom she marries. Heathcliff at that moment has no name, no money, no schooling, and is pinned in a labourer's place by her brother. Marry him and they sink together; she cannot save him and has thrown away the only social leverage she possesses. Her plan has a certain engineering elegance: convert a respectable marriage into resources, and use the resources to buy him out of Hindley's power. And she says it to Nelly beforehand — it is a plan, not a retrospective excuse. Everyone today who says "I'll do a few years at the big firm and then do the thing I actually want" is keeping the same ledger.

Now the other side, stated harder than her critics usually state it. The fatal omission in her plan is that she never asks him. She designs a life for him, including his accepting money from her husband, without a word of consultation — because in her logic he is not someone you consult, he is part of her. "I am Heathcliff" sounds like love at maximum concentration, and it abolishes the other person's separate existence: nobody consults their own hand. More practically, what she actually wants is both — Edgar and Heathcliff at once; the immediate cause of her collapse is her husband demanding she pick one. Her tragedy is not being forced to choose. It is refusing to admit that choices cost.

Brought home: nearly everyone has run this accounting — take A for security while telling yourself the real you is safely stored at B. What is cruel about the book is that it never tells you the sum is wrong. It simply demonstrates: the part of your life you thought you had left in storage is alive, and it walks off while you are not looking.

Question 2
Victim turned perpetrator: how large a moral discount does a systematically ruined childhood buy?

The case for Heathcliff is harder than most people assume. First, he was selected for it: a boy of seven or eight with no language, no kin and no surname, taken in by a family and then demoted to livestock by that same family, having done nothing wrong in between. Second, withholding education is a precise form of violence — it leaves no mark and caps a life permanently, and the man who turns the key gets to say the boy was always coarse. Third, and hardest to answer: his revenge is almost entirely lawful. He kills nobody and burns nothing; he uses gambling debts, marriage settlements, wills and entail — instruments that society invented and had already used on him. He simply learned the rules and then played them better than their owners. A system that locks a man out is poorly placed to complain that he finally learned its language.

Now the prosecution at its strongest. Look at whom he selects. Hindley was the real enemy, and Hindley drank and gambled himself to death with almost no help. The people he works on for twenty years are Isabella (a girl whose head was turned by novels), Hareton (his enemy's child), Linton (his own son, used as a legal component until it kills him) and Cathy (the daughter of the woman he loved). Three of those four are children. What he does to Hareton replicates, step by step, the procedure performed on him — which means he knows exactly how much it hurts, and does it anyway. "He was treated like this himself" explains him; it does not excuse him. Knowing is precisely what makes him responsible for what he is destroying.

The same dilemma turns up today wearing different clothes: when is "he was a victim too" an act of understanding, and when is it a permit? Explaining why something happened and conceding that it was acceptable are two different streets. This book is uncomfortable because it fills in both sides completely: you cannot only pity him and you cannot only condemn him, and it declines to assign you the proportions.

Question 3
Is the ending a reconciliation, or a surrender?

The case for reconciliation is strong. Hareton learns to read, Cathy learns not to be cruel, and two young people whose childhoods were wrecked by the same man dismantle in a few months a machine that took twenty years to build. They move down the hill and leave the stone house to the past. This is the one place in the novel where the cycle is shown to be breakable, and the means are reassuringly plain: a book, some patience, one person willing to teach another. If that is not hope, there is no such thing in fiction.

Now the opposing case, at full strength. The ending can also be read as a pacification. Everything that gives this book its charge — the refusal to be absorbed by family, marriage and good manners — is precisely what the last chapters undo by delivering the survivors into Thrushcross Grange, the house of carpets and rules. Hareton gains literacy; the price is that the illiterate free creature on the moor is gone. And note the author's own arrangement: she gives the closing line about quiet slumbers to Lockwood — a man the novel has been proving obtuse since chapter three — one page after a shepherd boy reports, in tears, two figures walking the hill. An author who genuinely wanted you to believe in the reconciliation would not assign the announcement of it to her densest character.

Where does Emily Brontë stand? The evidence is in the structure rather than in any statement: she gives the rational, worldly, surviving party the houses and the marriage, and gives the other party the ghost story and the moor. She appears to think both are true — life continues, and some things are not tamed. Turn it on yourself: do we like reconciliation endings because they are more accurate, or because without one the book will not close?

Question 4
How much damage can a conscientious bystander do?

Nelly Dean kills no one and plots nothing. Every single thing she does sounds like common sense: better not show the master this letter, it will only upset him; the young lady is in a temper, no need to trouble the doctor; the trip to the Heights cannot be avoided, so I shall go along. Her motive is keeping the peace, and her position requires loyalty, decorum and minding her own business.

But line up every occasion on which she decided not to say something and you have a complete causal chain. She is not the villain; she simply never believes she is authorised to make a judgement. And the novel demonstrates what follows: in a situation where everyone else has lost control, if the one clear-headed person defines herself as the one who merely relays, her neutrality becomes the fuel.

This character sits closer to us than Heathcliff does. Most people do not spend their lives playing the avenger; they play Nelly — present, informed, tactful, hands in the apron.

The obsession: what Emily Brontë kept writing

She left one novel and more than two hundred poems, and the preoccupations line up exactly with the book.

The first is confinement and escape. As children she and Anne invented an imaginary kingdom called Gondal and wrote a great deal of verse set there, full of dungeons, exile, and people looking out of windows. Every significant turn in Wuthering Heights happens on a threshold: a ghost shut out at a window, Catherine starving herself behind a locked door, Cathy held at the Heights, and a casement still standing open after a death. Her lifelong subject is inside and outside, and who gets to draw the line.

The second is death as a technical problem in reunion rather than an ending. Her two best-known poems are "Remembrance", spoken to a lover fifteen years in the grave, and "No Coward Soul Is Mine", which is close to a declaration of faith with the church left out. The man taking the side panel off a coffin comes from the same mind: what such people want is not heaven, it is not being parted — and if religion cannot guarantee that, they will see to it themselves.

It is worth measuring the distance between sisters. Charlotte's Jane Eyre is about a woman being accepted by the world without surrendering herself; it makes its peace with respectable order in the end. Emily makes no peace. Two sisters raised under one roof: one wrote how to come home, the other wrote why some people never can.

Criticism and controversy

  • The contemporary reception was ugly. Reviewers in 1847 generally found the book coarse, disagreeable and close to brutal; even those who conceded its power did so with distaste. This is not merely the usual story of an age failing to recognise a masterpiece — the novel really did violate the basic expectation that fiction should sort good from evil and distribute rewards accordingly.
  • Charlotte's editing and preface. After Emily's death, Charlotte reissued the book in 1850 with a biographical notice and a preface, and regularised old Joseph's dialect spellings and the punctuation. Her preface both defends her sister and concedes that she is not sure whether creating a figure like Heathcliff was advisable. Two consequences: modern editions do not all follow the same copy-text, and the enduring image of Emily as a solitary genius of the moors was largely shaped by that essay.
  • "The greatest love story" is a successful misreading. The book contains sustained domestic violence: Heathcliff imprisons and beats Isabella, strikes young Cathy, and uses his own son as an instrument until it kills him. A great many screen adaptations stop at Catherine's death and cut the second generation entirely — which removes exactly the half the author came to write, and repackages a book about the transmission of harm as a pair of doomed lovers. Loving this novel is one thing; treating it as a model for a relationship is another.
  • Heathcliff's origin and race. In the text he is picked up off the streets of Liverpool — then Britain's largest slave-trading port — his complexion is remarked on repeatedly, and characters reach for words like "gypsy" and "Lascar". His ancestry is never settled, and that is part of the design: how much of the hostility directed at this man comes from where he is from, and how much from what he does? Recent decades of criticism have pushed this to the front; Andrea Arnold's 2011 film, which cast a Black actor as Heathcliff, follows directly from that reading.
  • The old structural complaint: the second generation is weaker. Many readers — and nearly every adapter — feel the voltage drops after Catherine dies, with Linton in particular a trial to read. The defence is that the flatness is the point: the back half of a revenge is not supposed to be thrilling, because it is the invoice rather than the climax.

Ten sentences to take away

1. Not a love story: a set of working instructions for how harm is handed on intact to the next generation.

2. The fuse is a child with no surname carried into a house where surnames are everything; everything after that is interest on the debt.

3. Six hundred pages of catastrophe rest on a sentence heard halfway — he left before "I am Heathcliff".

4. His cruellest revenge is not a blow but withheld literacy: cancelling a future is more thorough than inflicting pain.

5. The whole revenge is legal, conducted with the society's own instruments — debts, marriage settlements, wills, entail.

6. Dismantling that machine takes one afternoon: a girl pushes a book across and teaches a farmhand his own name.

7. Catherine and Heathcliff never once plan a life together; what they demand is not to be separated, death included.

8. The gentleman who saws a ghost's wrist on broken glass in chapter three is the same man who declares at the end that they sleep quietly.

9. Nelly Dean resembles us far more than Heathcliff does: present, informed, reasonable, and doing nothing.

10. What stays with you is not the revenge but the window left open after a death, and the child outside who waited twenty years to be let in.
